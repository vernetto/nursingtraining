# Part V: Recognizing the Sick Patient

## Chapter 37. The First Minute: ABCDE Assessment and Immediate Life Threats

### Learning objectives

After studying this chapter you should be able to:

1. Recognise within seconds whether a patient is critically ill, using the general impression and the "can you talk to me?" test.
2. Perform a structured ABCDE assessment, treating life-threatening problems as they are found, and re-assessing after every intervention.
3. Identify the signs of airway obstruction, respiratory failure, shock, impaired consciousness and hypoglycaemia, and give the first-line treatment for each.
4. Recognise the commonest immediately reversible causes of deterioration: tension pneumothorax, anaphylaxis, severe asthma, haemorrhage, opioid overdose, hypoglycaemia, tamponade, and arrhythmia.
5. Recognise cardiac arrest, start basic life support, and use the 4 Hs and 4 Ts to look for reversible causes.
6. Use early warning scores (NEWS2), call for help early, and communicate with SBAR.
7. Adapt the approach for children, pregnant women, trauma and older adults.

---

### 37.1 The principle: treat as you assess

Most deaths in hospital that are potentially preventable follow a period of **unrecognised or poorly managed physiological deterioration.** Patients rarely die suddenly without warning; changes in respiratory rate, heart rate, blood pressure, consciousness and urine output precede cardiac arrest by hours (Chapter 44).

The ABCDE approach provides:

- a **common language** and structure for any clinician, in any setting (ward, clinic, street, emergency department);
- a **priority order** that follows the speed with which each failure kills (airway obstruction kills in minutes; hypoxia in minutes; shock in minutes to hours; hypoglycaemia and raised ICP in minutes to hours);
- a rule that **life-threatening problems are treated as they are found,** before moving on to the next step ("treat as you go"), not after a complete assessment.

> **A — Airway · B — Breathing · C — Circulation · D — Disability · E — Exposure**

And a rule that governs everything: **reassess after every intervention, and go back to A if the patient deteriorates.**

---

### 37.2 The first ten seconds

Before any equipment:

1. **Scene safety and self-protection** (gloves, eye protection, infection precautions; hazards, violence).
2. **General impression:** Does this patient look well, ill, or about to die? Colour, posture, sweating, effort, position (tripod), alertness.
3. **Introduce yourself and ask:** *"Hello, I'm Dr … Can you tell me your name and what's wrong?"*

   A **normal, full-sentence voice** tells you three things at once: the **airway** is patent, there is enough **breathing** to speak, and the **brain** is adequately perfused (the patient is conscious and coherent). A one-word or whispered answer indicates respiratory distress; **no answer** is an emergency.

4. **Call for help early** if the patient looks critically ill: ask a colleague to summon senior or rapid-response help (resuscitation team), bring the **emergency trolley and defibrillator,** while you continue. Calling for help is a mark of good practice, not weakness.
5. **Attach monitoring:** SpO₂, ECG, non-invasive BP, respiratory rate, temperature, glucose.

### 37.3 A: Airway

#### Look, listen, feel (10 seconds)

- **Look** for: chest and abdominal movement, **see-saw (paradoxical) breathing** (the chest sinks while the abdomen rises: complete obstruction), use of accessory muscles, central cyanosis, foreign material in the mouth, facial or neck swelling, burns, blood, vomit, neck injury.
- **Listen** for: **stridor** (upper airway narrowing: inspiratory = extrathoracic; biphasic = glottic/subglottic), **snoring** (tongue or soft tissues), **gurgling** (fluid), **hoarseness,** wheeze. **Silence with effortful breathing is ominous:** complete obstruction or exhaustion.
- **Feel** for air movement at the mouth and nose.

#### Causes of airway obstruction

| Cause | Examples |
|---|---|
| **Reduced consciousness** (loss of pharyngeal tone) | Coma, intoxication, seizures, head injury, stroke |
| **Foreign body** | Choking on food, toys |
| **Swelling** | Anaphylaxis, angioedema (ACE inhibitor), epiglottitis, retropharyngeal abscess, Ludwig angina, burns and inhalation injury, trauma, haematoma after thyroid surgery, croup, tumour |
| **Fluids** | Vomit, blood, secretions |
| **Laryngospasm, bronchospasm** | Aspiration, anaesthesia |

#### Management of airway problems (stepwise)

1. **Open the airway:** **head tilt–chin lift** (jaw thrust if cervical spine injury is suspected, with in-line stabilisation).
2. **Remove obstructions:** inspect the mouth; **suction** fluids; remove visible foreign bodies (do not blind-sweep).
3. **Basic adjuncts:** **oropharyngeal (Guedel) airway** (unconscious patient with no gag reflex), **nasopharyngeal airway** (conscious or semi-conscious patients; avoid in suspected base-of-skull fracture or coagulopathy).
4. **Give oxygen** (high-flow, 15 L/min via reservoir mask) to all critically ill patients, and **titrate** afterwards (target SpO₂ 94–98%; **88–92% in patients at risk of hypercapnic respiratory failure,** e.g. COPD, obesity hypoventilation, neuromuscular disease), once the patient is stabilised.
5. **Choking (foreign body):**
   - **Mild obstruction (effective cough):** encourage coughing.
   - **Severe obstruction (ineffective cough, unable to speak, conscious):** **5 back blows** between the shoulder blades, then **5 abdominal thrusts** (Heimlich; for adults and children >1 year), alternating. Infants (<1 year): 5 back blows (head-down) and 5 chest thrusts (no abdominal thrusts). If the patient becomes **unresponsive,** start CPR.
6. **Advanced airway** (anaesthetist or experienced clinician): supraglottic airway device, **tracheal intubation** (with rapid-sequence induction when indicated), and **front-of-neck access** (cricothyroidotomy) in a **"can't intubate, can't oxygenate"** situation.
7. **Anaphylaxis or angioedema with airway involvement:** **adrenaline IM** (0.5 mg of 1:1,000 in adults; 0.3–0.5 mL; repeat every 5 minutes) (Chapter 31), call anaesthesia early; expect a difficult airway.
8. **Epiglottitis and similar:** keep the patient calm, avoid examining the throat or lying flat, urgent senior anaesthetic/ENT involvement.
9. **Trauma:** apply **manual in-line stabilisation** of the cervical spine and maintain it until cleared.

**Re-check the airway every time the patient's condition changes.**

### 37.4 B: Breathing

#### Assess

- **Respiratory rate** (count for a full 60 seconds if time allows: normal adult 12–20/min; **>25 or <8 is an alarm**).
- **Work of breathing:** accessory muscles, recession (intercostal, subcostal), nasal flaring, tracheal tug, position, ability to speak in sentences, exhaustion.
- **SpO₂** on air or oxygen (note: oximetry does not reflect ventilation: hypercapnia is not detected; it is unreliable in shock, CO poisoning, nail varnish, motion).
- **Chest examination:** symmetrical expansion, **tracheal position,** percussion (hyper-resonance, dullness), auscultation (reduced or absent breath sounds, wheeze, crackles, bronchial breathing), surgical emphysema, flail segments, open wounds.
- **Cyanosis, JVP, neck veins.**
- **Arterial blood gas (ABG)** or venous gas: PaO₂, PaCO₂, pH, bicarbonate, lactate.
- **Chest X-ray** (portable) after initial stabilisation (do not delay treatment of tension pneumothorax).

#### Life-threatening breathing problems and immediate treatment

| Problem | Features | Immediate action |
|---|---|---|
| **Tension pneumothorax** | Severe respiratory distress, **hypotension, tachycardia,** raised JVP, tracheal deviation (late), unilateral hyper-resonance and absent breath sounds, trauma or ventilation; **clinical diagnosis** | **Immediate needle decompression** (or finger thoracostomy) followed by chest drain; do not wait for X-ray |
| **Open pneumothorax ("sucking chest wound")** | Chest wound with air movement | Occlusive dressing taped on three sides; chest drain |
| **Massive haemothorax** | Shock, dull percussion, absent breath sounds | Large-bore IV access, blood, chest drain, surgical review |
| **Flail chest** | Paradoxical movement, pain, hypoxia | Oxygen, analgesia (regional), ventilatory support |
| **Severe or life-threatening asthma** | Cannot speak, **silent chest, cyanosis, exhaustion,** bradycardia, confusion, PEF <33% | High-flow oxygen, **nebulised salbutamol and ipratropium** (continuous), **IV hydrocortisone or oral prednisolone,** IV magnesium sulphate, senior/ICU involvement, consider IV salbutamol or aminophylline, intubation as last resort |
| **Acute pulmonary oedema** | Orthopnoea, crackles, frothy sputum, hypertension | Sit up, oxygen/CPAP, IV diuretic, IV nitrate, treat cause (Chapter 23) |
| **COPD exacerbation with hypercapnia** | Drowsiness, flapping tremor, acidosis | **Controlled oxygen (88–92%),** nebulised bronchodilators, steroids, antibiotics, **non-invasive ventilation (NIV)** |
| **Pulmonary embolism (massive)** | Sudden dyspnoea, shock, raised JVP | Oxygen, anticoagulation, thrombolysis or embolectomy (Chapter 45) |
| **Pneumonia with respiratory failure, ARDS** | Fever, hypoxia, infiltrates | Oxygen, antibiotics, CPAP/HFNO, ICU |
| **Anaphylaxis** | Wheeze, stridor, urticaria, hypotension | IM adrenaline (below) |
| **Opioid or sedative overdose** | Slow, shallow breathing, pinpoint pupils | Bag-valve-mask ventilation, **naloxone** |
| **Respiratory muscle failure** (neuromuscular disease) | Shallow breathing, paradoxical movement, weak cough | NIV or intubation, measure vital capacity |

#### Oxygen delivery and ventilatory support

- **Reservoir (non-rebreather) mask at 10–15 L/min** for critical illness; **titrate to SpO₂ once stable** (excess oxygen may be harmful in many conditions, including stroke, MI without hypoxia and post-cardiac arrest).
- **High-flow nasal oxygen (HFNO), CPAP, NIV** for respiratory failure (CPAP in pulmonary oedema; NIV in COPD and neuromuscular failure).
- **Bag-valve-mask ventilation** (two-person technique preferred) for inadequate breathing.
- **Intubation and mechanical ventilation** for failure of oxygenation or ventilation, airway protection (GCS ≤8), or anticipated deterioration.

### 37.5 C: Circulation

#### Assess

- **Heart rate and rhythm,** **blood pressure** (and **pulse pressure**), **capillary refill time** (central: sternum or forehead; peripheral: fingertip; >2 seconds is prolonged), **skin colour, temperature, mottling,** pulse volume and character, **JVP,** heart sounds, signs of heart failure.
- **Urine output** (<0.5 mL/kg/hour suggests inadequate renal perfusion; catheterise to measure in critical illness).
- **Look for sources of fluid or blood loss:** external bleeding, GI bleeding, abdominal distension, pelvic or long-bone fractures, retroperitoneal bleeding, third-space losses (pancreatitis, bowel obstruction), vomiting and diarrhoea, burns, fever, sepsis, polyuria (DKA).
- **12-lead ECG,** blood tests (FBC, U&E, glucose, **lactate,** gas, troponin, clotting, **group and cross-match,** cultures, toxicology as indicated).
- **Shock index** (heart rate ÷ systolic BP): >0.9–1.0 suggests significant shock.
- **Bedside ultrasound** (focused cardiac and IVC, FAST scan in trauma, lung ultrasound) if available (Chapter 67).

#### Shock: the clinical syndrome of inadequate tissue perfusion

(See Chapter 38.) Look for **tachycardia, hypotension (late),** cool clammy peripheries (or warm in distributive shock), prolonged capillary refill, mottling, **confusion or drowsiness,** oliguria, tachypnoea, **raised lactate,** metabolic acidosis.

#### Immediate management

1. **Two large-bore IV cannulae (14–16 G)** (intraosseous access if IV access is impossible: a fast, reliable alternative in adults and children), take bloods.
2. **Control external haemorrhage:** **direct pressure,** pressure dressing, **tourniquet** for catastrophic limb bleeding, **pelvic binder** for suspected pelvic fracture; in trauma, **haemorrhage control precedes airway** (xABCDE).
3. **Fluids:** **250–500 mL bolus of warmed balanced crystalloid** (Hartmann's, Ringer's lactate, or normal saline) over 5–15 minutes in adults (20 mL/kg in children, 10 mL/kg in neonates or when cardiac function is poor), **reassess after each bolus** (HR, BP, capillary refill, JVP, lung sounds, urine output); **stop if signs of overload** (rising JVP, crackles, hypoxia). **Blood products early** in haemorrhage (balanced transfusion, major haemorrhage protocol) with tranexamic acid in trauma and obstetric haemorrhage; **avoid large crystalloid volumes in uncontrolled haemorrhage** (permissive hypotension, e.g. target systolic ~80–90 mmHg until bleeding is controlled; not in head injury).
4. **Treat the cause:** sepsis (antibiotics within one hour, source control), anaphylaxis (adrenaline), cardiogenic shock (inotropes, revascularisation, avoid large fluid loads), obstructive causes (needle decompression for tension pneumothorax, pericardiocentesis for tamponade, anticoagulation or thrombolysis for PE), adrenal crisis (hydrocortisone), arrhythmia.
5. **Vasopressors** (noradrenaline) if hypotension persists despite fluids (and early in sepsis), via central or peripheral line, in a monitored setting (Chapter 38).
6. **Arrhythmia management:** bradycardia with adverse features (shock, syncope, myocardial ischaemia, heart failure): **atropine 500 µg IV (repeat up to 3 mg),** then pacing or inotropes; tachycardia with adverse features: **synchronised DC cardioversion** (Chapter 28).
7. **Urinary catheter** and fluid balance chart; monitor lactate; transfer to higher-level care.

#### Anaphylaxis (a combined A, B, C emergency)

- **Features:** rapid onset (minutes) of skin/mucosal changes (urticaria, flushing, angioedema), **plus** respiratory compromise (stridor, wheeze, dyspnoea) **and/or** circulatory compromise (hypotension, collapse), **or** hypotension after a known trigger (drug, sting, food, latex).
- **Treatment (all in parallel):** **remove the trigger,** **adrenaline 0.5 mg (0.5 mL of 1:1,000) IM into the anterolateral thigh immediately, repeating every 5 minutes if there is no improvement** (children: 0.01 mg/kg, i.e. 0.15 mg <6 years, 0.3 mg 6–12 years, 0.5 mg >12 years per guideline); **lie the patient flat** with legs raised (sit up if breathless; left lateral if pregnant); **high-flow oxygen;** **IV fluid bolus;** **nebulised salbutamol/adrenaline** for bronchospasm or stridor; secondary measures (antihistamine, hydrocortisone) do not replace adrenaline; observe for at least 6–12 hours (biphasic reactions); **serum tryptase** (1–2 hours after onset); **adrenaline auto-injector prescription, allergy referral** before discharge.
- **Refractory anaphylaxis:** IV adrenaline infusion in a monitored setting by experienced staff.

### 37.6 D: Disability

#### Assess

- **Level of consciousness:** AVPU or **GCS** (Chapter 33). Pay attention to **change** from baseline.
- **Pupils:** size, equality, reactivity (pinpoint: opioids; unilateral dilated and fixed: herniation).
- **Blood glucose (immediately in any patient with altered consciousness, seizure, or neurological deficit).**
- **Limb movements and posture,** focal deficit (FAST: Face, Arm, Speech, Time), seizure activity, tone, neck stiffness.
- **Pain.**

#### Immediate management

| Problem | Action |
|---|---|
| **Hypoglycaemia (<4 mmol/L / 70 mg/dL, or symptomatic)** | IV glucose (e.g. 10% glucose 150–200 mL over 15 minutes, or 50 mL of 50% via a large vein; repeat), or IM glucagon 1 mg if no IV access; **thiamine** if alcohol misuse or malnutrition; follow with oral carbohydrate or infusion; identify cause |
| **Seizure** | Protect, oxygen, glucose; benzodiazepine if >5 minutes (Chapter 33) |
| **Opioid toxicity** | Naloxone titrated |
| **Suspected stroke** | Immediate stroke team, time of onset, CT; **do not give oral intake;** glucose (Chapter 41) |
| **Reduced consciousness (GCS ≤8)** | Airway protection; find cause (Chapter 33) |
| **Suspected raised ICP/herniation** | Head up 30°, osmotherapy, urgent neurosurgical review |
| **Meningitis suspected** | Immediate antibiotics (Chapter 41) |
| **Hyperglycaemia/DKA** | Fluids, insulin after potassium check (Chapter 43) |
| **Poisoning** | Specific antidotes, call poison centre |

### 37.7 E: Exposure

- **Undress the patient completely** (with dignity), and **inspect the whole body, front and back (log-roll in trauma, with spinal precautions):** wounds, bruising, rashes (petechiae: meningococcaemia; urticaria: anaphylaxis; erythema: cellulitis; livor), injection marks, surgical scars, burns, swellings, deformities, bleeding, medical devices (insulin pump, patches, catheters, drains, lines), **medical alert bracelet** or cards, medication lists, signs of abuse.
- **Measure temperature** (core): hypothermia (<35 °C) and hyperthermia (>38.3 °C) both may be life-threatening and call for treatment (warming blankets, warmed fluids; cooling).
- **Examine the abdomen, spine, perineum and limbs** (including peripheral pulses and compartments).
- **Prevent heat loss:** cover the patient again after examination, warm environment.
- Consider **digital rectal examination** (GI bleeding, spinal injury).
- **Check for pressure areas** and skin integrity in immobile patients.

### 37.8 After the primary survey: the next steps

Once life-threatening problems are addressed and the patient is stabilised or improving:

1. **Reassess ABCDE** (every 5–15 minutes in unstable patients).
2. **Get a focused history:** **AMPLE** (Allergies, Medications, Past medical history, Last meal, Events leading to the presentation), or **SAMPLE** (+ Signs and symptoms); collateral from relatives, ambulance crew, notes.
3. **Secondary survey:** a full head-to-toe examination looking for injuries and causes.
4. **Investigations:** bloods (FBC, U&E, glucose, LFTs, CRP, lactate, coagulation, group and save/cross-match, troponin, cultures, toxicology), ECG, chest X-ray, blood gas, urinalysis, imaging as indicated, bedside ultrasound.
5. **Definitive treatment** for the diagnosis; specific treatment protocols (sepsis bundle, ACS pathway, DKA, stroke, etc.).
6. **Monitoring:** continuous ECG, SpO₂, BP, urine output, repeated lactate, level of consciousness; consider arterial line.
7. **Escalate:** consult senior clinicians, the **ICU/outreach team,** specialists; plan transfer to the appropriate level of care; consider **treatment limitation and resuscitation status** (document ceilings of care, discuss with patient and family where appropriate).
8. **Communication:** use **SBAR** when calling for help:
   - **S:** "I'm calling about [name], bed [x], I'm worried because …"
   - **B:** "Admitted with …, treatments given …"
   - **A:** "NEWS2 is …; respiratory rate …; BP …; I think the problem is …"
   - **R:** "I need you to come and review now; I have given … and would like … "
9. **Family communication** and documentation (times, observations, interventions, responses, communications).

### 37.9 Early warning scores and recognising deterioration

Track **vital signs** at regular intervals (frequency determined by the score) using a validated aggregate early warning system. **NEWS2** (National Early Warning Score 2) is widely used in the UK and elsewhere:

| Parameter | 3 | 2 | 1 | 0 | 1 | 2 | 3 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Respiratory rate (/min)** | ≤8 | | 9–11 | 12–20 | | 21–24 | ≥25 |
| **SpO₂ Scale 1 (%)** | ≤91 | 92–93 | 94–95 | ≥96 | | | |
| **SpO₂ Scale 2 (%)** (target 88–92%, hypercapnic respiratory failure) | ≤83 | 84–85 | 86–87 | 88–92 or ≥93 on air | 93–94 on O₂ | 95–96 on O₂ | ≥97 on O₂ |
| **Air or oxygen** | | Oxygen | | Air | | | |
| **Systolic BP (mmHg)** | ≤90 | 91–100 | 101–110 | 111–219 | | | ≥220 |
| **Pulse (/min)** | ≤40 | | 41–50 | 51–90 | 91–110 | 111–130 | ≥131 |
| **Consciousness** | | | | Alert | | | CVPU (confusion, voice, pain, unresponsive) |
| **Temperature (°C)** | ≤35.0 | | 35.1–36.0 | 36.1–38.0 | 38.1–39.0 | ≥39.1 | |

**Response thresholds (typical):**

- **Score 0–4 (low):** ward-based response; a **single parameter score of 3 (red score)** needs urgent ward-based response.
- **Score 5–6 (medium):** **urgent response** by a clinician competent in acute illness assessment, with escalation.
- **Score ≥7 (high):** **emergency response** (critical care outreach/resuscitation team), likely transfer to a higher level of care.

Early warning scores support but do not replace **clinical concern**: "the patient looks unwell," "worried" is a legitimate trigger to escalate regardless of the score. Also be aware of **false reassurance** (young, fit patients compensate until late; older adults, immunosuppressed, beta-blocked patients may not mount tachycardia or fever; pregnant women and children have different ranges) (Chapter 44).

### 37.10 Cardiac arrest: recognise and respond

#### Recognition

**Unresponsive and not breathing normally** (absent or only gasping/agonal breaths). Check for a pulse only if trained, taking no more than 10 seconds; if in doubt, **start CPR.**

#### Basic life support (BLS) summary

1. **Shout for help;** call the **resuscitation team/emergency number;** send for the **defibrillator (AED).**
2. **Start chest compressions:** hands on the **centre of the chest (lower half of the sternum),** **rate 100–120/min, depth 5–6 cm** (adult), allow full recoil, minimise interruptions, **30 compressions : 2 breaths** (if trained) or continuous compressions only (if untrained or unwilling to give breaths).
3. **Attach the defibrillator/AED as soon as it arrives** and follow prompts; **shockable rhythms** (VF or pulseless VT) need immediate defibrillation, then 2 minutes of CPR; **non-shockable rhythms** (asystole, PEA): CPR, adrenaline 1 mg IV as early as possible, look for reversible causes.
4. **Airway:** advanced airway when skilled personnel are available, without interrupting compressions.
5. **Drugs (ALS):** **adrenaline 1 mg IV every 3–5 minutes** (as soon as possible in non-shockable rhythms; after the third shock in shockable rhythm), **amiodarone 300 mg after the third shock** (150 mg after the fifth).
6. **Treat reversible causes (4 Hs and 4 Ts):**

| 4 Hs | 4 Ts |
|---|---|
| **H**ypoxia | **T**ension pneumothorax |
| **H**ypovolaemia | **T**amponade (cardiac) |
| **H**yper-/hypokalaemia and other metabolic disorders (acidosis, hypoglycaemia, hypocalcaemia) | **T**oxins (overdose, poisoning) |
| **H**ypothermia / hyperthermia | **T**hrombosis (coronary: MI; pulmonary: PE) |

7. **Post-resuscitation care** after return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC): ABCDE, avoid hypoxia and hyperoxia (SpO₂ 94–98%), avoid hypotension (MAP ≥65), 12-lead ECG (urgent angiography for STEMI/ongoing ischaemia), glucose control, temperature control (avoid fever), neurological assessment, ICU, find and treat cause.
8. **Special circumstances:** pregnancy (manual uterine displacement to the left; consider emergency hysterotomy at about 4–5 minutes if there is no ROSC and gestation ≥20 weeks), hypothermia (continue CPR until warmed), drowning, anaphylaxis, trauma (treat reversible causes first), poisoning.
9. **Decisions:** **do-not-attempt-CPR (DNACPR) decisions** should be made in advance with patients and families when appropriate; ensure they are clear and accessible. **Stop when reversible causes are excluded and the chance of survival is nil,** after senior discussion.

### 37.11 Special situations

#### Children (see Chapter 59)

- **Paediatric assessment triangle (PAT):** **appearance** (tone, interactiveness, consolability, look/gaze, speech/cry), **work of breathing,** **circulation to skin** (pallor, mottling, cyanosis), in the first few seconds.
- **Normal ranges by age** (approximate): 
  - Heart rate: infant (<1 year) 110–160; 1–2 years 100–150; 2–5 years 95–140; 5–12 years 80–120; >12 years 60–100.
  - Respiratory rate: infant 30–40; 1–2 years 25–35; 2–5 years 25–30; 5–12 years 20–25; >12 years 15–20.
  - Systolic BP lower limit: 70 + (2 × age in years) mmHg (hypotension is a **late and ominous** sign in children: they maintain BP until they are close to collapse).
- **Children deteriorate quickly from respiratory failure to arrest;** primary cardiac arrest is rare, usually secondary to hypoxia and shock. Use paediatric formulae (weight-based drug doses, length-based tapes), **early senior help.**
- **Fluids:** 10–20 mL/kg boluses with frequent reassessment.
- **Glucose:** check always; **hypoglycaemia** common in sick children (2 mL/kg of 10% glucose).
- **BLS:** 5 initial rescue breaths, then 15:2 for healthcare professionals, compressions to one-third of chest depth; infants: two-finger or two-thumb encircling technique.

#### Trauma: xABCDE

- **x:** exsanguinating (catastrophic) external haemorrhage controlled first (direct pressure, tourniquet, haemostatic dressing).
- **A:** airway with **cervical spine protection;** **B:** breathing (tension pneumothorax, open pneumothorax, massive haemothorax, flail chest); **C:** circulation (shock, internal bleeding: chest, abdomen, pelvis, long bones, retroperitoneum; **FAST ultrasound;** **tranexamic acid** within 3 hours; **massive transfusion protocol;** pelvic binder); **D:** disability (GCS, pupils; avoid hypoxia and hypotension in head injury); **E:** exposure with prevention of hypothermia.
- **"Trauma triad of death":** hypothermia, acidosis, coagulopathy.
- Early **definitive control of haemorrhage** (surgery, interventional radiology), CT when stable.

#### Pregnancy

- After 20 weeks, the gravid uterus compresses the IVC: **left lateral tilt** or manual uterine displacement to avoid supine hypotension. **Causes of collapse:** haemorrhage (ectopic, abruption, placenta praevia, postpartum), pre-eclampsia/eclampsia, amniotic fluid embolism, pulmonary embolism, peripartum cardiomyopathy, sepsis, anaphylaxis, cardiac disease, local anaesthetic toxicity, magnesium toxicity.
- Call the **obstetric and neonatal teams** early; perimortem caesarean delivery within 5 minutes of arrest if no ROSC (improves maternal and neonatal outcomes).

#### Older adults

- Atypical presentations (confusion, falls), blunted physiological responses (no fever, no tachycardia), polypharmacy (beta-blockers, anticoagulants), limited reserve; **low threshold to escalate;** discuss goals of care early.

#### Immunosuppressed, post-operative, and chronic disease patients

- Risk of sepsis, adrenal insufficiency (steroid-dependent), neutropenia; discuss ceilings of treatment.

### 37.12 Human factors and teamwork

- **Leadership:** a single team leader, clear roles (airway, compressions, drugs, scribe), assignment by name, calm tone.
- **Closed-loop communication:** instruction, repeat-back, confirmation when done.
- **Situational awareness:** regular summaries ("What do we know? What have we done? What next?").
- **Speak up:** all team members can raise concerns (two-challenge rule; the "stop the line" culture).
- **Avoid fixation errors:** reassess if the patient is not responding as expected; ask "What else could this be?"
- **Resource management:** call for help early; use checklists and cognitive aids.
- **Debrief:** after critical events (clinical and emotional).
- **Documentation and handover:** time of events, interventions, responses; use SBAR or I-PASS (Chapter 9).

### 37.13 Worked cases

#### Case 1: Anaphylaxis

*A 29-year-old woman collapses in a restaurant 10 minutes after eating prawns, with a hoarse voice, wheeze, urticaria, facial swelling, HR 140, BP 70/40.*

**ABCDE.** **A:** swelling and hoarseness; **B:** wheeze, SpO₂ 88%; **C:** shock; **D:** drowsy; **E:** urticaria.

**Action.** **IM adrenaline 0.5 mg immediately into the thigh,** call for help, lie flat with legs raised, high-flow oxygen, IV access and fluid bolus, repeat adrenaline in 5 minutes if no improvement, nebulised bronchodilator, call anaesthetist for airway; adjuncts afterwards; monitor 6–12 hours, tryptase, auto-injector, allergy referral.

#### Case 2: Tension pneumothorax

*A 35-year-old cyclist hit by a car has severe dyspnoea, RR 40, SpO₂ 80%, BP 70/40, distended neck veins, absent breath sounds on the right with hyper-resonance.*

**Action.** **Immediate needle decompression** (or finger thoracostomy) of the right chest, oxygen, then chest drain; do not wait for imaging; IV access and fluids for associated blood loss; reassess ABCDE.

#### Case 3: Hypovolaemic shock from GI bleeding

*A 72-year-old man vomits bright red blood and collapses. HR 128, BP 82/48, cold, clammy, GCS 14. Hb 70.*

**Action.** **A:** protect airway, suction; **B:** oxygen; **C:** two large-bore cannulae, crystalloid bolus cautiously, **blood** (major haemorrhage protocol if ongoing), cross-match, correct coagulopathy, **PPI,** urgent endoscopy; **vasoactive drug and antibiotics if variceal;** monitor urine output (Chapter 27).

#### Case 4: Opioid overdose

*A 24-year-old man is found unresponsive with a syringe, RR 4, SpO₂ 75%, pupils pinpoint.*

**Action.** Open airway, bag-valve-mask ventilation with oxygen, **naloxone** titrated (e.g. 0.4 mg IV/IM, repeat), then monitor for recurrence of sedation (observation), check glucose, co-ingestants and paracetamol, address overdose prevention and harm reduction.

#### Case 5: Septic shock

*A 66-year-old woman with fever, rigors, confusion, RR 28, HR 124, BP 78/40, mottled skin, lactate 5.8 mmol/L.*

**Action.** **Sepsis six within the first hour:** oxygen, blood cultures, **IV broad-spectrum antibiotics,** IV fluids (30 mL/kg crystalloid for hypoperfusion), measure lactate, monitor urine output; vasopressor (noradrenaline) if MAP <65 after fluids; source control; ICU (Chapter 39).

#### Case 6: Hypoglycaemic coma

*A 55-year-old man with type 2 diabetes on glibenclamide is found drowsy and sweaty, GCS 9. Glucose 1.6 mmol/L.*

**Action.** Airway positioning, **IV glucose** (and thiamine if alcohol-dependent), recheck glucose in 10–15 minutes, continue infusion, admission for monitoring because of long-acting sulfonylurea effect, find the cause.

#### Case 7: Choking

*A 68-year-old man at a dinner party suddenly cannot speak, clutching his throat, with a silent cough and cyanosis.*

**Action.** Severe airway obstruction: **5 back blows, then 5 abdominal thrusts,** alternating; if he becomes unresponsive, lower to the ground, call for help, start CPR; remove visible foreign body; advanced airway by skilled personnel; observe afterwards (abdominal thrust complications).

#### Case 8: Cardiac arrest (shockable)

*A 58-year-old man collapses at a gym; unresponsive, no normal breathing. A bystander starts CPR; the AED advises a shock.*

**Action.** Continue compressions at 100–120/min, defibrillate immediately, CPR for 2 minutes, repeat; adrenaline and amiodarone per ALS; reversible causes (hypoxia, electrolytes, thrombosis); on ROSC: ABCDE, 12-lead ECG, urgent angiography, temperature management, ICU.

### 37.14 Common pitfalls

- **Not calling for help early.**
- **Starting with the history or examination of the "presenting problem"** before checking airway and breathing.
- **Not treating life threats as they are found** (waiting for the end of the assessment).
- **Failing to reassess** after interventions.
- **Ignoring the respiratory rate** (the first vital sign to change in deterioration; often poorly recorded).
- **Relying on SpO₂** without considering ventilation, perfusion, and hypercapnia.
- **High-flow oxygen indefinitely or in COPD** without titration.
- **Waiting for imaging** in tension pneumothorax or airway obstruction.
- **Giving glucose without checking thiamine need,** or not checking glucose at all.
- **Under-resuscitating (too little, too late)** or **over-resuscitating** (fluid overload; large volumes in haemorrhage).
- **Missing hypotension as a late sign,** especially in children, young adults and fit patients.
- **Delaying adrenaline** in anaphylaxis, or giving it intravenously by inexperienced staff.
- **Failing to examine the whole body (exposure).**
- **Fixation errors:** treating one problem while another worsens.
- **Poor communication and unclear roles** in emergencies.
- **Not discussing treatment ceilings** and resuscitation status early.

---

### 37.15 Key points

- A patient who can speak in full sentences has a patent airway, adequate breathing, and a perfused brain: assess this first.
- **ABCDE:** Airway (open, suction, oxygen), Breathing (rate, effort, SpO₂, chest: decompress tension pneumothorax), Circulation (IV access, fluids, blood, control bleeding, treat cause), Disability (AVPU/GCS, pupils, **glucose**), Exposure (full inspection, temperature).
- Treat life threats as they are found; **reassess after each intervention.**
- Use early warning scores (NEWS2) and clinical concern to call for help; use SBAR.
- Anaphylaxis: **IM adrenaline first,** then oxygen, fluids, and airway support.
- Cardiac arrest: unresponsive and not breathing normally → CPR, defibrillation for VF/pVT, look for 4 Hs and 4 Ts.
- Children, pregnant women, trauma patients and older adults need adapted approaches.
- Teamwork, leadership, closed-loop communication and early escalation save lives.

### 37.16 Self-assessment

1. What does a normal spoken reply to "Can you tell me your name?" tell you?
   *Answer: The airway is patent, breathing is adequate to speak, and the brain is adequately perfused (the patient is conscious and coherent).*

2. List five signs of airway obstruction.
   *Answer (any five): stridor, snoring, gurgling, see-saw breathing, silence with effortful chest movement, hoarseness, cyanosis, inability to speak, swelling of the face or tongue, tracheal tug.*

3. Describe the treatment of anaphylaxis in an adult.
   *Answer: IM adrenaline 0.5 mg (1:1,000) into the anterolateral thigh immediately, repeat every 5 minutes if no improvement, lie flat with legs raised, high-flow oxygen, IV fluid bolus, nebulised bronchodilators, airway support; secondary drugs afterwards; observe and arrange follow-up.*

4. What are the 4 Hs and 4 Ts?
   *Answer: Hypoxia, hypovolaemia, hyper/hypokalaemia and metabolic disorders, hypothermia/hyperthermia; tension pneumothorax, tamponade, toxins, thrombosis (coronary or pulmonary).*

5. Why must blood glucose be measured in every patient with altered consciousness?
   *Answer: Hypoglycaemia is a rapidly reversible and potentially fatal cause that can mimic stroke or other disease; delay causes brain injury.*

6. How is the NEWS2 score used to escalate care?
   *Answer: A score of 0–4 prompts ward-based response (a single 3 in one parameter needs urgent review), 5–6 triggers urgent clinical review, and ≥7 triggers emergency critical care response, in addition to escalation for clinician concern regardless of score.*
