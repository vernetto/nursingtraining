## Chapter 39. Sepsis Recognition

### Learning objectives

After studying this chapter you should be able to:

1. Define sepsis and septic shock according to Sepsis-3, and explain the historical SIRS-based definitions and their limitations.
2. Recognise sepsis early at the bedside, including in older adults, children, pregnant women, and immunosuppressed patients who may not have fever.
3. Use screening and risk-stratification tools appropriately (SIRS, qSOFA, SOFA, NEWS2, paediatric and maternal tools) and understand their strengths and weaknesses.
4. Apply the first-hour sepsis bundle: lactate, cultures, antibiotics, fluids, vasopressors, and source control.
5. Choose empirical antibiotics by source and risk factors, and de-escalate based on cultures and response.
6. Recognise sepsis mimics and avoid both under- and over-diagnosis.
7. Organise follow-up for survivors, understanding post-sepsis syndrome.

---

### 39.1 The importance of sepsis

**Sepsis is a leading cause of death worldwide.** Global estimates suggest about 49 million cases and 11 million sepsis-related deaths per year (almost one in five deaths worldwide), with the highest burden in low- and middle-income countries and in children. In high-income countries, sepsis is one of the most common causes of hospital admission to intensive care and of in-hospital death, and survivors suffer long-term morbidity.

Outcomes depend strongly on **how early the patient is recognised and treated:** each hour of delay in antibiotics in septic shock is associated with increased mortality. Early recognition is a **clinical** skill: it depends on suspecting infection, noticing physiological derangement, and acting without waiting for confirmatory tests.

---

### 39.2 Definitions

#### Sepsis-1 and Sepsis-2: the SIRS era

- **Systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS):** at least **two** of:
  - **Temperature** >38 °C or <36 °C
  - **Heart rate** >90/min
  - **Respiratory rate** >20/min (or PaCO₂ <32 mmHg)
  - **White cell count** >12 × 10⁹/L or <4 × 10⁹/L (or >10% immature forms)
- **Sepsis** = SIRS due to **suspected or confirmed infection;** **severe sepsis** = sepsis with organ dysfunction; **septic shock** = sepsis with hypotension despite adequate fluid resuscitation.
- **Limitations:** SIRS criteria are **sensitive but not specific** (they are met by many hospitalised patients with non-infectious conditions: pancreatitis, trauma, burns, surgery, PE, MI) and miss some patients with organ dysfunction who do not meet two criteria (e.g. normothermic older adults).

#### Sepsis-3 (2016)

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Sepsis** | **Life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated host response to infection.** Organ dysfunction is operationalised as an **acute increase in SOFA score of ≥2 points** from baseline (baseline assumed zero in patients with no known pre-existing dysfunction) |
| **Septic shock** | A subset of sepsis in which **underlying circulatory and cellular/metabolic abnormalities** are profound enough to substantially increase mortality: **persistent hypotension requiring vasopressors to maintain MAP ≥65 mmHg and serum lactate >2 mmol/L despite adequate fluid resuscitation** (in-hospital mortality >40%) |

The key shift: **infection plus organ dysfunction** (not "SIRS plus infection"); the term "severe sepsis" is no longer used.

**Practical use:** the definitions describe a **disease state** and prognosis; they are **not** by themselves treatment triggers. Clinicians must act on **suspicion** of infection with physiological deterioration, without waiting for a SOFA calculation.

#### Terminology to remember

- **Bacteraemia:** viable bacteria in the blood (may be transient and benign).
- **Infection:** invasion by microorganisms causing a host response.
- **Neutropenic sepsis:** sepsis in the neutropenic patient (a medical emergency).
- **Sepsis-associated organ dysfunction:** acute kidney injury, ARDS, encephalopathy, coagulopathy, hepatic dysfunction, shock.

### 39.3 Pathophysiology in brief

1. **Pathogen recognition:** pathogen-associated molecular patterns (lipopolysaccharide, peptidoglycan, flagellin, viral nucleic acids) bind **pattern-recognition receptors** (toll-like receptors) on immune cells; damage-associated molecular patterns amplify the response.
2. **Hyperinflammation and immunosuppression** (simultaneously): release of cytokines (TNF-α, IL-1, IL-6, IL-8), complement activation, neutrophil activation, followed by **immune paralysis** (lymphocyte apoptosis, monocyte deactivation, increased susceptibility to secondary infection).
3. **Endothelial dysfunction:** loss of the glycocalyx, increased permeability (capillary leak, oedema, hypovolaemia), nitric oxide–mediated vasodilatation (vasoplegia), microvascular thrombosis.
4. **Coagulopathy:** activation of coagulation, depletion of anticoagulant proteins, consumption (DIC; Chapter 35).
5. **Myocardial depression** (septic cardiomyopathy), **mitochondrial dysfunction** (cytopathic hypoxia: lactate and organ failure despite adequate oxygen delivery), **altered microcirculation.**
6. **Result:** hypotension, tissue hypoperfusion, **multi-organ dysfunction.**

### 39.4 Recognising sepsis at the bedside

#### The clinical picture

Sepsis is rarely subtle in a young, previously healthy adult who is deteriorating, but **often presents atypically** in vulnerable patients. Suspect sepsis when a patient with a **possible infection** has **any** of:

| Domain | Features |
|---|---|
| **General** | "Looks unwell," rigors, sweats, fever, **or hypothermia,** profound malaise, severe myalgia, non-specific deterioration, **"not right" per family or carers** |
| **Respiratory** | **Tachypnoea (RR ≥22–25),** breathlessness, hypoxia (often the earliest sign) |
| **Cardiovascular** | **Tachycardia (>90–100),** low or falling BP, **mottled or ashen skin,** **capillary refill >2–3 seconds,** cold peripheries (late) or warm flushed (early distributive), reduced urine output |
| **Neurological** | **New confusion, drowsiness, agitation,** reduced GCS (a frequent early sign in older adults) |
| **Renal** | **Oliguria (<0.5 mL/kg/hour), rising creatinine** |
| **Skin** | **Non-blanching rash** (meningococcaemia), cellulitis, erythema, purpura, necrosis, blistering |
| **Hepatic** | Jaundice |
| **Haematological** | Bruising, bleeding (DIC), low platelets |
| **Metabolic** | **Raised lactate,** hypoglycaemia or hyperglycaemia |

#### Source: where is the infection?

Always look for the source: the commonest sources in adults are **lung (pneumonia), urinary tract, abdomen (cholangitis, diverticulitis, perforation, abscess, peritonitis), skin and soft tissue (including necrotising fasciitis), and device- or line-related infection;** also **meningitis/encephalitis, endocarditis, bone and joint (septic arthritis, osteomyelitis, spinal abscess), gynaecological/obstetric** (pelvic inflammatory disease, endometritis, septic abortion, chorioamnionitis), **post-operative sites,** **dental abscess,** **primary bacteraemia,** and unknown. A careful history (symptoms by system, recent procedures, travel, contacts, drugs) and **full exposure examination** (skin, perineum, mouth, lines, wounds, joints, back, rectal and pelvic when indicated) are essential. **Source control** is a core part of therapy.

#### Patients at high risk

- **Extremes of age:** infants (especially <3 months) and older adults (>75).
- **Immunosuppression:** **neutropenia (chemotherapy), corticosteroids and biologic agents, HIV, transplant recipients, asplenia/hyposplenism, malnutrition, chemotherapy.**
- **Comorbidities:** diabetes, cirrhosis, CKD, heart failure, COPD, malignancy, alcohol misuse, dementia.
- **Recent surgery, trauma, burns, invasive procedures, mechanical ventilation.**
- **Indwelling devices:** central lines, urinary catheters, prosthetic joints or valves, pacemakers, drains.
- **Intravenous drug use.**
- **Pregnancy and the post-partum period (up to 6 weeks)** and recent miscarriage or termination.
- **Residents of long-term care facilities.**
- **Recent hospital admission or antibiotic use** (resistant organisms).

#### Atypical presentations

- **Older adults:** may be **afebrile,** with **delirium, falls, reduced appetite, urinary incontinence** or "off legs" as the only sign; temperature and white cell response blunted; **low threshold** for sepsis evaluation.
- **Immunosuppressed (steroids, neutropenia):** minimal localising signs, no pus, no infiltrate on X-ray, muted fever; **fever in neutropenia is an emergency.**
- **Diabetic patients:** hypo- or hyperglycaemia, DKA as a presentation of sepsis.
- **Hypothermia** is associated with higher mortality.
- **Pregnant women:** may remain compensated until a sudden collapse (physiological tachycardia and tachypnoea; Chapter 38).
- **Beta-blocked patients:** no tachycardia.
- **Cirrhosis:** spontaneous bacterial peritonitis may have little abdominal pain.

### 39.5 Screening and assessment tools

Tools help identify patients at risk, standardise escalation, and improve outcomes **when linked to a defined response.** No tool is perfect; clinical judgement remains central.

#### SIRS criteria

High sensitivity (about 80–90%) but low specificity; useful as a "trigger" for considering infection in many screening programmes (e.g. US CMS SEP-1 measure uses SIRS); poor for prognosis.

#### qSOFA (quick SOFA)

Three bedside items: **respiratory rate ≥22/min, altered mentation (GCS <15), systolic BP ≤100 mmHg.** A score of **≥2** identifies patients with suspected infection outside the ICU who are at increased risk of death or prolonged ICU stay.

**Use and misuse:**

- qSOFA was designed as a **prompt** to consider organ dysfunction and escalate, **not** as a diagnostic criterion for sepsis, and **not** as a tool to rule out sepsis.
- Its **sensitivity as a screening tool is low** (many septic patients have a qSOFA score <2, especially early); a negative qSOFA must **not** reassure.
- The Surviving Sepsis Campaign (2021) **recommends against using qSOFA as a single screening tool** compared with SIRS, NEWS, or MEWS.

#### SOFA (Sequential Organ Failure Assessment)

An ICU-oriented score quantifying dysfunction of six organ systems (0–4 each); an acute **rise of ≥2 points** defines sepsis-related organ dysfunction.

| System | 0 | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Respiration** PaO₂/FiO₂ (mmHg) | ≥400 | <400 | <300 | <200 with respiratory support | <100 with respiratory support |
| **Coagulation** platelets (×10³/µL) | ≥150 | <150 | <100 | <50 | <20 |
| **Liver** bilirubin mg/dL (µmol/L) | <1.2 (<20) | 1.2–1.9 (20–32) | 2.0–5.9 (33–101) | 6.0–11.9 (102–204) | >12.0 (>204) |
| **Cardiovascular** | MAP ≥70 | MAP <70 | Dopamine ≤5 or any dobutamine | Dopamine >5, or adrenaline ≤0.1, or noradrenaline ≤0.1 (µg/kg/min) | Dopamine >15, or adrenaline >0.1, or noradrenaline >0.1 |
| **CNS** Glasgow Coma Scale | 15 | 13–14 | 10–12 | 6–9 | <6 |
| **Renal** creatinine mg/dL (µmol/L) or urine output | <1.2 (<110) | 1.2–1.9 (110–170) | 2.0–3.4 (171–299) | 3.5–4.9 (300–440) or <500 mL/day | >5.0 (>440) or <200 mL/day |

#### Early warning scores (NEWS2 and others)

- **NEWS2** (Chapter 37) is recommended in the UK (NICE sepsis guidance updated in 2024) to **assess adults with suspected infection:**
  - **NEWS2 ≥7 (high risk):** immediate review by a senior clinician; **antibiotics within 1 hour** if sepsis is likely (and as soon as possible and within 1 hour if septic shock or high likelihood).
  - **NEWS2 5–6 (medium risk):** review by a senior clinician within 1 hour; antibiotics within **3 hours** if infection is likely and sepsis not excluded.
  - **NEWS2 1–4:** clinical judgement; consider infection and risk factors; escalate if clinical concern.
  - **Clinician concern** about sepsis always overrides a low score.
- Other aggregate scores (MEWS, PEWS in children, MEOWS for obstetric patients) serve similar purposes.

#### Lactate

- **Serum lactate** (venous or arterial) is a marker of hypoperfusion and cellular stress: **>2 mmol/L abnormal; ≥4 mmol/L** denotes severe disease and is a trigger for the full resuscitation bundle. **Not specific:** also raised in seizures, liver disease, metformin, adrenaline, thiamine deficiency, and other conditions; a normal lactate does not exclude sepsis.
- **Repeat if initial >2** to guide resuscitation (lactate-guided therapy reduces mortality in some trials).

#### Biomarkers

- **CRP, procalcitonin (PCT)** can support the diagnosis of bacterial infection and help **de-escalate or stop antibiotics** (PCT-guided duration reduces antibiotic exposure), but **neither should delay treatment in a patient who looks septic.** None is sufficiently accurate to rule sepsis in or out on its own.
- **White cell count** (leucocytosis, leucopenia, neutrophilia, bandaemia); **platelets** (falling); **bilirubin, creatinine, INR.**

#### Organ dysfunction bloods and tests

Blood gas (pH, lactate, PaO₂/FiO₂), FBC, coagulation, U&E, creatinine, LFTs, glucose, CRP/PCT, troponin, ECG, chest X-ray, urinalysis; **imaging** for source (CT, ultrasound) when the source is not obvious and the patient can safely go.

### 39.6 Management: the first hour

All elements are performed **in parallel** by a team, within the first hour of recognition (the **"Hour-1 bundle"** of the Surviving Sepsis Campaign; the UK's **"Sepsis Six"**).

| Step | Action |
|---|---|
| **1. Oxygen** | Give oxygen to maintain SpO₂ ≥94% (88–92% in hypercapnic risk) |
| **2. Cultures** | **Take blood cultures** (at least two sets, before antibiotics, **without delaying antibiotics by more than 45 minutes**), plus cultures from the suspected source: urine, sputum, wound, CSF (if safe), etc. |
| **3. Antibiotics** | **Give IV broad-spectrum antibiotics** with activity against the likely pathogens **as soon as possible:** **within 1 hour for septic shock or high likelihood of sepsis; within 3 hours for possible sepsis without shock** after rapid assessment (SSC 2021) |
| **4. Lactate** | **Measure lactate,** repeat if >2 mmol/L |
| **5. Fluids** | **IV crystalloid resuscitation:** for hypotension or lactate ≥4, give **about 30 mL/kg** within the first 3 hours (SSC 2021: weak recommendation); use **balanced crystalloids;** guide by dynamic assessments and reassess frequently |
| **6. Urine output** | **Monitor** hourly urine output (catheter) |
| **7. Vasopressors** | **If hypotension persists during or after fluids, start noradrenaline** (peripheral initially if necessary) **to maintain MAP ≥65 mmHg** |
| **8. Source control** | **Identify and control the source within 6–12 hours** (earlier for necrotising infection, obstructed infected system) |
| **9. Escalate** | Senior review, ICU outreach, consider ICU for septic shock or organ failure |

#### Antibiotic therapy

- **Early:** every hour of delay in septic shock is associated with increased mortality. Do not delay antibiotics for imaging, specialist opinion, or LP (take blood cultures and give antibiotics immediately in suspected meningitis).
- **Empirical choice depends on:**
  - **Source:** community-acquired pneumonia (beta-lactam + macrolide), urinary (ceftriaxone, piperacillin-tazobactam, or carbapenem if ESBL risk; aminoglycoside), intra-abdominal (piperacillin-tazobactam or carbapenem; metronidazole with cephalosporins), skin and soft tissue (anti-staphylococcal, MRSA cover if risk, **clindamycin** for toxin suppression in necrotising infection or toxic shock), meningitis (ceftriaxone ± amoxicillin for *Listeria,* + vancomycin if resistant pneumococcus risk, dexamethasone), biliary (piperacillin-tazobactam or carbapenem), endocarditis (vancomycin + gentamicin or according to guidelines), neutropenic (anti-pseudomonal beta-lactam), obstetric (broad-spectrum including anaerobes and group A streptococcal coverage with clindamycin).
  - **Community vs hospital acquisition,** recent antibiotics, **local antibiogram,** and patient risk factors for **multidrug-resistant organisms** (previous MRSA, ESBL, *Pseudomonas,* recent hospitalisation, long-term care, immunosuppression, travel to high-resistance regions): broaden empirical cover accordingly.
  - **Allergy, renal and hepatic function,** drug interactions, pregnancy.
- **Combination therapy:** two agents may be used for the sickest patients (until susceptibilities are known) or for specific pathogens; not routine once cultures are available.
- **Dosing:** give the **full loading dose** of the first antibiotics (do not reduce for renal impairment on the first dose), consider **extended or continuous infusions** of beta-lactams after the first dose, and monitor drug levels where appropriate (aminoglycosides, vancomycin).
- **Antifungal and antiviral therapy** if risk factors (candidaemia in ICU patients with broad-spectrum antibiotics, TPN, abdominal surgery; influenza: oseltamivir; HSV encephalitis: aciclovir).
- **De-escalation:** review at **48–72 hours** with culture results and clinical response; narrow, change, or stop therapy; set a planned duration (**typically 7–10 days** for most infections; shorter courses are adequate for many conditions (e.g. 5–7 days for bacteraemia from a urinary source, 7 days for uncomplicated Gram-negative bacteraemia), longer for undrained abscesses, bone, endocarditis, *S. aureus* bacteraemia); **procalcitonin** may guide cessation.

#### Source control

- **Drain abscesses** (percutaneous or surgical), **debride necrotic tissue** (necrotising fasciitis: urgent surgery), **remove infected devices** (central lines, catheters, prosthetic material when possible), **decompress obstructed infected systems** (ureteric stent or nephrostomy for obstructed pyelonephritis; ERCP for cholangitis), **laparotomy for perforation or ischaemic bowel,** remove retained products of conception, treat endocarditis complications.
- **Source control is as important as antibiotics:** delay is associated with increased mortality.

#### Haemodynamic support

- **Fluids:** judicious (see Chapter 38); reassess; **albumin** may be added when large volumes of crystalloid are required; avoid starches.
- **Vasopressors:** **noradrenaline first-line,** then **vasopressin** (add at about 0.25–0.5 µg/kg/min noradrenaline), then adrenaline if needed; dobutamine for persistent hypoperfusion with cardiac dysfunction; **MAP target 65 mmHg.**
- **Corticosteroids:** **hydrocortisone 200 mg/day** (50 mg every 6 hours or infusion) for septic shock with ongoing vasopressor requirement (noradrenaline ≥0.25 µg/kg/min for ≥4 hours).

#### Other supportive therapies

- **Ventilation:** lung-protective settings (tidal volume 6 mL/kg predicted body weight, plateau pressure <30 cmH₂O), prone positioning for moderate–severe ARDS, conservative oxygen targets.
- **Blood transfusion** when Hb <70 g/L (unless active bleeding, ischaemia, or cardiac disease).
- **Glucose control:** insulin infusion to maintain glucose approximately 8–10 mmol/L (start when ≥10 mmol/L / 180 mg/dL).
- **VTE prophylaxis** (LMWH), **stress-ulcer prophylaxis** in patients at risk of GI bleeding, **early enteral nutrition,** **renal replacement therapy** when indicated (not for fluid overload alone), **sedation minimisation,** **early mobilisation.**
- **Sodium bicarbonate** only for severe acidaemia (pH ≤7.2) with AKI.
- **Immunoglobulins, anticoagulants, and other adjuncts** are not routinely recommended; **hydroxyethyl starch and gelatins** should be avoided.
- **Goals-of-care discussion** early in patients with advanced comorbidity or frailty; document treatment limitation decisions.

### 39.7 Special populations

#### Children and infants

- **Sepsis in children** is a leading cause of paediatric mortality worldwide. **Recognition:** altered behaviour (lethargy, irritability, floppiness), **tachycardia, tachypnoea,** prolonged capillary refill, mottling, poor feeding, fever or hypothermia, reduced urine output, non-blanching rash, **hypotension is a late sign.**
- **Tools:** age-specific vital sign ranges; **PEWS;** **NICE traffic-light** system; **Phoenix Sepsis Criteria (2024):** sepsis = suspected infection with **Phoenix Sepsis Score ≥2** (respiratory, cardiovascular, coagulation and neurological dysfunction); **septic shock** = sepsis with at least **1 cardiovascular point** (severe hypotension for age, raised lactate, vasoactive drugs).
- **Neonates:** early-onset (<72 hours: GBS, *E. coli,* *Listeria*), late-onset (>72 hours: coagulase-negative staphylococci, *S. aureus,* Gram-negatives, candida); signs: poor feeding, temperature instability, apnoea, grunting, tachycardia, lethargy, jaundice, seizures; **empirical antibiotics promptly (benzylpenicillin + gentamicin typically)** with full sepsis work-up.
- **Management:** early IV access (or intraosseous), **fluid boluses 10–20 mL/kg** in aliquots with reassessment, antibiotics within 1 hour, **early vasoactive support** (adrenaline for cold shock, noradrenaline for warm shock), treatment of hypoglycaemia, early senior and PICU involvement.
- **Meningococcal disease** and **toxic shock** in children: emergencies (Chapters 21, 34, 41).

#### Pregnancy and the puerperium (maternal sepsis)

- A leading cause of maternal death. Sources: **chorioamnionitis, endometritis (post-partum, post-caesarean), septic abortion, urinary tract infection and pyelonephritis, pneumonia (including influenza), perineal or wound infection (including necrotising fasciitis), mastitis/breast abscess, group A streptococcal infection** (may progress very rapidly).
- **Physiological changes** (tachycardia, tachypnoea, lowered BP) can mask sepsis; use pregnancy-adapted scores (**MEOWS, omqSOFA:** SBP <90, RR >30, altered mentation), a **low threshold** to start broad-spectrum antibiotics (within 1 hour), **early senior obstetric, anaesthetic, and critical care** involvement, **delivery/source control** (evacuation of retained products, caesarean in chorioamnionitis with unstable mother) and fetal assessment.
- **Most antibiotics are safe in pregnancy** when needed; avoid delaying antibiotics for fear of fetal effects.

#### Older adults

- Atypical presentations, blunted fever, delirium, and falls. Common sources: urinary, respiratory, skin, abdominal. **Avoid attributing delirium to a positive urine dipstick** without a systematic search (Chapters 2 and 32). Review pressure areas, devices, medications. Higher mortality; **early discussion of goals** with patient and family.

#### Immunosuppressed and neutropenic patients

- **Neutropenic fever (ANC <0.5 × 10⁹/L with temperature ≥38.3 °C or ≥38.0 °C sustained):** emergency; **broad-spectrum anti-pseudomonal beta-lactam within 1 hour,** cultures, assess for source (lines, mucositis, perianal, lung), consider antifungal therapy if persistent fever (4–7 days); G-CSF per oncology. Steroid-treated patients may be afebrile and lack localising signs; transplant recipients and those on biologics may have unusual infections (CMV, fungal, *Pneumocystis,* TB, *Listeria*).
- **Asplenic or hyposplenic:** overwhelming post-splenectomy infection (pneumococcus, meningococcus, *Haemophilus,* *Capnocytophaga* after dog bite): fulminant, rapid; treat immediately; education, vaccination, and prophylaxis.

#### Others

- **Injecting drug users:** endocarditis, soft tissue infection, spinal infection, bacteraemia.
- **Cirrhosis:** SBP, aspiration, bacterial infections; high mortality.
- **Burns, trauma, post-operative:** infection and non-infectious SIRS overlap; consider anastomotic leak, abscess, line infection.
- **Haematological malignancy:** treat early; ICU access.
- **Returning travellers:** malaria, typhoid, dengue, viral haemorrhagic fevers (Chapter 21).

### 39.8 Sepsis mimics and pitfalls in recognition

**Non-infectious conditions** that can look like sepsis (fever, tachycardia, tachypnoea, hypotension, high lactate):

| Mimic | Clues |
|---|---|
| **Pancreatitis, mesenteric ischaemia, bowel perforation** (non-infectious early phase) | Abdominal findings; lipase; CT |
| **Pulmonary embolism, myocardial infarction, cardiogenic shock** | ECG, troponin, echocardiography |
| **Anaphylaxis** | Urticaria, angioedema, exposure |
| **Adrenal crisis, thyroid storm, myxoedema coma, DKA** | Specific features and tests |
| **Drug reactions:** serotonin syndrome, neuroleptic malignant syndrome, malignant hyperthermia, anticholinergic toxicity, drug fever, withdrawal (alcohol, benzodiazepines), sympathomimetic toxicity | Drug history, rigidity, clonus |
| **Trauma, burns, rhabdomyolysis, haemorrhage** | History |
| **Heat stroke** | Environmental exposure |
| **Vasculitis, haemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH), Still disease, TTP** | Cytopenias, ferritin, rash |
| **Malignancy (lymphoma, leukaemia),** transfusion reaction | |
| **Acute coronary syndrome in older adults** | |
| **Stroke or intracranial haemorrhage** | Neurology |

**Pitfalls:**

- **Anchoring on infection** while another diagnosis is present.
- **Over-reliance on fever, CRP or white count.**
- **"Normal" vital signs in patients on beta-blockers or in athletes** may be misleading.
- **Failing to look for a source or source control** while escalating antibiotics.
- **Giving antibiotics to every febrile patient** without assessment (stewardship) versus **delaying antibiotics for fear of stewardship:** the two risks must be balanced; **suspect sepsis → treat, then reassess** at 48–72 hours.
- **Viral sepsis** (influenza, COVID-19, dengue, other) is real: antibiotics may be given empirically but stopped when a viral diagnosis is made and there is no bacterial co-infection.

### 39.9 After sepsis: recovery and post-sepsis syndrome

Survivors may experience **post-sepsis syndrome:** fatigue, weakness, cognitive impairment, memory and concentration difficulties, anxiety, depression, post-traumatic stress, sleep disturbance, pain, muscle wasting, hair loss, impaired organ function (chronic kidney disease, cardiovascular disease), reduced quality of life; increased risk of readmission, recurrent infection, and mortality in the following year.

**Follow-up:** early rehabilitation (physiotherapy, nutrition), review of medications, vaccinations, cognitive and psychological screening, patient and family information (support organisations), return-to-work planning, and review of the cause (e.g. unexplained recurrent sepsis → immunological or anatomical evaluation). **Plan a review** at about 1–3 months after discharge.

**Prevention:** vaccination (influenza, pneumococcus, COVID-19, meningococcus), infection control, prompt treatment of infections, hand hygiene, antimicrobial stewardship, catheter and line care, early mobilisation, maternal and neonatal health programmes, clean water and sanitation.

---

### 39.10 Systems for better sepsis care

- **Education** of staff and public about sepsis ("Think Sepsis").
- **Screening and escalation protocols** (triage and ward-based) linked to rapid response, with **time-to-antibiotic** and **bundle compliance** audits.
- **Standardised order sets,** antibiotic guidelines, and access to the first dose without delay (emergency drug cupboards, pharmacy support).
- **Electronic alerts** (must be tuned to reduce alert fatigue).
- **Quality improvement** programmes with performance data, feedback, and learning from cases.
- **Public reporting** and national programmes (e.g. NHS England Sepsis programme, Surviving Sepsis Campaign, WHO resolution on sepsis).

### 39.11 Worked cases

#### Case 1: Urosepsis in an older woman (atypical)

*An 82-year-old woman living in a care home has been "off her legs" and confused for 24 hours, with a poor oral intake. Temperature 36.4 °C, HR 106, RR 24, BP 98/52, SpO₂ 94%. She has suprapubic tenderness and a positive urine dipstick (leucocytes, nitrites). Lactate 3.2, creatinine 160 (baseline 80). NEWS2 = 6.*

**Reasoning.** Suspected infection (urinary) with **acute organ dysfunction** (confusion, AKI, raised lactate, borderline hypotension): **sepsis,** even though she is afebrile. qSOFA = 3 (RR ≥22, altered mentation, SBP ≤100) and NEWS2 = 6.

**Action.** Blood and urine cultures, lactate, **IV antibiotics within the hour** (e.g. ceftriaxone, plus gentamicin or according to ESBL risk), IV fluids with reassessment (balanced crystalloid), oxygen, urinary catheter/monitoring, bladder scan and ultrasound/CT to exclude obstruction, senior review, goals-of-care conversation; avoid nephrotoxins where possible; de-escalate at 48–72 hours.

#### Case 2: Pneumonia with septic shock

*A 58-year-old man with 3 days of cough and fever collapses. T 39.4 °C, HR 132, RR 34, BP 78/42, SpO₂ 86% on air, confusion; right lower lobe consolidation on chest X-ray; lactate 6.2.*

**Reasoning.** **Septic shock from severe community-acquired pneumonia** with respiratory failure.

**Action.** High-flow oxygen/CPAP or intubation, blood cultures, **IV antibiotics (beta-lactam + macrolide) within 1 hour,** 30 mL/kg crystalloid, **noradrenaline** for MAP <65, ICU, urine antigens (pneumococcus, Legionella), consider hydrocortisone; ventilate with lung-protective settings; consider influenza and COVID testing.

#### Case 3: Necrotising fasciitis

*A 49-year-old man with type 2 diabetes has a 2-day history of leg pain after minor trauma. The pain is severe and out of proportion; skin dusky with bullae; HR 126, BP 90/50, temperature 38.8 °C, lactate 4.8, creatinine 190.*

**Reasoning.** **Necrotising soft-tissue infection with septic shock.**

**Action.** Immediate broad-spectrum IV antibiotics (carbapenem or piperacillin-tazobactam + vancomycin + **clindamycin**), resuscitation, **emergency surgical debridement** (source control within hours), ICU, repeat debridement; IVIG for streptococcal toxic shock.

#### Case 4: Neutropenic sepsis

*A 41-year-old woman 8 days after chemotherapy has a temperature of 38.6 °C, chills, and HR 118. ANC 0.1 × 10⁹/L. No localising symptoms. BP 100/60.*

**Reasoning.** **Febrile neutropenia** = presumed sepsis.

**Action.** Blood cultures (central line and peripheral), lactate, **anti-pseudomonal beta-lactam (e.g. piperacillin-tazobactam) within 60 minutes,** fluids, source search (lines, mouth, perianal, chest), oncology involvement; add antifungal if fever persists after 4–7 days; isolation as per policy.

#### Case 5: Maternal sepsis

*A 31-year-old woman, 3 days after emergency caesarean delivery, has fever 38.9 °C, RR 26, HR 124, BP 94/52, abdominal pain, and foul lochia. Lactate 3.6. omqSOFA = 1.*

**Reasoning.** **Post-partum sepsis (endometritis, possible wound or intra-abdominal source).**

**Action.** Blood cultures, high vaginal and wound swabs, IV broad-spectrum antibiotics within the hour (e.g. piperacillin-tazobactam or a carbapenem ± clindamycin, with consideration of group A streptococcus), fluids, obstetric and senior review, ultrasound/CT for collection or retained products, source control (drainage, evacuation, surgery) as needed.

#### Case 6: Paediatric sepsis

*A 3-year-old boy with a 12-hour fever is lethargic, with HR 180, RR 40, capillary refill 5 seconds, cold hands and feet, and a few purpuric spots on the legs. BP 80/40.*

**Reasoning.** **Septic shock, probable meningococcal sepsis** (Phoenix criteria: cardiovascular, neurological, coagulation features).

**Action.** Oxygen, IV/IO access, **ceftriaxone immediately (don't wait for investigations),** 10–20 mL/kg isotonic fluid boluses with reassessment, check glucose, early adrenaline infusion for cold shock, PICU transfer, public health notification and contact prophylaxis.

#### Case 7: Normothermic young adult with "normal" vitals but sepsis

*A 24-year-old woman with a recent pelvic infection has severe abdominal pain, nausea, HR 118, RR 22, BP 104/60, temperature 37.1 °C, lactate 3.4, SpO₂ 98%, and is slightly drowsy. qSOFA = 1, SIRS = 2.*

**Reasoning.** qSOFA is negative but she has **tachycardia, tachypnoea, drowsiness, raised lactate, and a likely pelvic source:** sepsis (possible tubo-ovarian abscess).

**Action.** Do not be falsely reassured by qSOFA; cultures, antibiotics (within 1–3 hours based on likelihood), fluids, pregnancy test, imaging, gynaecology review, source control.

#### Case 8: A sepsis mimic

*A 36-year-old woman with a history of depression, on sertraline and recently started tramadol, presents with fever 39.5 °C, agitation, tremor, sweating, diarrhoea, HR 130, BP 150/90, hyperreflexia, ankle clonus, and dilated pupils. WBC 14.*

**Reasoning.** Features of **serotonin syndrome** (clonus, hyperreflexia, hyperthermia, diaphoresis), not sepsis; however, infection should be excluded in parallel.

**Action.** Stop serotonergic drugs, benzodiazepines, cooling, cyproheptadine, ICU if severe; cultures and consider antibiotics if infection is plausible until excluded.

### 39.12 Common pitfalls

- **Waiting for fever or abnormal white count** before considering sepsis.
- **Relying on qSOFA** to rule out sepsis, or using it as a diagnostic test.
- **Delaying antibiotics for imaging, cultures, or senior review.**
- **Delaying cultures** so long that antibiotics are given late (take cultures quickly, but not at the expense of the first dose).
- **Inadequate first-dose antibiotics** (wrong spectrum, reduced dose for renal function, not accounting for local resistance).
- **Not seeking or controlling the source.**
- **Over-reliance on fluids,** causing overload, or **under-treatment** with vasopressors when needed.
- **Missing sepsis in older adults, the immunosuppressed, pregnant, and young children.**
- **Treating the number (lactate, CRP) rather than the patient.**
- **No reassessment at 48–72 hours** with failure to de-escalate.
- **Missing non-infectious mimics.**
- **Forgetting post-sepsis follow-up and rehabilitation.**
- **No discussion of goals of care** in frail patients.

---

### 39.13 Key points

- Sepsis = life-threatening organ dysfunction due to a dysregulated response to infection (SOFA rise ≥2); septic shock = vasopressor-dependent hypotension with lactate >2 despite fluids.
- Recognise sepsis by **suspecting infection** in any deteriorating patient: tachypnoea, tachycardia, hypotension, altered mentation, oliguria, mottling, raised lactate; fever may be absent.
- Use screening tools (NEWS2, SIRS, qSOFA) as prompts, not as rules; clinical concern overrides.
- Hour-1 bundle: lactate, blood cultures, **IV antibiotics,** fluids (up to ~30 mL/kg for hypoperfusion), vasopressors (noradrenaline) for MAP ≥65, and **source control.**
- Choose antibiotics by source, community vs hospital onset, and resistance risk; **de-escalate** at 48–72 hours and set a duration.
- Adapt for children (Phoenix criteria, early inotropes), pregnancy (omqSOFA, early delivery or source control), older adults, and the neutropenic.
- Beware mimics and plan post-sepsis recovery.

### 39.14 Self-assessment

1. State the Sepsis-3 definitions of sepsis and septic shock.
   *Answer: Sepsis is life-threatening organ dysfunction (acute SOFA rise ≥2) caused by a dysregulated host response to infection; septic shock is sepsis with persisting hypotension requiring vasopressors to maintain MAP ≥65 mmHg and lactate >2 mmol/L despite adequate fluid resuscitation.*

2. What are the three components of qSOFA and its limitations?
   *Answer: RR ≥22, altered mentation, SBP ≤100; limitations: low sensitivity, not a diagnostic tool, a negative score does not exclude sepsis.*

3. List the elements of the first-hour sepsis bundle.
   *Answer: Measure lactate, blood cultures before antibiotics, IV broad-spectrum antibiotics, crystalloid (about 30 mL/kg for hypotension or lactate ≥4), vasopressors for MAP <65, plus source control and escalation.*

4. Which group of patients may have sepsis without fever, and name three others at risk of atypical presentation.
   *Answer: Older adults; also the immunosuppressed (steroids, neutropenia), neonates, pregnant women, patients on beta-blockers, and those with cirrhosis or CKD.*

5. Why is source control essential, and give two examples.
   *Answer: Antibiotics alone cannot eradicate undrained or necrotic foci; examples: drainage of abscess, debridement of necrotising fasciitis, ureteric stenting for obstructed pyelonephritis, ERCP for cholangitis, removal of an infected line.*

6. Name five conditions that may mimic sepsis.
   *Answer (any five): pancreatitis, PE, MI, anaphylaxis, adrenal crisis, thyroid storm, DKA, serotonin syndrome, neuroleptic malignant syndrome, heat stroke, vasculitis/HLH, drug withdrawal.*
