# Chapter 50. Hematology and Oncology

## Learning objectives

After studying this chapter you should be able to:

1. Interpret a full blood count (FBC/CBC) and blood film as a diagnostic pattern, not as isolated numbers.
2. Classify anaemia by MCV and reticulocyte response and reach a cause with a stepwise approach.
3. Recognise iron deficiency, B12/folate deficiency, haemolysis, anaemia of chronic disease, haemoglobinopathies and bone marrow failure.
4. Recognise the clinical presentations of acute leukaemia, chronic leukaemias, myeloproliferative neoplasms, myeloma and lymphoma, and know which findings need same-day haematology contact.
5. Approach isolated abnormalities: leukocytosis, neutropenia, lymphocytosis, eosinophilia, thrombocytopenia, thrombocytosis, polycythaemia.
6. Recognise haematological and oncological emergencies: febrile neutropenia, tumour lysis, hyperviscosity, spinal cord compression, superior vena cava obstruction, hypercalcaemia of malignancy, TTP/HUS, DIC.
7. Identify the warning signs of cancer in primary care, use the "urgent suspected cancer" pathways sensibly, and avoid both delay and over-investigation.
8. Describe the diagnostic pathway for cancer: imaging, tissue diagnosis, staging, and multidisciplinary review.

> **Note on thresholds and doses.** Values below follow common adult reference ranges and widely used guidelines (WHO, ASH, ESMO, NICE, BSH). Reference ranges vary with laboratory, altitude, ethnicity, age and pregnancy. Verify against your local laboratory and guideline before acting.

## 1. Reading the blood count

### 1.1 The components

| Parameter | Adult reference (approx.) | What it tells you |
|---|---|---|
| Haemoglobin (Hb) | Men 130–175 g/L; women 120–155 g/L | Oxygen-carrying capacity; WHO anaemia: < 130 g/L (men), < 120 g/L (non-pregnant women), < 110 g/L (pregnancy) |
| MCV | 80–100 fL | Red cell size: micro-, normo-, macrocytic |
| MCH / MCHC | 27–33 pg / 320–360 g/L | Haemoglobin content; low in iron deficiency and thalassaemia |
| RDW | 11.5–14.5% | Anisocytosis; raised early in iron deficiency and mixed deficiencies |
| Reticulocytes | 0.5–2.5% (absolute 25–100 × 10⁹/L) | Marrow response; low in production failure, high in haemolysis or bleeding |
| WBC | 4–11 × 10⁹/L | Total leukocytes |
| Neutrophils | 2.0–7.5 × 10⁹/L | Infection defence |
| Lymphocytes | 1.0–4.0 × 10⁹/L | |
| Eosinophils | < 0.5 × 10⁹/L | |
| Platelets | 150–400 × 10⁹/L | |

### 1.2 Three rules of interpretation

1. **Look at all three lines (red cells, white cells, platelets).** Single-line abnormalities are usually reactive or nutritional; multi-line abnormalities (pancytopenia, bicytopenia, or high counts in several lines) point to the marrow.
2. **Compare with previous results.** A haemoglobin of 118 g/L is normal for one person and a 30 g/L fall for another. The trend matters more than the threshold.
3. **Look at the film.** An experienced pair of eyes (or a haematology scientist) can tell you in a minute what an analyser takes a day of tests to suggest: blasts, schistocytes, spherocytes, rouleaux, target cells, hypersegmented neutrophils, parasites, left shift, toxic granulation, and platelet clumps (a classic cause of spurious thrombocytopenia).

> **Spurious results.** Platelet clumping in EDTA tubes (repeat in citrate), cold agglutinins (spurious high MCV, low red cell count), severe hyperglycaemia or hypertriglyceridaemia (spurious indices), and delays in processing all produce results that do not match the patient. If the result does not fit the patient, question the result.

## 2. Anaemia

### 2.1 Clinical assessment

Symptoms depend on speed of onset, severity and cardiopulmonary reserve: fatigue, exertional breathlessness, palpitations, headache, light-headedness, angina in patients with coronary disease, claudication, tinnitus, restless legs (iron deficiency), pica (ice, clay), cold intolerance. Chronic anaemia is remarkably well tolerated; a young woman with Hb 70 g/L may come only for tiredness, while an older man with Hb 100 g/L may present with heart failure.

Signs: conjunctival and palmar pallor (poor sensitivity: absence does not exclude anaemia), tachycardia, flow murmur, koilonychia, angular stomatitis, glossitis (iron, B12, folate), jaundice (haemolysis, B12, liver), splenomegaly, lymphadenopathy, bone tenderness, petechiae and bruising (marrow failure).

**Key history points**: blood loss (menstrual history, GI symptoms, melaena, haematuria, epistaxis), diet (vegan, restricted), pregnancy, malabsorption (coeliac disease, bariatric surgery, Crohn disease), alcohol, drugs (NSAIDs, aspirin, anticoagulants, metformin, PPIs, methotrexate, trimethoprim, azathioprine, chemotherapy, antiepileptics, dapsone, primaquine), family history and ethnic origin (haemoglobinopathies, G6PD deficiency), travel (malaria, hookworm, schistosomiasis), chronic disease (renal, inflammatory, thyroid), and constitutional symptoms (cancer, lymphoma, TB).

### 2.2 A stepwise approach

**Step 1: Is it real, and how severe?** Confirm with a repeat sample if unexpected. Haemodilution (pregnancy, fluid overload) lowers Hb without red cell mass reduction.

**Step 2: Classify by MCV and reticulocytes.**

| MCV | Common causes |
|---|---|
| **Microcytic** (< 80 fL) | Iron deficiency (commonest worldwide), thalassaemia trait, anaemia of chronic disease (some), sideroblastic anaemia, lead poisoning |
| **Normocytic** (80–100 fL) | Acute blood loss, anaemia of chronic disease/inflammation, CKD, early iron deficiency, mixed deficiencies, haemolysis (with reticulocytosis), marrow infiltration or failure, endocrine (hypothyroidism, hypopituitarism) |
| **Macrocytic** (> 100 fL) | B12 or folate deficiency, alcohol, liver disease, hypothyroidism, drugs (hydroxycarbamide, methotrexate, azathioprine, zidovudine), myelodysplastic syndromes, reticulocytosis, aplastic anaemia, cold agglutinins (spurious) |

Reticulocyte count: **high** (> 2–3% or absolute > 100 × 10⁹/L) means loss or destruction with intact marrow (bleeding, haemolysis, recovery after treatment). **Low or inappropriately normal** means production failure.

**Step 3: Targeted tests.**

- Microcytic: ferritin, transferrin saturation (TSAT), CRP. Ferritin < 30 µg/L (some use < 45) indicates iron deficiency in the absence of inflammation; in inflammation, ferritin up to 100 µg/L may still be iron deficiency, and TSAT < 20% supports it. Hb electrophoresis or HPLC if ferritin normal and MCV disproportionately low for the degree of anaemia (red cell count high, Mentzer index MCV/RBC < 13 suggests thalassaemia trait).
- Macrocytic: B12, folate (red cell folate or serum), TSH, LFTs, alcohol history, reticulocytes, film (hypersegmented neutrophils, oval macrocytes). Anti-intrinsic factor antibodies if B12 low.
- Normocytic: reticulocytes, creatinine/eGFR, CRP/ESR, ferritin, LDH, haptoglobin, bilirubin, direct antiglobulin test (DAT) if haemolysis suspected, film, TSH.
- Pancytopenia or unexplained cytopenia: film, B12/folate, HIV, hepatitis, then bone marrow aspirate and biopsy.

**Step 4: Find the cause, not just the number.** "Iron deficiency" is a laboratory finding, not a diagnosis.

### 2.3 Iron deficiency anaemia

**Causes by group**

- *Loss*: heavy menstrual bleeding (most common in premenopausal women), GI blood loss (peptic ulcer, NSAIDs, gastric or colorectal cancer, angiodysplasia, hookworm, IBD), haematuria, repeated blood donation.
- *Reduced intake or absorption*: vegetarian/vegan diet, coeliac disease, atrophic gastritis, *Helicobacter pylori*, bariatric surgery, PPI use.
- *Increased need*: pregnancy, growth.

**Do not accept "diet" or "periods" as the explanation in men, postmenopausal women or anyone over 50 until the gastrointestinal tract has been assessed.** Bidirectional endoscopy (gastroscopy plus colonoscopy) with coeliac serology is the standard approach; consider CT colonography if colonoscopy is unsuitable. Two lesions (upper and lower) coexist in a minority of patients.

**Treatment**: oral iron on alternate days or once daily at a modest dose (e.g., ferrous sulfate 200 mg or equivalent, giving 60–65 mg elemental iron, on alternate mornings, with vitamin C or an acidic drink, apart from PPIs, tea and calcium). Expect Hb to rise by about 10 g/L in 1–2 weeks and normalise in 6–8 weeks; continue for 3 months after correction to replenish stores. Failure of response suggests non-adherence, continued loss, malabsorption, wrong diagnosis or mixed deficiency. **Intravenous iron** (ferric carboxymaltose, ferric derisomaltose, iron sucrose) for intolerance, malabsorption, ongoing losses, CKD, heart failure with iron deficiency, pregnancy after the first trimester when rapid correction is needed, and pre-operative correction. Monitor phosphate after ferric carboxymaltose.

### 2.4 Vitamin B12 and folate deficiency

**B12 deficiency** causes: pernicious anaemia (autoimmune gastritis; anti-intrinsic factor antibodies), atrophic gastritis in older adults, gastrectomy/ileal resection, Crohn disease, vegan diet, metformin, PPIs, *Diphyllobothrium latum*, nitrous oxide misuse. Presents with macrocytic anaemia (not always), glossitis, paraesthesia, loss of vibration and position sense (subacute combined degeneration of the cord), cognitive change, depression. **Neurological involvement can occur without anaemia.**

Interpretation: serum B12 < 150 pmol/L (200 ng/L) is deficient; 150–250 is indeterminate: check methylmalonic acid or holotranscobalamin. Treat: hydroxocobalamin 1 mg IM on alternate days for 2 weeks (loading) in neurological involvement per local guidance (then every 2 months), or 1 mg IM 3 times weekly for 2 weeks then every 3 months for pernicious anaemia. High-dose oral B12 (1–2 mg daily) is effective in dietary deficiency and in many with malabsorption. Check potassium early in severe anaemia: hypokalaemia can appear during brisk marrow recovery.

**Folate deficiency**: poor diet (alcohol, elderly), pregnancy, haemolysis, malabsorption, drugs (methotrexate, trimethoprim, phenytoin, sulfasalazine). Treat with folic acid 5 mg daily for 4 months; **never give folic acid alone in unexplained macrocytic anaemia before excluding B12 deficiency**, because it can mask haematological signs while neurological damage progresses. Peri-conceptional folic acid (400 µg daily, 5 mg in high-risk groups) prevents neural tube defects.

### 2.5 Anaemia of chronic disease/inflammation

Normocytic (or mildly microcytic), low serum iron, low TSAT, normal or raised ferritin, raised CRP/ESR. Driven by hepcidin: iron is sequestered. Treat the underlying disease. Consider concurrent iron deficiency (ferritin < 100 with TSAT < 20% in inflammatory states). Anaemia of CKD is addressed in chapter 49.

### 2.6 Haemolytic anaemia

Suspect when anaemia comes with jaundice, dark urine, splenomegaly, high reticulocytes, high LDH, high indirect bilirubin, low haptoglobin, and film abnormalities.

- **Immune**: warm autoimmune haemolytic anaemia (DAT positive for IgG ± C3; associated with CLL, lymphoma, SLE, drugs), cold agglutinin disease (cold exposure, acrocyanosis, DAT positive for C3), drug-induced, transfusion reactions, paroxysmal cold haemoglobinuria.
- **Membrane/enzyme**: hereditary spherocytosis (spherocytes, increased MCHC, family history, splenomegaly, gallstones), G6PD deficiency (X-linked, episodic haemolysis after infection, fava beans, oxidant drugs such as primaquine, dapsone, rasburicase, nitrofurantoin; "bite" and "blister" cells; test G6PD activity at a time of steady state, as it can read falsely normal immediately after a haemolytic crisis).
- **Haemoglobinopathies**: sickle cell disease, thalassaemias.
- **Mechanical/microangiopathic**: schistocytes in TTP, HUS, DIC, malignant hypertension, mechanical heart valves, HELLP.
- **Infection**: malaria (see chapter 53), babesiosis, clostridial sepsis.
- **PNH**: haemolysis with haemoglobinuria, thrombosis, cytopenias; flow cytometry.

Folic acid 5 mg daily is given in chronic haemolysis (increased turnover). Warm AIHA: prednisolone 1 mg/kg/day, then rituximab if relapse; transfuse only when life-threatening, ideally with specialist input and crossmatch support.

### 2.7 Haemoglobinopathies

**Sickle cell disease (HbSS, HbSC, HbS/β-thalassaemia)**: Autosomal recessive; high prevalence in people of African, Mediterranean, Middle Eastern and Indian descent. Clinical pattern: chronic haemolytic anaemia and vaso-occlusive crises (pain in bones, chest, abdomen), acute chest syndrome (fever, chest pain, new infiltrates, hypoxia: **emergency**), stroke (children and adults), splenic sequestration in children, aplastic crisis (parvovirus B19), priapism, leg ulcers, retinopathy, infections with encapsulated organisms (functional asplenia), osteonecrosis, nephropathy. Management of crisis: early, adequate analgesia (opioids within 30 minutes of arrival, per local pathway), hydration (avoid overhydration), oxygen if hypoxic, treat infection, incentive spirometry; transfusion/exchange for stroke, acute chest syndrome with severe hypoxia, multi-organ failure. Preventive: hydroxycarbamide, penicillin prophylaxis in children, vaccinations, folate; newer agents (crizanlizumab, voxelotor withdrawn in some regions, L-glutamine), and curative therapies (allogeneic transplant, gene therapy) in selected patients. **Sickle trait** rarely causes problems, though it may cause haematuria, splenic infarction at altitude, and exertional rhabdomyolysis.

**Thalassaemia**: α-thalassaemia (deletion of 1–4 α genes) is common in South-East Asia, the Mediterranean and Africa; β-thalassaemia is common in the Mediterranean, Middle East, South Asia and South-East Asia. Trait: microcytosis (MCV often 60–75 fL) with normal or mildly reduced Hb and a high red cell count, normal ferritin; HbA2 raised in β-trait (> 3.5%); α-trait may have a normal electrophoresis (DNA analysis needed). Major forms (transfusion-dependent β-thalassaemia major, HbH disease, Hb Bart hydrops): chronic transfusion, chelation, and now gene therapy or transplantation. Antenatal screening and carrier counselling are important; avoid unnecessary iron treatment in trait.

### 2.8 Aplastic anaemia, myelodysplasia and marrow failure

**Aplastic anaemia**: pancytopenia with a hypocellular marrow; causes: idiopathic autoimmune, drugs (chloramphenicol, carbamazepine, sulfonamides), viral infection (hepatitis, parvovirus, EBV, HIV), radiation, inherited marrow failure. Severe cases (neutrophils < 0.5 × 10⁹/L) are emergencies: infection and bleeding. Treatment: allogeneic transplant (young with a matched donor), or immunosuppression (ATG + ciclosporin) with eltrombopag.

**Myelodysplastic syndromes (MDS)**: clonal disorders of older adults (median age about 70): macrocytic anaemia, neutropenia or thrombocytopenia, dysplasia on film and marrow, risk of transformation to acute myeloid leukaemia (AML). Evaluation by marrow, cytogenetics and molecular testing; supportive care (transfusion, ESA, iron chelation), lenalidomide for del(5q), hypomethylating agents, transplantation in fit patients.

### 2.9 Transfusion thresholds

Current evidence supports **restrictive transfusion**: Hb threshold about 70 g/L for most hospitalised adults, 80 g/L for pre-existing cardiovascular disease or orthopaedic/cardiac surgery, with higher thresholds individualised for acute coronary syndrome (evidence evolving, usually 80–100 g/L), haematological malignancy or chronic transfusion regimes. Give one unit at a time and reassess. Do not transfuse to correct a number in a stable, compensated patient with treatable deficiency (iron, B12). In massive haemorrhage use a major haemorrhage protocol with balanced products and tranexamic acid early (see chapter 42).

## 3. White cell and platelet disorders

### 3.1 Neutrophilia and leukocytosis

- **Reactive** (the vast majority): bacterial infection, inflammation, steroids, stress, smoking, pregnancy, post-splenectomy, tissue necrosis, exercise, drugs (lithium, G-CSF). Film shows left shift, toxic granulation, Döhle bodies.
- **Clonal**: CML (marked leukocytosis with basophilia and a spectrum of myeloid precursors; Philadelphia chromosome, *BCR::ABL1*), other myeloproliferative neoplasms, CMML.
- **Leukaemoid reaction**: extreme reactive leukocytosis, differentiated from CML by high leukocyte alkaline phosphatase (historical), absence of BCR::ABL1, and clinical context.

### 3.2 Lymphocytosis

- **Reactive**: viral infection (EBV, CMV, pertussis in children), toxoplasmosis, acute stress.
- **Clonal**: chronic lymphocytic leukaemia (CLL; persistent lymphocytosis > 5 × 10⁹/L in older adults, smear cells, flow cytometry), lymphoma in leukaemic phase, hairy cell leukaemia, Sézary syndrome.
- **Monoclonal B-cell lymphocytosis**: asymptomatic precursor; low rate of progression.

### 3.3 Neutropenia

Defined as neutrophils < 1.5 × 10⁹/L (lower normal in people of African, Middle Eastern and some other ancestries: "benign ethnic neutropenia" in which counts may be 1.0–1.5 × 10⁹/L without infection risk). Severe: < 0.5 × 10⁹/L.

Causes: viral infections (transient), drugs (chemotherapy, clozapine, carbimazole, sulfasalazine, antithyroid drugs, trimethoprim–sulfamethoxazole, penicillins in high dose, deferiprone), autoimmune (SLE, Felty syndrome), B12/folate deficiency, marrow disorders (aplastic anaemia, MDS, leukaemia), hypersplenism, congenital. **Drug-induced agranulocytosis** (e.g., clozapine, antithyroid drugs) presents with sore throat, fever and mouth ulcers: stop the drug and seek urgent help.

**Febrile neutropenia** (a single temperature ≥ 38.3 °C, or ≥ 38.0 °C sustained for 1 hour, with neutrophils < 0.5 × 10⁹/L or expected to fall below it) is a medical emergency, particularly after chemotherapy. Give broad-spectrum anti-pseudomonal IV antibiotics (e.g., piperacillin–tazobactam) **within 60 minutes** of presentation without waiting for neutrophil counts if the patient is on chemotherapy and unwell. Culture blood (including from any central line), urine; chest X-ray. Add vancomycin or equivalent only for line infection, skin/soft tissue infection, pneumonia or haemodynamic instability; add antifungals if fever persists after 4–7 days. Use risk scoring (MASCC) to select patients for outpatient oral therapy.

### 3.4 Eosinophilia

Absolute count > 0.5 × 10⁹/L; > 1.5 × 10⁹/L is "hypereosinophilia" and carries a risk of tissue damage (heart, lungs, skin, nerves). Causes: **allergy** (asthma, atopic dermatitis, drug reaction, including DRESS), **parasites** (helminths with tissue migration: strongyloidiasis, schistosomiasis, filariasis, toxocariasis, hookworm; check stool and serology in people from or travelling to endemic areas, and before immunosuppression), **skin disease**, **adrenal insufficiency**, **vasculitis** (EGPA), **malignancy** (Hodgkin, T-cell lymphomas, solid tumours), **hypereosinophilic syndrome** and clonal eosinophilia (FIP1L1::PDGFRA, treat with imatinib), eosinophilic oesophagitis, and cholesterol embolisation.

### 3.5 Thrombocytopenia

Platelets < 150 × 10⁹/L. Bleeding risk: spontaneous bleeding is rare above 30 × 10⁹/L, mucocutaneous bleeding and petechiae occur at 10–30, and the risk of serious spontaneous haemorrhage (ICH, GI) is highest below 10 × 10⁹/L.

**Pseudothrombocytopenia first**: repeat in a citrate tube and look at the film.

**By mechanism**:

- *Decreased production*: MDS, aplastic anaemia, leukaemia, marrow infiltration, B12/folate deficiency, alcohol, drugs, viral infection (HIV, hepatitis C, parvovirus), liver disease (low thrombopoietin and splenic sequestration), congenital.
- *Increased destruction*: immune thrombocytopenia (ITP), drug-induced (heparin, quinine, linezolid, valproate, trimethoprim–sulfamethoxazole, carbamazepine), autoimmune disease (SLE, antiphospholipid syndrome), infection (HIV, HCV, *H. pylori*), lymphoproliferative disease.
- *Consumption/microangiopathic*: DIC, TTP, HUS, HELLP, malignant hypertension, sepsis.
- *Sequestration*: hypersplenism (cirrhosis, portal hypertension).
- *Dilutional*: massive transfusion.
- *Gestational thrombocytopenia*: mild (> 70 × 10⁹/L), in late pregnancy, resolves after delivery.

**Immune thrombocytopenia (ITP)**: isolated thrombocytopenia with otherwise normal film and well patient; a diagnosis of exclusion. Children often have acute, self-limited disease after infection. Adults have chronic ITP. Management based on bleeding and platelet count rather than count alone: observation if > 30 × 10⁹/L without bleeding; first-line prednisolone or dexamethasone (with IVIG for bleeding or urgent need to raise count); second-line thrombopoietin receptor agonists (eltrombopag, romiplostim, avatrombopag), rituximab, fostamatinib, or splenectomy. Test for HIV, hepatitis C and *H. pylori* in adults.

**Heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT)**: platelet fall > 50% (usually to 30–70 × 10⁹/L) 5–10 days after starting heparin (earlier if prior exposure in last 100 days), with **paradoxical thrombosis**. Use the 4Ts score (Thrombocytopenia, Timing, Thrombosis, oTher causes). Stop all heparin (including flushes and heparin-coated lines), start a non-heparin anticoagulant (argatroban, bivalirudin, fondaparinux, DOAC in selected settings), test for anti-PF4 antibodies, and avoid platelet transfusions unless life-threatening bleeding. Do not start warfarin until platelets recover (risk of venous limb gangrene).

**TTP** (thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura): severe thrombocytopenia (often < 30 × 10⁹/L), microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (schistocytes, high LDH, low haptoglobin, negative DAT), and variable renal impairment, fever or neurological signs; due to severe ADAMTS13 deficiency (< 10%). Use the PLASMIC score to assess pre-test probability. **Emergency**: urgent plasma exchange, steroids, caplacizumab and rituximab; delay costs lives. **Avoid platelet transfusions** unless life-threatening bleeding. **HUS** (typical, shiga-toxin associated in children with bloody diarrhoea; atypical/complement-mediated) manifests predominantly with renal failure.

### 3.6 Thrombocytosis

Platelets > 450 × 10⁹/L. **Reactive** in the vast majority: iron deficiency, infection, inflammation, post-splenectomy, bleeding, malignancy, recovery from thrombocytopenia. **Clonal**: essential thrombocythaemia (JAK2, CALR, MPL mutations; thrombosis, erythromelalgia, bleeding in extreme counts due to acquired von Willebrand syndrome). A persistent, unexplained platelet count > 450 with normal CRP and ferritin warrants mutation testing and haematology review. Persistent thrombocytosis in an adult, especially with iron deficiency, also raises the possibility of occult cancer.

### 3.7 Polycythaemia (erythrocytosis)

Raised Hb (> 165 g/L in men, > 160 g/L in women) or haematocrit (> 0.49 in men, > 0.48 in women), per WHO; confirm on repeated samples and exclude relative polycythaemia (dehydration, diuretics, alcohol, smoking).

- **Secondary**: hypoxia (COPD, sleep apnoea, high altitude, right-to-left shunt), carbon monoxide (smokers), testosterone therapy/anabolic steroids, SGLT2 inhibitors (mild), erythropoietin-secreting tumours (renal cell carcinoma, hepatocellular carcinoma, cerebellar haemangioblastoma), renal artery stenosis, post-renal transplant.
- **Polycythaemia vera (PV)**: JAK2 V617F mutation in >95%; low serum erythropoietin; pruritus after hot bath (aquagenic pruritus), erythromelalgia, splenomegaly, thrombosis (arterial and venous, including splanchnic and cerebral venous thrombosis), gout, headaches, visual disturbances. Management: venesection to haematocrit < 0.45, low-dose aspirin, cytoreduction (hydroxycarbamide, ruxolitinib, ropeginterferon) in high-risk patients; manage cardiovascular risk factors.

## 4. Haematological malignancies

### 4.1 Acute leukaemia

**Presentation** (days to weeks): fatigue and pallor (anaemia), fever and infections (neutropenia), bruising, bleeding, petechiae, gum bleeding, epistaxis (thrombocytopenia), bone pain, hepatosplenomegaly, lymphadenopathy, gum hypertrophy (monocytic AML), skin infiltrates, testicular enlargement (ALL), CNS involvement (headache, cranial nerve palsies), mediastinal mass (T-ALL), and in acute promyelocytic leukaemia (APL), **severe coagulopathy** with DIC.

**Blood count**: high, normal or low WBC but with **circulating blasts**, anaemia and thrombocytopenia. Film with blasts ± Auer rods. Pancytopenia with blasts, or a unexplained severe cytopenia, requires **same-day haematology discussion**. A suspected acute leukaemia is a medical emergency: avoid transfusing platelets for non-bleeding patients without advice, avoid lumbar puncture, start tumour lysis prophylaxis, and transfer to a centre that can give induction therapy.

**APL (t(15;17), PML::RARA)** is a haematological emergency within the emergency. Haemorrhage (intracranial, pulmonary) is the main cause of early death. Start **all-trans retinoic acid (ATRA)** at the first suspicion, before genetic confirmation, with platelet and fibrinogen support. APL is highly curable (>90%) with ATRA plus arsenic trioxide.

**Diagnosis**: flow cytometry (immunophenotype), bone marrow, cytogenetics, molecular genetics. **Treatment**: AML: induction chemotherapy (anthracycline plus cytarabine, or venetoclax with a hypomethylating agent in unfit patients), then consolidation or allogeneic transplant by risk; ALL: multi-agent chemotherapy, tyrosine kinase inhibitors in Philadelphia-positive disease, blinatumomab, inotuzumab, CAR-T cell therapy.

### 4.2 Chronic myeloid leukaemia (CML)

Often found incidentally on a blood count (neutrophilia with myelocytes/metamyelocytes, basophilia, thrombocytosis), or with fatigue, weight loss, night sweats, left upper quadrant fullness from splenomegaly. BCR::ABL1 on PCR or FISH is diagnostic. Treatment is with tyrosine kinase inhibitors (imatinib, dasatinib, nilotinib, bosutinib, ponatinib, asciminib): most patients with chronic-phase disease have near-normal life expectancy; some can attempt treatment-free remission. Monitor molecular response with quantitative PCR.

### 4.3 Chronic lymphocytic leukaemia (CLL)

The commonest adult leukaemia in Western countries, median age at diagnosis about 70. Often asymptomatic, found through a high lymphocyte count; may produce lymphadenopathy, splenomegaly, anaemia (including autoimmune haemolysis), thrombocytopenia (including ITP), recurrent infections (hypogammaglobulinaemia). Diagnosis from the blood film (smudge cells) and flow cytometry (CD5+, CD19+, CD23+). Stage with the Rai or Binet systems. **"Watch and wait" is the standard for asymptomatic early-stage disease**; treatment indications are progressive marrow failure, massive or symptomatic nodes or spleen, constitutional symptoms (B symptoms), autoimmune cytopenias not responding to steroids, or rapid lymphocyte doubling. Targeted therapy (BTK inhibitors such as ibrutinib, acalabrutinib, zanubrutinib; venetoclax with obinutuzumab) has largely replaced chemoimmunotherapy; test for *TP53* mutation and deletion 17p, and IGHV status, before treatment. Richter transformation (to aggressive lymphoma) is suggested by rapidly enlarging nodes, high LDH, fever and weight loss.

### 4.4 Lymphoma

**Presentation**: painless lymphadenopathy (rubbery, non-tender, progressive), B symptoms (fever > 38 °C, drenching night sweats, weight loss > 10% in 6 months), pruritus, alcohol-induced pain in nodes (Hodgkin), mediastinal mass causing cough or SVC obstruction, splenomegaly, extranodal disease (stomach, skin, CNS, bone, thyroid, testis), cytopenias, hypercalcaemia, and in primary CNS lymphoma, focal neurological signs or confusion.

**Hodgkin lymphoma**: bimodal incidence (young adults and older adults); contiguous nodal spread; Reed–Sternberg cells; very high cure rates with ABVD-type regimens, with brentuximab vedotin and checkpoint inhibitors in advanced disease or relapse. Late effects (secondary cancers, cardiomyopathy, lung toxicity, infertility, hypothyroidism) need long-term follow-up.

**Non-Hodgkin lymphoma (NHL)**: heterogeneous. Indolent (follicular lymphoma, marginal zone, small lymphocytic): slowly progressive, often incurable but long-lived; "watch and wait" if asymptomatic. Aggressive (diffuse large B-cell lymphoma, Burkitt, mantle cell, T-cell lymphomas): rapid growth, can be cured. DLBCL treated with R-CHOP (rituximab plus cyclophosphamide, doxorubicin, vincristine, prednisolone) or polatuzumab-based regimens, with about 60–70% cure. Burkitt lymphoma is the fastest-growing human tumour and presents as an abdominal or jaw mass in children (endemic, EBV-associated) with high risk of tumour lysis. **Excisional lymph node biopsy** (not fine needle aspiration alone) is needed for diagnosis and subtyping; core biopsy if excision is not feasible. Staging with PET-CT (Ann Arbor/Lugano), marrow biopsy in selected cases, and LDH.

**When to biopsy a lymph node**: persistent (> 4–6 weeks) unexplained lymphadenopathy, node > 2 cm, hard or fixed, supraclavicular node (any size; very high risk of malignancy), generalised lymphadenopathy with systemic symptoms, or progressive enlargement. A trial of antibiotics for a single non-tender node is a reasonable step only when there is a clear infective cause; do not give corticosteroids before biopsy because they can obscure the diagnosis of lymphoma.

### 4.5 Multiple myeloma and plasma cell disorders

Myeloma is a clonal plasma cell malignancy, median age about 70. Remember the **CRAB** features: hypercalcaemia, renal impairment, anaemia, bone lesions; and newer biomarkers of malignancy (≥ 60% clonal plasma cells in marrow, involved:uninvolved free light chain ratio ≥ 100, > 1 focal lesion on MRI).

Presentation: bone pain (back pain with lytic lesions or vertebral fractures; pain worse at night and with movement), fatigue, recurrent infections, renal failure (light chain cast nephropathy), hypercalcaemia (thirst, confusion, constipation), hyperviscosity (rare), amyloidosis, carpal tunnel syndrome, peripheral neuropathy, spinal cord compression (emergency).

**Screening tests for suspected myeloma** (older adult with unexplained back pain, anaemia, raised ESR, renal impairment, hypercalcaemia): serum protein electrophoresis with immunofixation, **serum free light chains**, urine electrophoresis for Bence Jones protein, FBC, calcium, creatinine, albumin, β2-microglobulin, LDH. Note a very high ESR/plasma viscosity with a normal CRP (rouleaux on film). Imaging: low-dose whole-body CT, MRI or PET-CT (plain skeletal survey is less sensitive). Marrow biopsy with cytogenetics.

**MGUS** (monoclonal gammopathy of undetermined significance): paraprotein < 30 g/L, marrow plasma cells < 10%, no end-organ damage; progression to myeloma at about 1% per year. Monitor annually (risk-stratified). Smouldering myeloma: higher burden but no end-organ damage; observation or trials.

Treatment of myeloma: combinations of proteasome inhibitors (bortezomib, carfilzomib), immunomodulatory drugs (lenalidomide), anti-CD38 antibodies (daratumumab, isatuximab) and dexamethasone, with autologous stem cell transplant in fit patients; bispecific antibodies and CAR-T for relapse; bisphosphonates or denosumab; radiotherapy for painful lesions; thromboprophylaxis with immunomodulatory drugs. Hydration and avoidance of nephrotoxins (NSAIDs, contrast when feasible) are urgent in suspected myeloma kidney.

### 4.6 Myeloproliferative neoplasms

PV, essential thrombocythaemia and primary myelofibrosis (with CML in the wider group). JAK2, CALR and MPL mutations define most. Myelofibrosis presents with anaemia, massive splenomegaly (early satiety, left upper quadrant pain), constitutional symptoms, leuco-erythroblastic film with teardrop cells; ruxolitinib/fedratinib/pacritinib/momelotinib for symptoms and spleen; transplantation is the only curative option.

## 5. Haematological and oncological emergencies

### 5.1 Tumour lysis syndrome (TLS)

Rapid cell death releases potassium, phosphate, nucleic acids (uric acid) and causes hypocalcaemia and AKI. Occurs in bulky, fast-growing tumours (Burkitt, high-grade lymphoma, ALL, high-count AML) and sometimes spontaneously, especially after steroid or chemotherapy start. Prevention: vigorous hydration (about 2.5–3 L/m²/day), allopurinol or **rasburicase** (high-risk; contraindicated in G6PD deficiency), monitoring of K, phosphate, calcium, urate and creatinine every 6–8 hours in the early phase. Treat hyperkalaemia and hypocalcaemia only if symptomatic (calcium raises the risk of calcium-phosphate precipitation); early renal replacement if refractory.

### 5.2 Hypercalcaemia of malignancy

Presents with thirst, polyuria, constipation, nausea, abdominal pain, confusion, and short QT. Mechanisms: PTHrP secretion (squamous cell carcinomas, breast, renal), osteolytic metastases, myeloma, lymphoma (calcitriol). Treat: IV isotonic saline (aim for urine output of 100–150 mL/h; careful in heart failure), **zoledronic acid** 4 mg (adjust for renal function) or pamidronate, calcitonin for rapid effect in severe cases, denosumab for refractory disease or severe renal impairment, steroids for lymphoma and myeloma. Corrected calcium > 3.5 mmol/L (14 mg/dL) or symptomatic needs urgent therapy. Hypercalcaemia in a cancer patient is a poor prognostic marker; goals of care matter.

### 5.3 Spinal cord compression (metastatic)

Back pain (often thoracic, worse lying down, night pain, nocturnal awakenings, radicular band-like pain), then limb weakness, sensory level, bladder and bowel dysfunction (late). In anyone with known cancer (breast, lung, prostate, myeloma, kidney) and new back pain, ask about neurological symptoms and examine. **Urgent MRI of the whole spine** (within 24 hours, same day if neurological signs). Start dexamethasone (e.g., 16 mg daily, with gastric protection and glucose monitoring) after discussion with oncology (avoid before biopsy if lymphoma is suspected), keep the patient flat if spinal instability is suspected, and arrange urgent radiotherapy or surgical decompression. The likelihood of regaining walking depends on the pre-treatment neurological status: **an ambulant patient at diagnosis usually stays ambulant**.

### 5.4 Superior vena cava obstruction

Facial and neck swelling, dilated chest wall veins, dyspnoea, headache worse on bending, arm swelling, plethora, stridor (rare). Causes: lung cancer, lymphoma, thymoma, germ-cell tumours, thrombosis around central venous catheters. Get a CT with contrast. Do not biopsy before assessing airway risk. Treatment: stenting for severe symptoms (stridor, cerebral oedema), definitive oncological therapy once histology is obtained (steroids only if lymphoma is not a diagnosis to be preserved), anticoagulation for thrombosis.

### 5.5 Hyperviscosity

Waldenström macroglobulinaemia (IgM), severe polycythaemia or leukostasis (WBC > 100 × 10⁹/L): headache, visual disturbance (retinal haemorrhages, "sausage" veins), dizziness, confusion, mucosal bleeding. Treat with plasmapheresis for IgM hyperviscosity, leukapheresis plus cytoreduction for leukostasis, hydration; avoid transfusion before plasmapheresis in IgM disease because it increases viscosity.

### 5.6 DIC and coagulopathies

DIC: sepsis, malignancy (APL, adenocarcinoma), obstetric complications, trauma, burns, transfusion reaction. Labs: low platelets, prolonged PT/aPTT, low fibrinogen, raised D-dimer, schistocytes. Treat the cause; replace components for bleeding or before procedures (platelets > 50 × 10⁹/L if bleeding, fibrinogen > 1.5 g/L with cryoprecipitate or fibrinogen concentrate, FFP for prolonged clotting times if bleeding); give prophylactic anticoagulation if no bleeding.

### 5.7 Malignancy-associated thrombosis

Cancer raises the risk of venous thromboembolism four- to sevenfold (pancreas, brain, lung, GI, haematological). Treatment: DOACs (apixaban, rivaroxaban, edoxaban) or LMWH for at least 3–6 months and while cancer is active; monitor bleeding risk, particularly with GI and genitourinary tumours.

### 5.8 Immune-related adverse events (checkpoint inhibitors)

Immune checkpoint inhibitors can cause colitis, pneumonitis, hepatitis, thyroiditis, hypophysitis, adrenal insufficiency, nephritis, rash, myocarditis, neurological syndromes and diabetes, weeks to months after therapy and occasionally after it has finished. In any patient who has received them, treat new diarrhoea, dyspnoea, rash, fatigue, headache, or visual change as possible immune toxicity: grade it, hold the drug and escalate to steroids with oncology input.

## 6. Warning signs of cancer and the diagnostic pathway

### 6.1 Why this matters

The aim is earlier diagnosis of cancers where earlier treatment improves outcome, without exposing the large number of people with benign symptoms to unnecessary tests. In primary care, a GP may see a case of a specific cancer only once every few years, while the same symptoms are common and mostly benign. Positive predictive values (PPV) for single symptoms are low (often 1–3%); combinations raise them. A pragmatic threshold in many national guidance documents (e.g., NICE NG12) for urgent investigation is a PPV of about 3% or higher.

### 6.2 Warning signs and symptoms

| Feature | Think of |
|---|---|
| Unexplained weight loss | GI, lung, pancreatic, ovarian, haematological cancers; also hyperthyroidism, diabetes, depression, infection (see chapter 30) |
| Persistent change in bowel habit (> 6 weeks, age > 50) | Colorectal cancer |
| Rectal bleeding with change in bowel habit | Colorectal cancer; rectal bleeding alone in younger patients is usually haemorrhoids, but not always |
| Iron deficiency anaemia (men, postmenopausal women) | GI cancer (colorectal, gastric), coeliac disease, other |
| Dysphagia (progressive, solids then liquids), odynophagia | Oesophageal or gastric cancer: urgent endoscopy |
| New-onset dyspepsia > 55 with alarm features | Gastric/oesophageal cancer |
| Persistent hoarseness (> 3 weeks) in smokers or > 45 | Laryngeal cancer, lung cancer (recurrent laryngeal nerve) |
| Cough > 3 weeks, haemoptysis, chest signs, finger clubbing | Lung cancer: chest X-ray (a normal film does not exclude it; CT if suspicion persists) |
| Painless visible haematuria | Bladder or kidney cancer |
| Post-menopausal bleeding | Endometrial cancer (urgent transvaginal ultrasound, endometrial sampling) |
| Persistent bloating, early satiety, pelvic or abdominal pain in women > 50 | Ovarian cancer (CA-125, pelvic ultrasound) |
| Breast lump, nipple retraction, skin changes, new nipple discharge | Breast cancer: triple assessment (clinical, imaging, biopsy) |
| Lump in the neck, supraclavicular node, hard fixed node | Lymphoma, head and neck, thyroid, lung, GI cancers |
| Testicular swelling or hardness | Testicular cancer |
| Change in a mole (ABCDE: asymmetry, border, colour, diameter, evolution) | Melanoma; see chapter 54 |
| Non-healing ulcer or lesion (> 4 weeks) in the mouth or skin | Oral cancer, skin cancer |
| Persistent unexplained pain at night, new back pain in cancer patient or age > 50 with systemic features | Metastases, myeloma |
| New-onset diabetes with weight loss, age > 50 | Pancreatic cancer |
| Painless jaundice | Pancreatic, biliary or hepatic cancer: urgent imaging |
| Thrombosis without obvious cause (and age-appropriate cancer screening only) | Occult cancer: history, examination, FBC, LFTs, chest X-ray and age-appropriate screening are enough; extensive CT screening is not routinely beneficial |
| Fever of unknown origin, night sweats | Lymphoma, renal cell carcinoma, leukaemia |

### 6.3 Paraneoplastic syndromes

Clues that a cancer may be causing symptoms at a distance: SIADH (small cell lung cancer), ectopic ACTH (Cushing), hypercalcaemia (PTHrP), Lambert–Eaton myasthenic syndrome (small cell lung cancer), cerebellar degeneration (anti-Yo, ovary/breast; anti-Hu), limbic encephalitis, dermatomyositis (ovarian, lung, GI cancers), acanthosis nigricans (gastric), Trousseau syndrome (migratory thrombophlebitis), hypertrophic osteoarthropathy (lung cancer), polycythaemia (renal, hepatic cancers), membranous nephropathy, and opsoclonus–myoclonus (neuroblastoma in children, breast and lung cancer in adults).

### 6.4 The diagnostic pathway

1. **Suspicion**: history, examination, and basic tests (FBC, ferritin, calcium, LFTs, creatinine, CRP/ESR, chest X-ray, faecal immunochemical test (FIT) for suspected colorectal cancer).
2. **Imaging**: choose the test that answers the question (ultrasound, CT, MRI, PET-CT). Imaging cannot replace histology.
3. **Tissue diagnosis**: obtain enough tissue for histology, immunohistochemistry and molecular testing. Core needle biopsy usually beats fine-needle aspiration for lymph nodes and many masses; excision biopsy for suspected lymphoma. Biopsy the **site that gives the most information with the least risk** (an accessible metastasis may be easier and equally informative to the primary). Discuss with the radiologist and pathologist before biopsy; place biopsy tracks so they can be resected.
4. **Staging**: TNM system (tumour, nodes, metastases), site-specific imaging, tumour markers where validated.
5. **Multidisciplinary team (MDT) discussion**: oncology, surgery, radiotherapy, radiology, pathology, specialist nursing, palliative care.
6. **Molecular profiling**: EGFR, ALK, ROS1, KRAS, BRAF, PD-L1 in lung cancer; HER2, hormone receptors in breast cancer; MSI/MMR in colorectal and endometrial cancer; BRCA in ovarian, breast, prostate and pancreatic cancer.
7. **Patient-centred conversation**: prognosis, goals, fertility, work, family, and decisions about treatment intent (curative, life-prolonging, symptom control).

### 6.5 Tumour markers

Tumour markers are rarely suitable for screening or diagnosis in unselected people. They are useful for monitoring (CEA in colorectal cancer, CA-125 in ovarian cancer, AFP and β-hCG in germ cell tumours, PSA in prostate cancer, CA 19-9 in pancreatic cancer, calcitonin in medullary thyroid cancer, thyroglobulin in differentiated thyroid cancer, paraprotein in myeloma). CA 19-9 and CA-125 are raised in many benign conditions (biliary obstruction, endometriosis, ascites). **A normal marker never excludes cancer, and a raised marker never proves it.**

### 6.6 Cancer of unknown primary

Metastatic disease with no primary identified despite thorough work-up. Do the history and examination (including breast, thyroid, skin, testes, prostate, rectum, pelvis), imaging (CT chest/abdomen/pelvis, PET-CT selectively), histopathology with immunohistochemistry panels, and tumour markers (PSA in men, AFP/β-hCG in midline disease, young men). Identify treatable subsets (e.g., germ cell tumour in a young man with midline disease; adenocarcinoma with axillary node involvement in a woman = occult breast cancer; squamous cell carcinoma in cervical nodes = head and neck primary; peritoneal carcinomatosis in a woman = ovarian type). Most patients have a poor prognosis, and early involvement of palliative care is appropriate.

## 7. Cancer screening: a short guide

Screening finds disease in people without symptoms. It must be considered with its harms: over-diagnosis, false positives, anxiety, complications of investigation. Recommendations vary by country, and only a few programmes have clear mortality benefit.

- **Cervical cancer**: HPV-based screening (HPV primary testing every 5 years in many programmes, ages ~25/30–65), plus HPV vaccination. Cytology triage.
- **Breast cancer**: mammography every 1–3 years in women from about 40–50 to 69–74, depending on programme. Counsel on over-diagnosis.
- **Colorectal cancer**: FIT every 1–2 years or colonoscopy every 10 years from age 45–50 to 74.
- **Lung cancer**: annual low-dose CT in heavy smokers or ex-smokers (e.g., ≥ 20–30 pack-years; age 50/55–74/80), in programmes with smoking cessation.
- **Prostate cancer**: informed shared decision-making on PSA (chapter 49).
- **Not recommended** for population screening: ovarian cancer (CA-125 plus ultrasound), pancreatic cancer (except high-risk families), whole-body scans, broad "multi-cancer" blood tests (evidence of benefit not yet established).
- **High-risk groups**: *BRCA1/2* and Lynch syndrome carriers, familial adenomatous polyposis, Li–Fraumeni, strong family histories: genetic counselling and intensified surveillance.

## Worked cases

### Case 1: Microcytic anaemia in a 62-year-old man

**Presentation.** Mr G., 62, has felt tired for four months and breathless on hills. No GI symptoms he can name; he eats a mixed diet. He takes ibuprofen for backache. Pale conjunctivae, pulse 88, BP 136/84. Abdomen soft, no mass. Rectal examination: brown stool, negative for blood on the glove.

**Data.** Hb 92 g/L, MCV 71 fL, RDW 18%, platelets 480 × 10⁹/L, WBC normal. Ferritin 7 µg/L, TSAT 8%. CRP 4 mg/L. Creatinine normal. Coeliac serology (tTG-IgA) negative.

**Reasoning.** Iron deficiency anaemia (low ferritin, low TSAT, low MCV, reactive thrombocytosis). In a man this age, the source is gastrointestinal until proven otherwise. NSAID use is a risk factor, but it is not an explanation until the colon and stomach have been examined.

**Action.** Urgent colonoscopy and gastroscopy: a 3-cm caecal adenocarcinoma is found. CT staging shows no metastases. He is discussed at the MDT and has a right hemicolectomy. Hb corrected with IV iron before surgery. Stop ibuprofen. Lynch syndrome screening on the tumour (MMR immunohistochemistry).

**Lessons.** Iron deficiency in a man is a symptom, not a diagnosis. A negative rectal examination or negative faecal occult blood test on a single sample does not exclude a colonic source.

### Case 2: Macrocytosis and tingling feet

**Presentation.** Mrs F., 71, has fatigue, sore tongue, and tingling in both feet for six months. She is vegetarian, takes omeprazole, and takes metformin for type 2 diabetes. Examination: mild pallor, smooth red tongue, reduced vibration sense to the ankles, brisk knee reflexes and absent ankle jerks, Romberg positive.

**Data.** Hb 94 g/L, MCV 118 fL, platelets 128, WBC 3.6 with hypersegmented neutrophils on film. B12 62 pmol/L (very low), folate normal. LDH very high, bilirubin mildly raised (ineffective erythropoiesis). Anti-intrinsic factor antibodies positive.

**Reasoning.** Pernicious anaemia on top of metformin and PPI use, and a vegetarian diet. Neurological involvement (posterior column signs) means a more intensive replacement regimen.

**Action.** Hydroxocobalamin 1 mg IM on alternate days until no further improvement, then every 2 months for life; monitor potassium during recovery; no folic acid before B12 is replaced. Review again in a month: Hb should rise within a week and reticulocytes surge at 5–7 days. Counsel that neurological recovery may be partial and takes months. Consider screening for gastric cancer risk by symptom review (pernicious anaemia carries an increased risk of gastric carcinoma and carcinoid) and for other autoimmune disease (thyroid).

**Lessons.** Neurological B12 deficiency can be irreversible if treatment is delayed. Never give folic acid alone to a macrocytic patient before assessing B12.

### Case 3: Bruising, fever and a blood count that does not make sense

**Presentation.** Ms W., 34, has a week of fever, gum bleeding, easy bruising and extreme fatigue. She has petechiae on her legs and oral mucosa, a temperature of 38.4 °C and heart rate of 118. No lymphadenopathy, spleen not palpable.

**Data.** Hb 71 g/L, WBC 2.1 × 10⁹/L, neutrophils 0.3, platelets 14 × 10⁹/L. Film: abnormal promyelocytes with heavy granulation and Auer rods. PT and aPTT prolonged, fibrinogen 0.9 g/L, D-dimer very high.

**Reasoning.** Pancytopenia with abnormal promyelocytes and DIC: acute promyelocytic leukaemia until proven otherwise. This is a time-critical emergency, because of the risk of fatal intracranial haemorrhage.

**Action.** Call the haematology on-call immediately and transfer to a specialist centre. Start **ATRA** before genetic confirmation. Give platelets to keep > 30–50 × 10⁹/L, cryoprecipitate or fibrinogen concentrate to keep fibrinogen > 1.5 g/L, and FFP as needed. Blood cultures and broad-spectrum antibiotics for febrile neutropenia. Avoid central line insertion and lumbar puncture until coagulopathy is corrected. PML::RARA by PCR/FISH confirms. Treatment with ATRA and arsenic trioxide gives cure rates above 90%.

**Lessons.** The film is the fastest diagnostic test in haematology. APL must be recognised at the bedside, before the molecular result.

### Case 4: The painless node

**Presentation.** Tom, 24, has a painless right supraclavicular lump for six weeks, drenching night sweats, and itching after hot showers. Weight loss 4 kg. Examination: 3-cm firm, rubbery right supraclavicular node, smaller cervical nodes, no hepatosplenomegaly.

**Data.** Hb 118 g/L, ESR 62, LDH mildly raised, albumin 34. Chest X-ray: widened mediastinum. CT: bulky mediastinal mass and nodes above and below the diaphragm on the right and left. Excisional biopsy: nodular sclerosis classical Hodgkin lymphoma. PET-CT: stage IIIB (B symptoms).

**Reasoning.** Classic lymphoma presentation in a young adult. A supraclavicular node in a young man is lymphoma or germ cell tumour until proven otherwise, and fine-needle aspiration alone is inadequate. Steroids and antibiotics were avoided before biopsy.

**Action.** Fertility counselling and sperm banking before chemotherapy; baseline echocardiogram and lung function (anthracycline, bleomycin); ABVD or modern brentuximab-containing regimen with PET-adapted therapy; vaccinations; long-term survivorship plan (cardiac, thyroid, secondary cancer screening). Cure expected in about 80–90%.

**Lessons.** Supraclavicular nodes are never normal. Excisional biopsy is the standard. Cure is achievable, and treatment toxicity and late effects matter as much as survival.

### Case 5: Back pain and a high ESR

**Presentation.** Mr D., 69, has had low back pain for two months, worse at night, with fatigue and a 3-kg weight loss. He had two chest infections in the past year. No leg weakness. Mild pallor, tenderness over L2.

**Data.** Hb 101 g/L (normocytic), creatinine 168 µmol/L (previously 95), corrected calcium 2.84 mmol/L, ESR 118 mm/h, CRP 6 mg/L, albumin 31 g/L, total protein 98 g/L (raised globulin gap). Film: rouleaux. Serum electrophoresis: IgG kappa paraprotein 42 g/L; free light chain ratio markedly abnormal. MRI spine: multiple lytic lesions and L2 compression fracture, no cord compression.

**Reasoning.** Multiple myeloma with CRAB features. The high ESR with normal CRP and a "protein gap" is an important clue. The kidney injury is probably light chain cast nephropathy aggravated by hypercalcaemia and, he admits, NSAID use for his pain.

**Action.** Stop NSAIDs. Hydrate, treat hypercalcaemia (IV fluids, bisphosphonate dose-adjusted for renal function or denosumab). Haematology referral urgently for bone marrow biopsy, cytogenetics, and early treatment with a bortezomib-based regimen plus dexamethasone, because rapid light chain reduction can reverse renal failure. Analgesia, spinal precautions and consideration of radiotherapy for painful lesions; thromboprophylaxis if started on immunomodulatory drugs. Warn about spinal cord compression symptoms (new leg weakness, numbness, bladder or bowel changes) and give clear instructions to seek emergency care.

**Lessons.** In older adults with back pain, anaemia and renal impairment, think myeloma. Dipstick protein can be negative because light chains are not detected by dipstick.

### Case 6: Fever on day 10 of chemotherapy

**Presentation.** Mrs C., 56, receiving adjuvant chemotherapy for breast cancer, reports feeling hot and shivery for two hours. Temperature 38.6 °C, pulse 112, BP 108/64, respiratory rate 22. Peripheral line site clean, no focus found. Neutrophils 0.2 × 10⁹/L.

**Reasoning.** Febrile neutropenia. Her presentation is likely to underestimate severity, because neutropenic patients cannot mount a localised inflammatory response; absence of pus does not mean absence of infection.

**Action.** Immediately: sepsis screen, blood cultures from peripheral and central sites, lactate, FBC, U&E, LFTs, urine, chest X-ray, and **IV piperacillin–tazobactam within 60 minutes**; IV fluids; oxygen if hypoxic; consider adding an aminoglycoside or carbapenem if unstable. MASCC score and clinical stability guide decisions; she is admitted. Review at 48 hours with cultures; continue until afebrile and neutrophil recovery or per protocol. G-CSF is considered in subsequent cycles.

**Lessons.** Time to antibiotics determines survival in febrile neutropenia. Never wait for the neutrophil count in a chemotherapy patient who is unwell.

### Case 7: Unexplained leg thrombosis and a platelet count that keeps rising

**Presentation.** Mr B., 58, has a swollen left calf. Duplex confirms proximal DVT with no provoking factor. He has no personal cancer history. History: weight stable, mild itching after hot baths, occasional headaches and burning, red hands.

**Data.** Hb 186 g/L, haematocrit 0.55, WBC 13.4, platelets 640 × 10⁹/L. Oxygen saturation 97%, non-smoker, no sleep apnoea symptoms. Palpable spleen tip. Erythropoietin low.

**Reasoning.** Erythrocytosis with leukocytosis, thrombocytosis, splenomegaly, aquagenic pruritus and erythromelalgia: polycythaemia vera. *JAK2* V617F is positive.

**Action.** Anticoagulate (DOAC) for the DVT; venesection to haematocrit < 0.45; low-dose aspirin once safe with anticoagulation (balance bleeding risk); consider cytoreduction (hydroxycarbamide or ruxolitinib) as he has had a thrombosis, i.e., high-risk disease. Control cardiovascular risk factors. Review annually for transformation to myelofibrosis or acute leukaemia.

**Lessons.** An unprovoked thrombosis calls for a look at the blood count. Simple venesection and aspirin reduce thrombotic risk dramatically.

## Common pitfalls

1. **Accepting a cause for iron deficiency in men and postmenopausal women without GI investigation.**
2. **Treating a low MCV with iron without checking ferritin**, missing thalassaemia trait (iron is not helpful, and iron overload is a risk in some).
3. **Giving folic acid alone** in macrocytic anaemia before excluding B12 deficiency.
4. **Missing neurological B12 deficiency** because the haemoglobin is normal.
5. **Ignoring an abnormal film.** Blasts, schistocytes or parasites change management within minutes.
6. **Transfusing a stable, compensated patient** to a number, or transfusing in suspected TTP, HIT or immune haemolysis without specialist input.
7. **Missing APL** or delaying ATRA while waiting for genetics.
8. **Delaying antibiotics in febrile neutropenia** while waiting for counts or localising signs.
9. **Giving steroids before a lymph node biopsy**, obscuring lymphoma.
10. **Relying on fine-needle aspiration for lymphoma**, or on a normal chest X-ray to exclude mediastinal disease.
11. **Missing myeloma** because dipstick protein is negative and the CRP is normal; check ESR/viscosity, protein electrophoresis and free light chains.
12. **Attributing thrombocytopenia to "ITP" without checking the film, drug history, HIV, hepatitis C and liver disease.** Missing TTP and HIT are the two critical omissions.
13. **Over-using tumour markers** for screening, and under-using FIT, mammography and cervical screening, which are the tests that have proven benefit.
14. **Extensive "occult cancer" CT scanning** after unprovoked thrombosis: history, examination, basic tests and age-appropriate screening suffice.
15. **Forgetting fertility preservation, vaccination and survivorship** in young cancer patients.

## Key points

- Read the blood count as a pattern: three lines, the trend, and the film. Classify anaemia by MCV and reticulocytes, then find the cause.
- Iron deficiency in men and postmenopausal women is gastrointestinal cancer until excluded.
- Do not give folic acid alone before B12 is checked; treat B12 deficiency with neurological signs urgently and fully.
- Haemolysis: jaundice, high reticulocytes, high LDH, low haptoglobin, film changes, DAT.
- Acute leukaemia, APL, TTP, HIT, febrile neutropenia, tumour lysis, spinal cord compression and hypercalcaemia of malignancy are emergencies that need action within hours.
- Painless lymphadenopathy needs excisional biopsy if persistent; supraclavicular nodes are abnormal; avoid steroids beforehand.
- Myeloma: think of it in older adults with bone pain, anaemia, renal impairment, hypercalcaemia or infections; order protein electrophoresis and free light chains.
- Cancer red flags have low individual predictive value but become powerful in combination; use urgent pathways and safety-net the rest.
- Tumour markers monitor; they do not diagnose or screen.
- Screening saves lives only for a few cancers; counsel on benefits and harms.

## Self-assessment

**Questions**

1. A 45-year-old premenopausal woman has Hb 98 g/L, MCV 68 fL, ferritin 6 µg/L. She reports heavy periods. Is investigation of the gastrointestinal tract mandatory?
2. A woman of Southeast Asian ancestry has MCV 62 fL, Hb 118 g/L, red cell count 6.1 × 10¹²/L, ferritin 95 µg/L. What do you suspect and how do you test?
3. Which two tests are mandatory when macrocytic anaemia is first found, before folic acid is given?
4. Name four features that distinguish haemolytic anaemia from other normocytic anaemias.
5. A patient develops thrombocytopenia 7 days after starting heparin and a new DVT. What is the first action?
6. List the CRAB features and two additional markers that define active myeloma.
7. A 30-year-old on chemotherapy has a temperature of 38.5 °C and neutrophils 0.3. What should happen in the first hour?
8. What are three clinical clues that a persistent lymph node needs excisional biopsy rather than watchful waiting?
9. A man with known prostate cancer reports new thoracic back pain at night and difficulty climbing stairs. What is the next step and what time frame?
10. A 66-year-old man with a first unprovoked pulmonary embolism asks whether he needs a full-body CT to look for cancer. What is your advice?

**Answers**

1. Not mandatory in a premenopausal woman with an obvious cause and no GI symptoms, but menstrual loss should be treated (e.g., tranexamic acid, levonorgestrel IUS) and iron replaced. Investigate the GI tract (coeliac serology, endoscopy) if there are GI symptoms, if the anaemia is out of proportion to blood loss, if there is a family history of GI cancer, if age is ≥ 50–60, if there is no response to iron, or if there is any red flag.
2. Thalassaemia trait (α or β): low MCV disproportionate to mild anaemia, high red cell count, normal ferritin (Mentzer index < 13). Test with haemoglobin electrophoresis/HPLC (HbA2 raised in β-trait) and DNA studies for α-thalassaemia; offer genetic counselling and partner testing when planning pregnancy. Avoid iron unless iron deficiency is proven.
3. Vitamin B12 level (with holotranscobalamin or methylmalonic acid if borderline) and folate; also TSH, liver function, and review of alcohol and drugs; blood film for hypersegmented neutrophils.
4. Raised reticulocytes, raised LDH, raised unconjugated bilirubin, low haptoglobin; also abnormal film (spherocytes, schistocytes, bite cells), positive DAT (immune), and splenomegaly or dark urine.
5. Suspect HIT: calculate the 4Ts score, stop all heparin including flushes, start a non-heparin anticoagulant (e.g., argatroban, fondaparinux), send anti-PF4 antibody, and avoid platelet transfusions unless there is life-threatening bleeding. Do not start warfarin until platelets recover.
6. Hypercalcaemia, Renal impairment, Anaemia, Bone lesions. Additional myeloma-defining events: ≥ 60% clonal plasma cells in marrow, involved:uninvolved free light chain ratio ≥ 100 (with involved level ≥ 100 mg/L), more than one focal MRI lesion.
7. Treat as febrile neutropenia: sepsis screen with blood cultures (peripheral and central), lactate, FBC, U&E, urine, chest X-ray, and **broad-spectrum IV antipseudomonal antibiotics within 60 minutes** (e.g., piperacillin–tazobactam), IV fluids, oxygen if needed, and senior/oncology review.
8. Any of: persistence beyond 4–6 weeks, size > 2 cm, firm/hard/fixed or matted nodes, supraclavicular location, progressive enlargement, generalised lymphadenopathy, B symptoms, splenomegaly, or no reactive cause.
9. Suspect metastatic spinal cord compression: an emergency. Urgent MRI of the whole spine within 24 hours (immediately if neurological deficit), neurological examination, discuss with oncology, dexamethasone, keep flat if spinal instability suspected, and arrange urgent radiotherapy/surgical review.
10. No. Evidence shows extensive screening CT does not improve outcome. Take a careful history, examine, perform basic blood tests (FBC, calcium, LFTs, urinalysis), chest X-ray, and age-appropriate screening (colorectal, breast, cervical, prostate discussion). Investigate further if symptoms or abnormal results point to a specific cancer.
