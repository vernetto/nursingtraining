# Chapter 61. Older Adults

## Learning objectives

After studying this chapter you should be able to:

1. Explain why diagnosis is harder in older people: atypical presentations, multimorbidity, sensory and cognitive impairment, and changed physiology.
2. Perform a comprehensive geriatric assessment (CGA) and use validated tools to assess frailty, function, cognition, mood, nutrition, vision, hearing and continence.
3. Recognise the common atypical presentations of infection, myocardial infarction, acute abdomen, heart failure, hyperthyroidism and depression in later life.
4. Evaluate falls and syncope systematically, and plan prevention.
5. Differentiate delirium, dementia and depression, and make a diagnosis of dementia subtype.
6. Review medication safely: polypharmacy, deprescribing, the Beers and STOPP/START criteria, and prescribing cascades.
7. Assess common geriatric syndromes: incontinence, pressure injuries, malnutrition, dysphagia, sleep problems, pain, and elder abuse.
8. Apply principles of capacity, advance care planning, shared decision-making and appropriate (not excessive) investigation and treatment.

> **Note on thresholds and doses.** Reference values, drug thresholds and screening cut-offs vary by guideline and population. Use your national geriatric formulary, local protocols and clinical judgement. In older adults, the aim is not only longevity but function, independence and quality of life, as defined by the patient.

## 1. Why older adults need a different approach

### 1.1 Physiology of ageing

| System | Changes | Diagnostic and therapeutic consequences |
|---|---|---|
| Cardiovascular | Stiffer arteries (systolic hypertension), reduced beta-adrenergic responsiveness (maximum heart rate falls), diastolic dysfunction, conduction disease | Tachycardia may be absent in sepsis or hypovolaemia; beta-blockers mask signs; orthostatic hypotension; atrial fibrillation; heart failure with preserved ejection fraction |
| Respiratory | Reduced lung elasticity, chest wall compliance, cough reflex, and ventilatory response to hypoxia and hypercapnia | Pneumonia may present without cough or fever; hypoxia is less symptomatic; aspiration risk |
| Renal | GFR falls ≈ 0.8–1 mL/min/year from 30–40 years; reduced concentrating ability and thirst | Creatinine overestimates GFR in the frail (low muscle mass); drug accumulation; dehydration and hyponatraemia easily develop |
| Hepatic | Reduced first-pass metabolism and blood flow; reduced CYP activity | Longer half-lives, more adverse effects |
| Body composition | Lean mass ↓ (sarcopenia), fat ↑, total body water ↓ | Lipophilic drugs (benzodiazepines) accumulate; water-soluble drugs reach higher concentrations |
| Immune | Immunosenescence, reduced febrile response | Infections are severe and atypical; blunted fever (up to 30–50% of bacteraemic older adults have no fever) |
| Nervous | Slower processing, reduced reserve, sleep fragmentation, sensory loss | Delirium vulnerability, falls |
| Thermoregulation | Reduced shivering and vasomotor responses | Hypothermia and heat illness |
| Skin | Thinner, less elastic | Pressure injuries, skin tears, delayed healing |
| Endocrine | Reduced insulin sensitivity, thyroid autoimmunity, osteoporosis, testosterone/oestrogen decline | Diabetes, subclinical hypothyroidism, fractures |

**Homeostenosis**: older people have narrowed physiological reserve. A small insult (infection, new drug, dehydration) can produce a large functional decline, often in an unexpected organ system (**the "weakest link" phenomenon**: pneumonia presenting as delirium, UTI presenting as falls, heart failure presenting as confusion).

### 1.2 Atypical presentation of disease: the "geriatric giants"

The classic "I" syndromes: **I**mmobility, **I**nstability (falls), **I**ntellectual impairment (delirium, dementia), **I**ncontinence, **I**atrogenesis (adverse drug events), plus **I**nfection, **I**nanition (malnutrition), **I**mpaired vision/hearing, and **I**solation.

**Non-specific presentations** that deserve a systematic search for a treatable cause: confusion, falls, "off legs", failure to thrive, incontinence, dizziness, fatigue, loss of appetite, functional decline, social withdrawal.

| Disease | Typical (young) | Atypical (older adult) |
|---|---|---|
| **Myocardial infarction** | Crushing chest pain | Painless in up to 30–40%: dyspnoea, fatigue, confusion, syncope, nausea, falls, new heart failure |
| **Pneumonia** | Fever, productive cough, pleuritic pain | Confusion, falls, reduced appetite, tachypnoea (often earliest sign), low-grade or no fever |
| **Urinary tract infection** | Dysuria, frequency | Delirium, falls, or asymptomatic bacteriuria (don't treat without localising symptoms or sepsis) |
| **Sepsis** | Fever, rigors, tachycardia | Hypothermia, confusion, hypotension, tachypnoea, oliguria, functional decline; fever absent in up to 1/3 |
| **Acute abdomen** | Guarding, rigidity, fever, leucocytosis | Minimal pain, little guarding, afebrile, normal WBC; perforation and ischaemia more common; delayed diagnosis and high mortality |
| **Hyperthyroidism (apathetic)** | Anxiety, tremor, weight loss, heat intolerance | Weight loss, atrial fibrillation, heart failure, depression, apathy |
| **Hypothyroidism** | Cold, weight gain | Confusion, falls, depression, heart failure, constipation |
| **Depression** | Low mood | Somatic complaints, irritability, cognitive complaints, anhedonia, agitation, alcohol, weight loss |
| **Heart failure** | Dyspnoea, orthopnoea | Fatigue, confusion, anorexia, nocturia, cough, falls |
| **Giant cell arteritis, polymyalgia rheumatica** | Headache, shoulder girdle pain | Fever of unknown origin, malaise, visual symptoms |
| **Hypoglycaemia** | Sweating, tremor | Confusion, falls, behavioural change, focal deficits (may mimic stroke) |
| **Hyponatraemia** | Headache, nausea | Falls, gait instability, confusion, seizures |
| **Pulmonary embolism** | Pleuritic pain, dyspnoea | Syncope, dyspnoea, confusion, tachycardia |
| **Aortic stenosis** | Chest pain, syncope, dyspnoea | Exertional dyspnoea, falls, fatigue |
| **Hip fracture** | Pain after trauma | Pain may be mild or referred to knee; impacted fractures can allow walking |

### 1.3 Challenges in history-taking

- Allow extra time; speak clearly and face the patient; check hearing aids and glasses are in place; reduce background noise; use plain words and short questions; avoid assuming impairment; address the patient directly even when a carer is present.
- Seek **collateral history** from family, carers, general practitioner, pharmacy list, nursing-home chart; ask "what has changed?" (baseline function, cognition, mobility, continence, appetite, sleep).
- The **medication history** is essential (including over-the-counter, herbal, eye drops, patches, inhalers, supplements, recent changes) and compare with pharmacy records.
- Ask about **function**: Activities of Daily Living (ADLs: bathing, dressing, toileting, transferring, continence, feeding) and Instrumental ADLs (IADLs: shopping, cooking, housework, finances, medications, transport, telephone).
- Ask about **falls, continence, memory, mood, alcohol, driving, social support, home environment, carers, advance directives, goals**.
- Patients under-report symptoms; "it's just my age" is not an answer to be accepted. Equally, do not attribute every symptom to ageing.

## 2. Comprehensive Geriatric Assessment (CGA)

CGA is a multidimensional, multidisciplinary diagnostic and therapeutic process that identifies medical, psychological and functional problems and develops a coordinated plan. It improves survival, independence at home and reduces institutionalisation in hospitalised older adults, and informs decisions about surgery and cancer treatment.

### 2.1 Domains and tools

| Domain | Tools and questions |
|---|---|
| **Function (ADL/IADL)** | Barthel Index, Katz ADL, Lawton IADL; "Can you manage on your own?" |
| **Mobility and falls** | Timed Up and Go (TUG: > 12–14 s predicts falls), gait speed (< 0.8 m/s abnormal; < 0.6 m/s high risk), 30-second chair stand, 4-stage balance test, Short Physical Performance Battery; history of falls |
| **Frailty** | Clinical Frailty Scale (CFS, 1–9; ≥ 5 frail), Fried phenotype (weight loss, exhaustion, weakness (grip), slow gait, low activity), Frailty Index, FRAIL scale, PRISMA-7 |
| **Cognition** | MMSE (< 24/30), MoCA (< 26/30), Mini-Cog (3-word recall + clock draw), 4AT or CAM for delirium, AMTS (≤ 7/10 abnormal), GPCOG |
| **Mood** | Geriatric Depression Scale (GDS-15, ≥ 5 positive), PHQ-2/9, Cornell Scale in dementia |
| **Nutrition** | Mini Nutritional Assessment (MNA-SF), MUST, weight trend, BMI, albumin is a poor marker; ask about dentition and swallowing |
| **Vision and hearing** | Snellen/near card, whisper test, finger rub, audiometry; refraction and cataract review |
| **Continence** | Frequency, urgency, nocturia, leakage; bladder diary; post-void residual; bowels |
| **Medications** | Full reconciliation, Beers, STOPP/START, Drug Burden Index, anticholinergic scale |
| **Social and environment** | Carer burden, home hazards, finances, transport, social isolation (UCLA Loneliness scale), abuse screening |
| **Goals and preferences** | What matters most? Advance care planning, resuscitation preferences, health care proxy |
| **Pain** | Numeric scale; PAINAD in dementia |
| **Skin and pressure injury risk** | Braden or Waterlow score |
| **Sleep** | Insomnia, apnoea, restless legs |
| **Oral health and dentition** | Teeth, dentures, mouth lesions |

### 2.2 Frailty

Frailty is a state of reduced physiological reserve and increased vulnerability to stressors, distinct from disability and comorbidity but overlapping. Present in ≈ 10–15% of community-dwelling people over 65 and > 25% over 85. Identify using the CFS at each hospital admission or clinic visit: scores 1–3 (fit to managing well), 4 (vulnerable), 5–6 (mildly to moderately frail), 7–9 (severely frail to terminally ill). Frailty predicts outcomes (falls, delirium, disability, hospitalisation, mortality, complications after surgery or chemotherapy) better than age. Management: exercise (resistance training), protein (1.0–1.2 g/kg/day) and calories, medication review, treatment of reversible conditions, vitamin D, addressing social isolation, advance care planning.

**Sarcopenia**: loss of muscle mass and strength (grip strength < 27 kg men, < 16 kg women; EWGSOP2); assess with SARC-F, grip, chair stand, gait speed, DXA/BIA.

## 3. Falls and syncope

### 3.1 Epidemiology

About 30% of people over 65 and 50% over 80 fall each year; falls cause injury in 10–20%, fractures in 5%, and are a leading cause of injury death and loss of independence. A fall is a symptom, not a diagnosis.

### 3.2 Evaluation of the patient who has fallen

1. **History of the event**: circumstances (activity, location, time, footwear, lighting), prodrome (dizziness, palpitations, chest pain, visual blurring, vertigo), loss of consciousness (witness account), ability to get up, time on the floor, injuries, previous falls, fear of falling.
2. **Differentiate**: mechanical (trip/slip) vs medical (syncope, arrhythmia, seizure, hypotension, hypoglycaemia, stroke/TIA) vs drop attack vs dizziness vs multifactorial.
3. **Risk factors (intrinsic)**: age, previous falls, gait/balance disorder, muscle weakness, cognitive impairment, visual impairment, neuropathy, arthritis, foot problems, postural hypotension, incontinence/nocturia, depression, alcohol, **medications** (psychotropics, sedatives, antihypertensives, diuretics, anticholinergics, opioids, polypharmacy), Parkinson disease, stroke. **Extrinsic**: loose rugs, poor lighting, stairs, wet floors, lack of grab rails, footwear, walking aids.
4. **Examination**: vital signs; **lying and standing BP** (after 5 min supine, then at 1 and 3 minutes standing; drop ≥ 20 systolic or ≥ 10 diastolic mmHg, or <90 systolic = orthostatic hypotension); heart rhythm and murmurs (aortic stenosis); carotid sinus massage (if indicated, in patients with unexplained syncope and no carotid bruit, recent stroke/TIA, or arrhythmia history; do it with ECG monitoring); neurological examination (gait, balance, Romberg, proximal strength, neuropathy, Parkinsonism, cerebellar signs, cognition, vision); feet and footwear; joints; head and neck for injury; signs of dehydration or infection; **hip examination and examination for occult fracture**.
5. **Investigations** guided by findings: ECG (conduction defects, AF, long QT), glucose, FBC, U&E, calcium, vitamin D, B12, TSH, CRP, urinalysis only if symptoms; echocardiogram for murmur/heart failure; 24-hour or longer ambulatory ECG monitoring/loop recorder for suspected arrhythmic syncope; CT head if head strike on anticoagulants or neurological signs; imaging for fractures; tilt testing in selected cases.

### 3.3 Syncope in older adults

Common causes: **orthostatic hypotension** (drugs, dehydration, autonomic failure: diabetes, Parkinson disease, Lewy body dementia, multiple system atrophy; post-prandial hypotension), **cardiac** (bradyarrhythmia, sick sinus, high-grade AV block, tachyarrhythmia including ventricular tachycardia, aortic stenosis, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, MI, PE), **neurally mediated** (vasovagal, carotid sinus hypersensitivity, situational: micturition, defaecation, cough), seizures, hypoglycaemia. Cardiac syncope has high mortality: red flags include syncope without prodrome, during exertion or supine, abnormal ECG, structural heart disease, family history of sudden death, palpitations preceding syncope. See chapter 28.

### 3.4 Prevention and management

- **Multifactorial intervention** reduces falls by about 20–30%: exercise programmes (balance and strength training such as Otago, Tai Chi, ≥ 3 h/week over 6 months), medication review (reduce psychotropics and antihypertensives where possible, stop unnecessary drugs), vision correction (cataract surgery, single-vision glasses for walking), treatment of orthostatic hypotension, treatment of osteoporosis, vitamin D supplementation only if deficient, footwear and foot care, home hazard modification (occupational therapy), appropriate walking aids, continence management, cognitive and nutritional support, pacemaker for cardioinhibitory carotid sinus syndrome, hip protectors in institutions.
- **Post-fall care**: assess for injury and long lie (rhabdomyolysis, pressure injuries, hypothermia), check for fracture (pain, shortening/external rotation of the leg, inability to bear weight; X-ray; MRI/CT if X-ray negative but suspicion remains, because occult hip fractures occur), and refer to a falls clinic.

## 4. Cognition: delirium, dementia and depression

### 4.1 Distinguishing the three Ds

| Feature | Delirium | Dementia | Depression |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Onset** | Acute (hours–days) | Insidious (months–years) | Variable (weeks–months) |
| **Course** | Fluctuating, worse at night | Progressive, steady | Often diurnal variation (worse in the morning) |
| **Attention** | Impaired | Preserved until late | Normal or reduced concentration |
| **Consciousness** | Altered (hyper/hypoactive) | Clear | Clear |
| **Orientation** | Often impaired | Impaired later | Usually intact |
| **Memory** | Impaired short-term | Impaired recent memory early | Complaints out of proportion ("don't know" answers) |
| **Hallucinations** | Visual, common | Late (except Lewy body: early, well-formed) | Rare (mood-congruent psychosis in severe disease) |
| **Mood** | Labile | Flat/labile | Persistently low/anhedonic |
| **Reversibility** | Often reversible | Not reversible (some causes treatable) | Treatable |
| **Insight** | Fluctuating | Reduced | Preserved, with negative thinking |

*They often coexist:* dementia is the strongest risk factor for delirium; depression may precede or accompany dementia. **If in doubt, treat as delirium first** (chapter 56), then reassess cognition once the acute illness has resolved.

### 4.2 Dementia: diagnosis

Dementia (major neurocognitive disorder) is a decline in one or more cognitive domains (memory, executive function, language, visuospatial, attention, social cognition) that interferes with independence in daily activities, not explained by delirium or another mental disorder. Prevalence ≈ 5–8% over age 65, doubling every five years after 65, > 30% over 85. Mild cognitive impairment (MCI): decline with preserved function (progression to dementia ≈ 10–15% per year).

*Diagnostic approach.*

1. **History from patient and informant**: onset, progression, domains, behaviour, mood, psychosis, function, safety (driving, finances, cooking, wandering), sleep (REM behaviour disorder), medications, alcohol, vascular risk factors, head injury, family history.
2. **Cognitive testing**: MoCA or MMSE, clock-drawing, Addenbrooke's; formal neuropsychology for atypical or early cases.
3. **Examination**: neurological (focal signs, Parkinsonism, gait, frontal release signs, eye movements), cardiovascular, hearing/vision.
4. **Investigations to exclude reversible causes**: FBC, U&E, calcium, glucose/HbA1c, LFT, TSH, B12, folate, ± HIV and syphilis serology if risk factors, vitamin D, lipids. **Neuroimaging**: MRI (preferred) or CT head once in most patients (exclude tumour, subdural, hydrocephalus (normal pressure hydrocephalus: gait apraxia, incontinence, dementia), vascular disease; pattern of atrophy). Lumbar puncture (CSF amyloid/tau), amyloid PET, FDG-PET, DaTscan, EEG as needed; genetic testing in early-onset or familial. Blood biomarkers (plasma p-tau217, Aβ42/40) are emerging for Alzheimer disease.

*Dementia subtypes.*

| Subtype | Key features |
|---|---|
| **Alzheimer disease** (60–70%) | Gradual episodic memory loss (forgetting recent events, repeating), then language, visuospatial, executive dysfunction; hippocampal and parietal atrophy; amyloid and tau; APOE ε4 risk. |
| **Vascular dementia** (15–20%, often mixed) | Stepwise or fluctuating, focal signs, executive dysfunction, apathy, gait problems, pseudobulbar affect, vascular risk factors; infarcts/white-matter disease on imaging |
| **Dementia with Lewy bodies** (10–15%) | Fluctuating cognition and alertness, well-formed visual hallucinations, REM sleep behaviour disorder, Parkinsonism (cognitive decline within 1 year of motor symptoms = DLB; after 1 year of established Parkinson disease = PD dementia), neuroleptic sensitivity (avoid antipsychotics: severe reactions); cholinesterase inhibitors help |
| **Frontotemporal dementia** (≈ 5–10%, younger onset 45–65) | Behavioural variant: disinhibition, apathy, loss of empathy, compulsions, hyperorality; primary progressive aphasia (semantic or non-fluent); relatively preserved memory early |
| **Normal pressure hydrocephalus** | Gait disturbance (magnetic), urinary incontinence, cognitive slowing; ventriculomegaly; improvement after high-volume LP or shunt |
| **Others** | Parkinson disease dementia, Creutzfeldt–Jakob disease (rapid decline over weeks to months, myoclonus, ataxia; MRI DWI, RT-QuIC), alcohol-related dementia (Korsakoff), HIV-associated, neurosyphilis, B12 deficiency, hypothyroidism, subdural haematoma, brain tumour, autoimmune encephalitis, chronic traumatic encephalopathy, Huntington disease |

*Management.* Non-drug: diagnosis disclosure with support, cognitive stimulation, exercise, treatment of hearing loss, vision, depression; carer education and respite; driving assessment; home safety; advance care planning early while capacity remains; legal and financial planning (power of attorney); behavioural interventions for BPSD (behavioural and psychological symptoms of dementia). Drug: **cholinesterase inhibitors** (donepezil, rivastigmine, galantamine) for mild–moderate Alzheimer disease, DLB and PDD (side effects: nausea, bradycardia, syncope, weight loss, vivid dreams; caution in conduction disease); **memantine** for moderate–severe disease; **anti-amyloid monoclonal antibodies** (lecanemab, donanemab) for selected early Alzheimer patients with confirmed amyloid, with monitoring for ARIA (amyloid-related imaging abnormalities) and MRI surveillance; treat vascular risk factors. Avoid antipsychotics except for severe distress or danger (increased mortality and stroke risk), avoid benzodiazepines and anticholinergics. Treat pain, constipation, infection, and sleep problems before attributing agitation to dementia. Palliative care in advanced disease (feeding tubes not recommended; careful hand feeding).

### 4.3 Depression in older adults

Common, under-recognised, associated with suicide (highest rates in older men), medical illness, disability, cognitive impairment. Screen with GDS-15 or PHQ-2. Treatment: psychotherapy, exercise, SSRIs (sertraline, escitalopram; monitor sodium, bleeding, QTc, falls), mirtazapine for insomnia/appetite, avoid tricyclics, ECT for severe or psychotic cases; see chapter 56. Rule out hypothyroidism, B12 deficiency, anaemia, Parkinson disease, cancer, drug causes.

## 5. Medications in older adults

### 5.1 Problem statement

Older adults are the largest consumers of drugs; 40–50% of those over 65 take ≥ 5 drugs (polypharmacy) and ≈ 10–20% take ≥ 10. Adverse drug events account for 10–20% of hospital admissions in older people and are often preventable. Pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic changes plus multimorbidity mean the usual dose may be too high; clinical trial evidence often excludes the frail elderly.

**Drugs most often implicated**: anticoagulants (warfarin, DOACs), insulin and sulfonylureas (hypoglycaemia), digoxin, diuretics, ACE inhibitors/ARBs (AKI, hyperkalaemia), NSAIDs (GI bleed, AKI, heart failure), opioids (sedation, constipation, falls), benzodiazepines/Z-drugs, antipsychotics, antidepressants (hyponatraemia), anticholinergics, lithium, antiepileptics, antihypertensives (orthostasis), and antibiotics (C. difficile, tendon rupture with fluoroquinolones).

### 5.2 Principles of prescribing

1. **Start low, go slow**, but go (do not under-treat).
2. Prescribe only for a clear indication; avoid drugs of marginal benefit.
3. Review renal function and use eGFR with caution in the frail (consider cystatin C or creatinine clearance).
4. Check interactions and the **anticholinergic burden**.
5. Avoid **prescribing cascades**: a drug causes an adverse effect that is misinterpreted as a new condition and treated with another drug (e.g., amlodipine → ankle oedema → furosemide → urinary urgency → oxybutynin → confusion → donepezil).
6. Simplify regimens (once-daily dosing, fixed-dose combinations, pill organisers), supply clear written instructions.
7. Review regularly (at least annually, and after any admission); discuss goals of care.

### 5.3 Tools

- **Beers Criteria** (AGS): lists potentially inappropriate medications (e.g., first-generation antihistamines, benzodiazepines, long-acting sulfonylureas (glibenclamide), muscle relaxants, TCAs, meperidine, sliding-scale insulin alone, PPI > 8 weeks without indication, NSAIDs in CKD/heart failure, antipsychotics in dementia).
- **STOPP/START** (European): STOPP identifies drugs to stop (e.g., aspirin for primary prevention in the very old, antihypertensives in orthostatic hypotension, NSAIDs with anticoagulants), START identifies omissions (statin in established CVD, anticoagulation in AF, bone protection in osteoporosis, ACE inhibitor in heart failure, vitamin D).
- **Deprescribing**: for each drug ask: Is there an indication? Is it working? Does it harm? Is it consistent with the patient's goals and life expectancy? Taper those with withdrawal risk (benzodiazepines, antidepressants, beta-blockers, PPIs, steroids). Time to benefit: statins for primary prevention take 2–5 years; bisphosphonates 1 year; intensive glycaemic control > 8–10 years. Relax targets in frailty (HbA1c 7.5–8.5%/58–69 mmol/mol; blood pressure systolic 130–150).

### 5.4 Specific drug cautions

| Drug class | Cautions |
|---|---|
| Anticoagulants (AF) | Do not withhold because of age or fall risk alone (stroke risk usually exceeds bleeding risk); DOAC dose by label criteria; HAS-BLED to modify risk factors |
| Antihypertensives | Treat to target if tolerated (SPRINT), but avoid orthostatic hypotension; reduce in frailty; check sodium/potassium |
| Diabetes | Avoid hypoglycaemia: stop sulfonylureas where possible; metformin dose by eGFR; SGLT2i (volume depletion, genital infection, euglycaemic DKA); GLP-1 (weight loss/sarcopenia) |
| Statins | Secondary prevention beneficial to age 75–85; primary prevention in ≥ 75: individualise |
| PPIs | Long-term risks (C. difficile, fractures, hypomagnesaemia, B12 deficiency); deprescribe if no indication |
| Benzodiazepines/Z-drugs | Avoid: falls, fractures, delirium; taper slowly |
| Opioids | Start low, prefer fentanyl/buprenorphine patches in renal impairment; always co-prescribe laxatives; avoid pethidine/tramadol (serotonin syndrome, seizures) |
| NSAIDs | Avoid; use paracetamol, topical NSAIDs; if essential, gastroprotection |
| Antipsychotics | Increased mortality in dementia (boxed warning); sudden severe reactions in DLB and Parkinson disease |
| Anticholinergics (oxybutynin, amitriptyline, chlorphenamine, hyoscine) | Delirium, cognitive decline, constipation, retention |
| Digoxin | Narrow therapeutic index, renal dependent; target level 0.5–0.9 ng/mL |
| Fluoroquinolones | Tendon rupture (with steroids), delirium, aortic aneurysm, hypoglycaemia |

## 6. Geriatric syndromes

### 6.1 Urinary and faecal incontinence

Urinary incontinence affects ≈ 30–40% of older women and 10–25% of older men; it is not a normal part of ageing. Types:

- **Stress**: leakage with cough/exertion; pelvic floor weakness; pelvic floor muscle training; surgery.
- **Urgency/overactive bladder**: urgency, frequency, nocturia, urge incontinence; bladder training; antimuscarinics (anticholinergic burden: avoid in dementia) or mirabegron/vibegron; vaginal oestrogen.
- **Overflow**: dribbling, weak stream, incomplete emptying (BPH, constipation, neuropathy, drugs: anticholinergics, opioids, calcium-channel blockers); bladder scan; catheterisation/ surgery.
- **Functional**: mobility/cognition problems, environment; prompted voiding, accessible toilet, commode.
- **Mixed**.
- **Transient (reversible) causes: DIAPPERS**: Delirium, Infection (symptomatic), Atrophic vaginitis/urethritis, Pharmaceuticals (diuretics, sedatives, alpha-blockers), Psychological (depression), Excess urine output (hyperglycaemia, heart failure, hypercalcaemia, nocturnal polyuria), Restricted mobility, Stool impaction.
Assessment: bladder diary, post-void residual, urinalysis, medication review, stress test, rectal and pelvic examination; avoid routine urodynamics unless complex.
- **Faecal incontinence/constipation**: faecal impaction with overflow is common (rectal examination), treat with osmotic laxatives (macrogol) ± stimulant; review drugs; sphincter damage; fibre/fluids; exclude colorectal cancer if change in bowel habit.

### 6.2 Pressure injuries

Risk: immobility, incontinence, malnutrition, sensory loss, poor perfusion. Stage by tissue involvement (stage 1 non-blanchable erythema to stage 4 with exposed bone/tendon; unstageable; deep tissue injury). Prevent: risk assessment (Braden), repositioning every 2–4 hours, pressure-redistributing mattresses, heel offloading, skin care, nutrition (protein and calories), moisture management. Treat: relieve pressure, debride necrosis, moist dressing, treat infection (osteomyelitis suspected: probe-to-bone, MRI), optimise nutrition; pain control.

### 6.3 Malnutrition, dysphagia and weight loss

Unintentional weight loss > 5% in 1 month or > 10% in 6 months is significant; assess for causes: **MEALS ON WHEELS** (Medications, Emotional problems/depression, Anorexia tardive/alcoholism, Late-life paranoia, Swallowing problems, Oral problems, No money, Wandering/dementia, Hyperthyroidism/hypercalcaemia/hypoadrenalism, Enteric problems, Eating problems, Low salt/low cholesterol diets, Stones/surgery/social isolation) and malignancy (chapter 30). Screen with MNA-SF/MUST. Management: treat causes; dentition; modify texture (speech therapist); fortify foods; oral nutritional supplements; assistance at meals; appetite stimulants are of limited value; tube feeding has no proven benefit in advanced dementia.

*Dysphagia*: oropharyngeal (neurological: stroke, Parkinson, dementia; coughing/choking with meals, wet voice, recurrent chest infections) or oesophageal; videofluoroscopy/FEES; thickened fluids/texture modification, positioning, oral hygiene; aspiration pneumonia prevention.

### 6.4 Sleep, pain, vision and hearing

- **Sleep**: fragmented sleep is common; exclude sleep apnoea, restless legs, nocturia, pain, depression, medications (diuretics, steroids, SSRIs, caffeine), REM behaviour disorder; CBT-I, light exposure and exercise; avoid hypnotics.
- **Pain**: persistent pain in 50% of community elders and > 80% in nursing homes; underreported and undertreated; assess with numeric scales or PAINAD in dementia; paracetamol, topical agents, cautious opioids, neuropathic agents at low dose; non-drug approaches. Behavioural change in dementia may reflect pain.
- **Vision**: cataract, AMD, glaucoma, diabetic retinopathy; annual eye review; correct refractive error; low-vision rehabilitation; Charles Bonnet syndrome (visual hallucinations in visual loss with preserved insight).
- **Hearing**: presbycusis affects > 60% over 70; associated with cognitive decline, depression, isolation; screen with whisper test/audiometry; cerumen removal; hearing aids; cochlear implant in selected patients; communication strategies.

### 6.5 Elder abuse and self-neglect

Physical, psychological, financial, sexual abuse, neglect and abandonment occur in 5–10% of community-dwelling older adults, more in cognitively impaired and dependent patients; perpetrators are often family members or carers. Red flags: unexplained injuries, repeated falls or burns, delay in seeking care, inconsistent history, poor hygiene, malnutrition, pressure sores, withdrawal, fearfulness, carer controlling or refusing to leave the patient alone, sudden financial changes, missing possessions. Approach: interview patient alone; use screening tools (e.g., Elder Abuse Suspicion Index), document carefully, assess capacity, ensure safety, involve social services/adult safeguarding, report as required by local law.

### 6.6 Functional decline in hospital

"Hospital-associated disability": bed rest produces rapid deconditioning (muscle loss 1–1.5% per day). Mobilise early, avoid unnecessary catheters, restraints, sedatives, and fasting; keep glasses and hearing aids; provide cognitive stimulation; involve physiotherapy and occupational therapy early; plan discharge from admission.

## 7. Specific diagnostic issues

### 7.1 Infection

- Use a lower threshold to investigate: blood cultures, urine (if localising signs or sepsis), chest X-ray, CRP, procalcitonin.
- **Fever criteria in older adults**: single oral temperature > 37.8 °C, repeated > 37.2 °C (oral) or > 37.5 °C (rectal), or increase of > 1.1 °C over baseline.
- Non-specific signs: new confusion, falls, reduced appetite, functional decline, hypotension, tachypnoea, tachycardia > 100.
- Pneumonia (aspiration risk), UTI (do not treat asymptomatic bacteriuria), skin and soft tissue infection, intra-abdominal sepsis (diverticulitis, cholecystitis), C. difficile (diarrhoea after antibiotics), tuberculosis (reactivation), herpes zoster (vaccinate), influenza and COVID-19, endocarditis, bone and joint infection.
- Vaccination: influenza, pneumococcal, COVID-19, RSV (≥ 60-75 years or at risk), zoster (recombinant), tetanus-diphtheria.

### 7.2 Cardiovascular and respiratory

- **Hypertension**: treat systolic ≥ 140–150 (individualise; target < 130–140 if well, less strict in frail); check orthostatic BP.
- **Atrial fibrillation**: anticoagulate by CHA₂DS₂-VASc (age ≥ 75 = 2 points); rate control; screen with pulse palpation or single-lead ECG.
- **Heart failure**: HFpEF predominates in older women; diuretics, SGLT2 inhibitors; exclude other contributors to dyspnoea.
- **Aortic stenosis**: severe symptomatic AS requires valve replacement (TAVI can be offered to frail patients).
- **COPD, pneumonia, PE, lung cancer** (chapter 46).

### 7.3 Endocrine, bone and musculoskeletal

- **Osteoporosis and fractures**: screen with FRAX/DXA; calcium (diet first), vitamin D, bisphosphonates, denosumab, anabolic agents; after any fragility fracture, treat (fracture liaison service). **Hip fracture**: surgery within 24–48 h, orthogeriatric care, early mobilisation, nutrition and delirium prevention.
- **Hyponatraemia**: drugs (thiazides, SSRIs), SIADH; interpret with volume status.
- **Thyroid**: check TSH when symptoms are non-specific; subclinical hypothyroidism often does not need treatment if TSH < 10 in older adults.
- **Polymyalgia rheumatica/GCA**: age > 50, proximal pain/stiffness, raised ESR/CRP; steroids; always ask about headache, jaw claudication, visual symptoms (chapter 52, 55).
- **Gout, pseudogout, osteoarthritis, septic arthritis** (monarthritis in an older adult with fever: aspirate).

### 7.4 Cancer

Common: prostate, lung, colorectal, breast, pancreas. Symptoms may be dismissed as "ageing". Use CGA to guide decisions on treatment (G8 screening tool), weigh benefit versus harm, and respect goals. Screening: stop when life expectancy < 10 years (breast, colorectal, cervical, prostate) unless symptoms.

### 7.5 Anaemia, blood tests and "normal" values

Anaemia (Hb < 130 g/L men, < 120 g/L women) is never "normal" with age: investigate (iron deficiency → GI blood loss/cancer; B12/folate; CKD; myelodysplasia; myeloma; chronic disease). Reference ranges: ESR rises with age (age/2 for men, (age+10)/2 for women); creatinine is misleading; troponin is higher at baseline (use high-sensitivity assays with age-specific awareness); D-dimer cut-off age-adjusted (age × 10 µg/L FEU after 50 years); sodium, calcium, glucose and TSH interpretation need care.

## 8. Capacity, ethics and care planning

### 8.1 Capacity

Presume capacity; assess decision-specific capacity if concern (understand, retain, weigh, communicate; chapter 56). Optimise conditions: treat delirium, hearing/vision aids, appropriate time and setting, simple language, repeated assessments. Unwise decisions do not indicate lack of capacity. If lacking capacity, act in the best interests, consult the legal representative, previous wishes, family and carers.

### 8.2 Advance care planning (ACP)

Discuss goals, values, preferences (where to be cared for, resuscitation, hospital transfer, ventilation, artificial nutrition, organ donation), appoint a health care proxy/lasting power of attorney, document and review. Resuscitation outcomes: survival to discharge after in-hospital CPR is ≈ 15–20% overall and lower in frail older adults with multimorbidity; honest discussions based on prognosis and preferences (not age alone). Respect treatment-escalation plans (e.g., ReSPECT form, POLST, MOLST).

### 8.3 Prognosis and de-escalation

Use frailty, comorbidity, function, nutritional state, and trajectory (ePrognosis tools) to inform decisions about investigation and treatment. Avoid overtreatment (intensive screening or invasive tests with little chance of benefit) and undertreatment (ageism). Palliative care principles apply early in frailty, dementia, organ failure and cancer (chapter 63).

### 8.4 Ageism

Age should not be the sole basis for withholding investigation or treatment. Evidence from trials in older adults (e.g., SPRINT, HYVET for blood pressure, anticoagulation in AF, hip fracture surgery, thrombolysis in stroke) shows benefit. Communicate respectfully, avoid "elderspeak", and involve patients in decisions.

## Worked cases

### Case 1: "Off her legs"

**Presentation.** Mrs M., 86, lives alone, sees daily carers, with mild memory problems. Over 2 days she became drowsy, refusing food, with new urinary incontinence. Temperature 36.4 °C, HR 96, BP 100/58, RR 26, SpO₂ 92%. Mild confusion, inattentive (cannot say months backwards), dry mouth. Chest: crackles right base. Urinalysis: leukocytes ++. CRP 156, WBC 15, creatinine 148 µmol/L (baseline 80), sodium 128.

**Reasoning.** Delirium superimposed on dementia, due to sepsis (probable pneumonia; urine dip is likely confounded by bacteriuria), with AKI and hyponatraemia; respiratory rate 26 and hypoxia are the best clues; absence of fever is typical. Sepsis screening is positive (qSOFA: RR ≥ 22, altered mentation).

**Action.** Sepsis bundle (chapter 39): cultures, lactate, IV fluids cautiously (AKI, hyponatraemia), antibiotics for community-acquired pneumonia (e.g., co-amoxiclav), oxygen, chest X-ray (right lower lobe consolidation), review medications (stop ramipril, furosemide), treat delirium non-pharmacologically, correct sodium slowly, urine culture, catheter avoidance, early mobilisation, delirium prevention (glasses, hearing aid, orientation), nutrition, speech therapy (aspiration risk). Discuss goals and resuscitation preferences with patient and family. CGA before discharge; reassess cognition at 3 months.

**Lessons.** In older adults, delirium, tachypnoea, and falls may be the only signs of sepsis. A positive urine dipstick does not make a UTI.

### Case 2: A fall with a "mechanical" explanation

**Presentation.** Mr G., 78, hypertensive and diabetic, fell in the bathroom at night; "tripped on the rug". No head strike; on ramipril, amlodipine, bendroflumethiazide, tamsulosin, glimepiride, zopiclone, oxybutynin. Lying BP 148/80; standing at 1 min 112/68; HR 72 unchanged. Gait slow. Glucose 3.4 mmol/L at the time of the paramedics' arrival.

**Reasoning.** Multifactorial fall: orthostatic hypotension (tamsulosin, thiazide, ACE inhibitor, amlodipine), nocturia, hypoglycaemia (sulfonylurea), sedatives (zopiclone), anticholinergic (oxybutynin), environment (rug). Not just a trip.

**Action.** Stop zopiclone (taper), oxybutynin, bendroflumethiazide; review need for tamsulosin (give at night, check nocturia cause: nocturnal polyuria, BPH), change glimepiride to a safer agent, relax HbA1c target (7.5–8.0%); BP target; check ECG, U&E, B12, vitamin D, TFT; physiotherapy balance programme, occupational therapy home hazard visit (remove rug, night lights, grab rails), cataract/optician review, footwear; falls clinic. Post-fall: examine for injury; check hip.

**Lessons.** "Tripped" is rarely the full story; look for orthostasis, hypoglycaemia and medication culprits.

### Case 3: Forgetful and hallucinating

**Presentation.** Mr L., 74, daughter notes 18 months of fluctuating confusion, vivid hallucinations of children in the house (he is not frightened), acting out dreams at night (kicked his wife), mild tremor and slowness, fainting episodes. He became severely rigid and unresponsive after a dose of haloperidol given in hospital for agitation. MoCA 17/30 with marked visuospatial and attention deficits, relatively preserved recall.

**Reasoning.** Dementia with Lewy bodies (core features: fluctuations, visual hallucinations, REM sleep behaviour disorder, Parkinsonism; severe neuroleptic sensitivity). Autonomic dysfunction causing syncope.

**Action.** Avoid antipsychotics (if needed for severe psychosis: quetiapine or clozapine at very low doses); start rivastigmine or donepezil; treat orthostatic hypotension (non-drug first, then fludrocortisone/midodrine/droxidopa with caution), melatonin or clonazepam low-dose for RBD with safety measures; DaTscan if diagnosis uncertain; MRI to exclude vascular/NPH; carer support, driving cessation, planning, discuss prognosis.

**Lessons.** Visual hallucinations and fluctuations point to Lewy body dementia; the drug choice can save his life.

### Case 4: The painless heart attack

**Presentation.** Mrs R., 82, diabetic, presents with 2 days of fatigue, nausea and new breathlessness on stairs, no chest pain. HR 108, BP 100/60, bibasal crackles, JVP raised. ECG: ST depression V4–V6; high-sensitivity troponin 480 ng/L (rising).

**Reasoning.** Non-ST-elevation myocardial infarction presenting atypically with dyspnoea and heart failure (diabetes, age, female sex increase atypical presentations).

**Action.** Admit to cardiology; antiplatelets (aspirin, ticagrelor/clopidogrel) and anticoagulation (fondaparinux/heparin) with renal dosing; oxygen if hypoxic; diuretic; echocardiogram; consider early coronary angiography after frailty/goals assessment (older patients benefit from invasive strategy when fit enough, with attention to bleeding and contrast nephropathy), statin and beta-blocker; reduce bleeding risk; cardiac rehabilitation; discuss goals.

**Lessons.** Always consider MI in older adults with dyspnoea, nausea, fatigue, confusion or syncope; check ECG and troponin.

### Case 5: Weight loss and a flat mood

**Presentation.** Mr S., 79, widower, lost 8 kg in 6 months, low appetite, poor sleep, withdrawn, says "I'm a burden". Mild constipation; MoCA 24/30; GDS-15 = 11. Examination: thin, no masses. Bloods: Hb 108, MCV 78, ferritin 8, TSH normal, calcium normal. Stool occult blood positive.

**Reasoning.** Depression is likely, but weight loss with iron deficiency anaemia needs a gastrointestinal malignancy work-up before attributing everything to mood.

**Action.** Colonoscopy/CT colonography and upper endoscopy (right colon cancer found); address depression (sertraline, psychotherapy, social support), suicide risk assessment (ask directly; widowed older men are high-risk), nutrition (dietitian, fortification), iron replacement, MDT discussion about treatment based on CGA and goals.

**Lessons.** Don't stop at the psychiatric explanation: depression and cancer coexist. Anaemia + weight loss in an older person is cancer until excluded.

### Case 6: Bruises and the quiet carer

**Presentation.** Mrs F., 84, dementia, lives with her son, brought in with finger-shaped bruises on both upper arms, a healing rib fracture, and a pressure sore on the sacrum. She is quiet, cowers when her son enters. Son answers all the questions, says she "falls a lot" and refuses to leave her side.

**Reasoning.** Elder abuse and neglect suspected: pattern bruising, fracture, pressure injury, inconsistent history, carer control.

**Action.** Examine her alone if possible, document injuries (body map, photographs per policy), investigate (X-ray survey for fractures, blood tests, bone profile), treat injuries and pressure sore, assess capacity and needs, inform the safeguarding team (adult protection), ensure safe environment (admit if needed), involve social work and police as per law, support the carer's needs without excusing abuse, ensure ongoing care plan.

**Lessons.** Document and escalate; patient safety is the priority; carers under strain also need help.

## Common pitfalls

1. **Attributing symptoms to "old age"** without searching for treatable causes.
2. **Missing atypical presentations** (painless MI, afebrile sepsis, silent abdomen).
3. **Treating a positive urine dipstick** in a confused older patient as a UTI (and stopping the search).
4. **Overlooking delirium** (especially hypoactive) and missing medication causes.
5. **Not measuring orthostatic blood pressure** after a fall or dizziness.
6. **Over-reliance on creatinine/eGFR** for drug dosing in the frail.
7. **Polypharmacy and prescribing cascades**; starting new drugs without stopping old ones.
8. **Starting antipsychotics or benzodiazepines** for agitation without searching for cause (pain, retention, constipation, infection).
9. **Ageism**: withholding anticoagulation, surgery or treatment solely because of age, or conversely, overtreatment with no benefit.
10. **Ignoring hearing and vision problems**, which mimic or worsen cognitive impairment.
11. **Diagnosing dementia during an acute illness** without reassessment after recovery.
12. **Missing depression** and suicide risk in older men.
13. **Failure to discuss goals of care** and resuscitation preferences.
14. **Not looking for elder abuse** or considering carer stress.
15. **Bed rest and restraint** leading to hospital-associated disability.
16. **Neglecting nutrition, dentition and swallowing.**

---

## Key points

- Older adults present atypically; confusion, falls, and functional decline are non-specific signs of acute illness. Always ask "what has changed?"
- Use CGA and frailty scales (CFS) to guide diagnosis, treatment and prognosis.
- Evaluate falls multifactorially: orthostatic BP, medications, vision, gait, ECG; intervene with exercise and medication review.
- Distinguish delirium (acute, fluctuating, inattentive), dementia (progressive) and depression; they overlap.
- Review medications at every contact; use STOPP/START and Beers; avoid prescribing cascades and anticholinergics.
- Treat urinary tract infection only when there are urinary symptoms or sepsis.
- Anaemia, weight loss and new symptoms deserve investigation tailored to the person's goals.
- Capacity is decision-specific; plan ahead; respect preferences; avoid ageism and overtreatment alike.
- Consider elder abuse in unexplained injury, neglect, and carer behaviour.

## Self-assessment

**Questions**

1. List five atypical presentations of acute illness in older adults and the likely underlying diagnoses.
2. How do you measure orthostatic hypotension, and what is the definition?
3. What features distinguish delirium from dementia?
4. A 75-year-old on warfarin for AF has had two falls in a year. Should anticoagulation be stopped?
5. What are the core features of dementia with Lewy bodies and the key prescribing caution?
6. Explain a "prescribing cascade" with an example.
7. A 90-year-old has a positive dipstick for leucocytes and nitrites but no symptoms. What do you do?
8. Name three drug classes that commonly cause falls in older adults and an alternative strategy for each.
9. What does a Clinical Frailty Scale of 6 imply?
10. Which findings in an older adult with a fall should prompt a search for abuse?

**Answers**

1. Examples: confusion/delirium (sepsis, pneumonia, UTI, MI, hypoglycaemia, drugs, retention); falls (infection, arrhythmia, orthostatic hypotension, anaemia); painless MI (dyspnoea, fatigue, nausea); silent abdomen with perforation/ischaemia; hypothermia in sepsis; apathetic hyperthyroidism (weight loss, AF).
2. Measure BP and pulse after 5 minutes supine, then at 1 and 3 minutes standing. Orthostatic hypotension: fall of ≥ 20 mmHg systolic or ≥ 10 mmHg diastolic, or standing systolic < 90 mmHg; absent heart-rate rise suggests autonomic failure or beta-blockade.
3. Delirium: acute onset, fluctuating course, inattention, altered consciousness, often reversible; dementia: insidious, progressive, clear consciousness until late, attention preserved early.
4. Generally no. Fall risk alone rarely outweighs stroke prevention benefit (one would need to fall about 295 times a year for the subdural risk to outweigh benefit in some analyses). Address modifiable fall risk, consider a DOAC (lower intracranial bleeding), discuss risks and preferences.
5. Fluctuating cognition, recurrent well-formed visual hallucinations, REM sleep behaviour disorder, Parkinsonism; supportive: severe neuroleptic sensitivity, autonomic dysfunction, hypersomnia. Avoid antipsychotics (haloperidol, risperidone); cholinesterase inhibitors help.
6. A drug causes an adverse effect that is misinterpreted as a new condition and treated with another drug. Example: amlodipine → ankle oedema → furosemide → urinary frequency/incontinence → oxybutynin → confusion. Solution: recognise and stop the offending drug.
7. Asymptomatic bacteriuria: no urine culture and no antibiotics. Look for other problems only if there are clinical concerns (symptoms, fever, sepsis, delirium with no other cause).
8. Benzodiazepines/Z-drugs (CBT-I, sleep hygiene, taper); antihypertensives/diuretics (de-escalate, review targets, check orthostatic BP); anticholinergics/antipsychotics/antidepressants (stop or alternative, e.g., mirabegron for overactive bladder, SSRIs with sodium monitoring); also sulfonylureas, opioids, alpha-blockers.
9. Moderately frail: needs help with all outside activities and housekeeping, difficulty with stairs and bathing; high risk of adverse outcomes; targets for treatment should consider function and goals; consider CGA.
10. Unexplained or patterned bruises (grip marks, inner arms, ears), burns, fractures inconsistent with history, delayed care, repeated "falls", poor hygiene, malnutrition, pressure injuries, fear or withdrawal, controlling carer, financial exploitation.
