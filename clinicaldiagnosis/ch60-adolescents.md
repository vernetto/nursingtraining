# Chapter 60. Adolescents

## Learning objectives

After studying this chapter you should be able to:

1. Conduct a confidential adolescent consultation, including a HEEADSSS psychosocial assessment, and explain the limits of confidentiality.
2. Assess pubertal development and growth using Tanner staging, and recognise delayed, early and abnormal puberty.
3. Evaluate common presentations: fatigue, headache, abdominal pain, menstrual disorders, acne, scoliosis, sports injuries, syncope and chest pain.
4. Recognise mental health problems in adolescents: depression, anxiety, self-harm, suicide risk, eating disorders, psychosis, and neurodevelopmental conditions.
5. Assess substance use, sexual health, contraception, sexually transmitted infections, and sexual violence.
6. Apply the principles of consent, capacity and safeguarding in minors.
7. Recognise medical conditions that present or worsen in adolescence (type 1 diabetes, asthma, epilepsy, IBD, cystic fibrosis) and plan transition to adult care.
8. Identify the leading causes of adolescent morbidity and mortality — injuries, suicide, violence, substance use — and use opportunities for prevention.

> **Note on thresholds and laws.** Age of consent, confidentiality rules, mandatory reporting and access to contraception differ by country and region. Learn and follow your local legislation and institutional policies.

---

## 1. Why adolescents are different

Adolescence (WHO: 10–19 years; many guidelines extend to 24) is a period of rapid physical, cognitive, emotional and social change. Adolescents are usually physically healthy, which means that their health problems are often behavioural, psychological and social. The leading causes of death worldwide in this group are road traffic injuries, suicide, interpersonal violence, drowning, and (in some regions) HIV, maternal conditions and infectious diseases. Most risky behaviours (smoking, alcohol, substance use, unsafe sex, poor diet, inactivity) begin in adolescence and carry into adulthood. Half of adult mental illness starts by age 14.

Adolescents often attend health services rarely, may be accompanied by a parent who does most of the talking, and fear judgement and breaches of confidentiality. A good consultation can change their trajectory.

### 1.1 Cognitive and developmental stages

| Stage | Typical age | Features |
|---|---|---|
| **Early** | 10–13 | Concrete thinking, body-image concerns, same-sex friendships, puberty onset, increasing independence |
| **Middle** | 14–16 | Abstract thinking emerging, peer influence peaks, risk-taking, identity exploration, romantic interests, conflict with parents |
| **Late** | 17–19+ | Future orientation, stable identity, greater impulse control, career and relationship planning |

The adolescent brain: the limbic (reward, emotion) system matures before the prefrontal cortex (judgement, impulse control). This partly explains risk-taking, sensation-seeking and susceptibility to peer pressure, and why information alone does not change behaviour.

## 2. The adolescent consultation

### 2.1 Setting up

- **Welcome the adolescent first**, address them directly, and treat them as the primary patient.
- **Explain the structure**: "I usually spend part of the visit with young people on their own. That is routine for everyone."
- **See them alone** for at least part of every visit, after first seeing them together with the parent/carer if present.
- **Explain confidentiality and its limits at the start**: "What we talk about stays between us, unless I'm worried that you or someone else is in danger of serious harm; if that happens I will tell you first who I need to talk to and why." Be familiar with local laws on mandatory reporting (abuse, serious self-harm risk, some sexual activity).
- Offer a **chaperone** for physical examination, especially intimate examinations, and respect modesty.
- Use plain, non-judgemental language; avoid assumptions about sexual orientation, gender identity, relationships or culture; use the young person's chosen name and pronouns.
- Pace the conversation: start with less sensitive topics, build rapport, and use open questions.
- Use technology (private questionnaires, tablet screening) to improve disclosure.

### 2.2 HEEADSSS psychosocial assessment

| Domain | Example questions | Red flags |
|---|---|---|
| **H**ome | Who lives at home? How do you get on? Any changes (move, divorce, new partner, bereavement)? Any safety concerns? | Violence, abuse, neglect, homelessness, running away, foster care |
| **E**ducation / **E**mployment | Which school or work? Grades, attendance, favourite/worst subjects, bullying, future plans | Truancy, school refusal, exclusion, bullying, falling grades, learning difficulties |
| **E**ating and **E**xercise | What do you usually eat? Body image? Dieting, bingeing, vomiting, exercise habits? | Restriction, purging, excessive exercise, body dissatisfaction, anabolic steroid or supplement use |
| **A**ctivities / peers | Friends, hobbies, social media use, screen time, online safety, gaming | Isolation, cyberbullying, grooming, gang involvement, excessive screen use |
| **D**rugs | Tobacco/vaping, alcohol, cannabis, other drugs? Do friends use? CRAFFT questions | Regular or solitary use, driving under influence, dependence |
| **S**exuality | Relationships, sexual attraction, sexual activity, contraception, condoms, STI/HIV testing, pregnancy, consent, coercion, sexual orientation/gender identity | Unprotected sex, multiple partners, coercion, exploitation, pregnancy, STI, distress about identity |
| **S**uicide / **S**elf-harm / **S**afety / depression | How is your mood? Sleep? Ever felt so sad you thought of hurting yourself or ending your life? Self-harm? Safety at home/in relationships/online? Seat belts, helmets, weapons? | Persistent low mood, anhedonia, hopelessness, self-harm, suicidal ideation, plan, access to means, weapons |

Add **S**leep and **S**ocial media; some use **SSHADESS** (Strengths, School, Home, Activities, Drugs, Emotions/depression, Sexuality, Safety), which begins with strengths to build rapport.

**Screening tools**: PHQ-A (adolescent depression), GAD-7, ASQ (Ask Suicide-screening Questions: four questions), CRAFFT (Car, Relax, Alone, Forget, Friends, Trouble), SCOFF (eating disorders), Strengths and Difficulties Questionnaire.

### 2.3 Examination

Check height, weight, BMI percentile, blood pressure (with appropriate cuff), pulse; pubertal staging if relevant and with consent (Tanner staging may be by inspection or self-assessment with pictures); scoliosis screen (Adams forward-bend test); skin (acne, striae, self-harm scars, signs of injecting), thyroid, heart, lungs, abdomen, joints. Avoid unnecessary genital examination; if needed, explain, obtain consent and use a chaperone. Pelvic examination is not a routine element of reproductive health in the absence of symptoms.

## 3. Puberty and growth

### 3.1 Normal puberty

| Tanner stage | Girls: breast / pubic hair | Boys: genitalia / pubic hair |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Prepubertal | Prepubertal |
| 2 | Breast bud / sparse pubic hair | Testicular volume ≥ 4 mL (or 2.5 cm); scrotal reddening / sparse hair |
| 3 | Breast enlarges / darker curly hair | Penile lengthening, testes enlarge / curly hair |
| 4 | Areola forms secondary mound / adult-type hair | Penile widening, glans development / adult-type hair |
| 5 | Adult breast / adult hair to thighs | Adult genitalia / adult hair to thighs |

- **Girls**: breast development (thelarche) 8–13 years, growth spurt early (peak height velocity ≈ 12 years, about 7–9 cm/year), menarche on average at ≈ 12.5–13 years (Tanner 3–4), 2–2.5 years after thelarche.
- **Boys**: testicular enlargement 9–14 years, growth spurt later (peak ≈ 14 years, about 8–10 cm/year), voice break at Tanner 3–4.
- Adolescent growth is influenced by genetics, nutrition, chronic illness, and sex hormones.

### 3.2 Delayed puberty

Girls: no breast development by 13 years or no menarche by 15 years (or 3 years after thelarche). Boys: no testicular enlargement by 14 years.

*Causes*: **constitutional delay of growth and puberty** (most common in boys; family history, short, delayed bone age; reassure and consider short course testosterone), hypogonadotropic hypogonadism (functional: chronic disease, malnutrition, anorexia nervosa, excessive exercise, coeliac disease, IBD, CF, hypothyroidism, hyperprolactinaemia; permanent: Kallmann syndrome with anosmia, pituitary tumours/craniopharyngioma, cranial radiotherapy), hypergonadotropic hypogonadism (Turner syndrome 45,X, Klinefelter syndrome 47,XXY, gonadal damage from chemotherapy or radiotherapy, autoimmune oophoritis, testicular torsion/orchitis).

*Assessment*: growth chart, family history (parental pubertal timing), examination (Tanner, dysmorphic features, anosmia), bone age X-ray, LH, FSH, oestradiol or testosterone, prolactin, TSH, IGF-1, coeliac serology, karyotype in girls with high gonadotropins, pelvic ultrasound, MRI pituitary if indicated.

### 3.3 Precocious puberty

Breast development < 8 years in girls or testicular enlargement < 9 years in boys. Central (gonadotropin-dependent): idiopathic in most girls; in boys, more often organic (CNS tumours, hydrocephalus, neurofibromatosis): MRI brain. Peripheral: CAH, adrenal or gonadal tumours, McCune–Albright syndrome, exogenous oestrogens/androgens, severe hypothyroidism. Assess bone age, LH/FSH (GnRH stimulation), sex steroids, 17-hydroxyprogesterone, TSH. Treat central precocious puberty with GnRH analogues.

### 3.4 Gynaecomastia and other benign variants

Pubertal gynaecomastia affects up to 50–60% of boys at mid-puberty, usually resolves within 1–2 years; reassure. Investigate if tender, rapidly growing, firm, eccentric, or with other abnormalities (testicular mass, liver disease, drugs including anabolic steroids, cannabis, spironolactone, antipsychotics; Klinefelter). Physiological leucorrhoea, premature thelarche, and adrenarche are benign. **Constitutional tall or short stature**: assess predicted adult height.

## 4. Menstrual and gynaecological problems

### 4.1 Normal menstruation in adolescents

Menarche median ≈ 12.4–13 years; cycles 21–45 days in the first years after menarche (adult range 24–38), bleeding 2–7 days, loss < 80 mL. Anovulatory cycles are common for 2–3 years. Menstrual cycle is the "fifth vital sign" (ACOG): ask about it.

### 4.2 Primary amenorrhoea

No menarche by 15 years or within 3 years of thelarche.

| Category | Causes and clues |
|---|---|
| Normal secondary sexual characteristics | Müllerian agenesis (Mayer–Rokitansky–Küster–Hauser: absent uterus/vagina, normal ovaries), imperforate hymen (cyclical pain, bluish bulging hymen, haematocolpos), transverse vaginal septum, androgen insensitivity (46,XY; scant pubic hair, breast development, blind vagina) |
| Absent/low sex characteristics, low FSH/LH | Constitutional delay, functional hypothalamic amenorrhoea, hypopituitarism, Kallmann |
| Absent characteristics, high FSH/LH | Turner syndrome, premature ovarian insufficiency |
| Excess androgens | PCOS (diagnose after 2 years from menarche), CAH, androgen-secreting tumours |

Always do a pregnancy test. Investigate: examination (Tanner, signs of Turner, hirsutism, galactorrhoea, genital anatomy), FSH, LH, oestradiol, TSH, prolactin, testosterone, DHEAS, 17-OHP, karyotype, pelvic ultrasound or MRI.

### 4.3 Secondary amenorrhoea and oligomenorrhoea

Absence of menses for > 3 months (previously regular) or > 6 months (irregular). **Always exclude pregnancy first.** Causes: functional hypothalamic amenorrhoea (low energy availability from dieting, eating disorder, athletic training: Relative Energy Deficiency in Sport (RED-S) and the "female athlete triad"), PCOS, hyperprolactinaemia, thyroid disease, premature ovarian insufficiency, stress, drugs (antipsychotics, hormonal contraception), chronic illness. Management: treat cause; restore energy availability and weight; bone health (DEXA if > 6–12 months amenorrhoea); hormone therapy per guidelines.

### 4.4 Dysmenorrhoea, heavy bleeding and pelvic pain

- **Primary dysmenorrhoea**: lower abdominal cramps with onset of menses, typically in ovulatory cycles; treat with NSAIDs, heat, hormonal contraception. **Secondary**: endometriosis (severe, progressive, with dyspareunia and gastrointestinal/bladder symptoms; diagnostic delay of 7–10 years), obstructive Müllerian anomalies, PID, ovarian cyst. Refer if refractory to NSAIDs and hormonal therapy.
- **Heavy menstrual bleeding**: > 7 days, clots > 2.5 cm, soaking pads hourly, anaemia, interference with life. Anovulatory bleeding is the commonest cause, but **bleeding disorders** are found in 10–30% of adolescents with heavy periods since menarche (von Willebrand disease, platelet function disorders, ITP, leukaemia). Check FBC, ferritin, pregnancy test, TSH, coagulation, VWF; acute severe bleeding: IV fluids, tranexamic acid, hormonal therapy (combined oral contraceptive taper, progestin), admission if Hb < 80 g/L or unstable.
- **Acute pelvic pain**: always consider pregnancy/ectopic, PID, ovarian torsion (sudden unilateral pain with vomiting; Doppler flow may be present), ruptured or haemorrhagic cyst, appendicitis, UTI, haematocolpos.
- **PCOS**: oligomenorrhoea/anovulation, hirsutism or biochemical hyperandrogenism, and (in adults) polycystic ovarian morphology; diagnose in adolescents when persistent irregular cycles > 2 years after menarche with hyperandrogenism; lifestyle, metformin, combined OCP.

### 4.5 Vulvovaginal symptoms and breast concerns

Discharge: physiological leucorrhoea (white, non-itchy), bacterial vaginosis, candidiasis, trichomonas, chlamydia/gonorrhoea, foreign body (retained tampon), toxic shock syndrome (tampon use). Breast: fibroadenoma (most common lump, mobile, painless), cysts, mastitis; breast cancer is rare, but examine and image if worrisome (ultrasound first).

## 5. Common adolescent presentations

### 5.1 Fatigue and sleep

Fatigue is a frequent complaint. Think first of **insufficient sleep and delayed sleep phase** (teenagers need 8–10 h; circadian shift toward later sleep, early school start, screens), then depression/anxiety, iron deficiency (menstruating girls, vegetarians), infection (EBV/infectious mononucleosis, COVID-19, post-viral fatigue), hypothyroidism, coeliac disease, diabetes, anaemia, vitamin D/B12 deficiency, obstructive sleep apnoea (obesity, snoring), eating disorders, substance use, medication, chronic fatigue syndrome/ME (≥ 3–6 months, post-exertional malaise), and chronic illness. Initial tests: FBC, ferritin, TSH, glucose/HbA1c, CRP, coeliac screen, EBV, U&E; further tests guided by clinical picture. Treat sleep hygiene (regular schedule, no screens 1 h before bed, caffeine reduction), mental health, and graded activity.

### 5.2 Headache and migraine

Migraine and tension-type headaches are common; red flags as in chapter 59. Keep a headache diary; identify triggers (sleep, skipping meals, dehydration, stress, caffeine, menstrual cycle); medication overuse headache (> 2–3 days/week of analgesics). Treatment: simple analgesia early (ibuprofen, paracetamol), triptans (rizatriptan, sumatriptan nasal, zolmitriptan) for moderate–severe, prophylaxis (propranolol, topiramate, amitriptyline) if frequent and disabling; avoid estrogen-containing contraceptives in migraine with aura.

### 5.3 Syncope and chest pain

*Syncope* is usually vasovagal or orthostatic (trigger, prodrome, rapid recovery), but exertional syncope, palpitations preceding syncope, family history of sudden death, or abnormal ECG warrant evaluation for **hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, long QT syndrome, Brugada, catecholaminergic polymorphic VT, WPW, anomalous coronary artery, myocarditis**. ECG for every adolescent with syncope; echocardiogram and cardiology if red flags. *Chest pain*: musculoskeletal (costochondritis), pleuritic (pneumonia, pneumothorax: tall thin young male), asthma, GORD, anxiety/hyperventilation; cardiac causes are rare but include pericarditis, myocarditis, HCM, coronary anomalies, arrhythmia, Marfan aortic dissection; cocaine use. Check ECG and, if indicated, troponin and echo.

### 5.4 Abdominal pain and gastrointestinal complaints

Constipation, functional abdominal pain/IBS, gastroenteritis, GORD, coeliac disease, IBD (weight loss, bloody diarrhoea, nocturnal symptoms, perianal disease, growth failure, delayed puberty, mouth ulcers; faecal calprotectin, CRP, FBC, albumin), lactose intolerance, peptic ulcer (H. pylori), appendicitis, mesenteric adenitis, gynaecological causes, UTI, eating disorders (abdominal pain, early satiety, constipation, bloating), cyclical vomiting, abdominal migraine. Ask about school avoidance, mood and stressors; offer reassurance with explanation; avoid excessive investigations without alarm features (weight loss, blood, nocturnal pain, fever, family history of IBD).

### 5.5 Acne

Mainstay in adolescence; see chapter 54. Early treatment prevents scarring and psychological harm. Screen for depression, body-image issues and isotretinoin counselling (pregnancy prevention and mood review). Consider PCOS and CAH in girls with severe acne, hirsutism and irregular menses.

### 5.6 Musculoskeletal: scoliosis, growing pains, sports injuries

- **Adolescent idiopathic scoliosis**: lateral curve with rotation, 2–3% of adolescents, mostly girls aged 10–15; asymmetry of shoulders/waist, rib hump on forward-bend test; X-ray for Cobb angle (≥ 10°); bracing for 25–40° in skeletally immature, surgery > 45–50°. Atypical features (painful, left thoracic curve, rapidly progressive, neurological signs, short stature, café-au-lait spots) need MRI.
- **Overuse injuries and apophysitis**: Osgood–Schlatter (tibial tuberosity, boys 10–15), Sever disease (heel), Sinding-Larsen–Johansson, medial epicondylar apophysitis (Little League elbow), osteochondritis dissecans, stress fractures (female athlete triad, RED-S), patellofemoral pain syndrome, ankle sprains (Ottawa rules), anterior cruciate ligament tear. **Slipped capital femoral epiphysis** (see chapter 59). **Spondylolysis/spondylolisthesis** (low back pain in extension, gymnasts, fast bowlers): oblique X-ray/MRI. **Back pain with red flags** (fever, night pain, neurological deficit, trauma, weight loss): MRI.
- **Hypermobility** (Beighton score ≥ 5-6; Ehlers–Danlos spectrum disorders; Marfan features: tall, arachnodactyly, pectus, high palate, lens dislocation; check echocardiogram).
- **Concussion**: stepwise return to play; no same-day return; see chapter 59 for head injury rules. Pre-participation screening: history of exertional symptoms and family history; ECG per local policy.
- **Female athlete**: RED-S; ensure adequate energy and bone health.

### 5.7 Obesity and nutrition

BMI-for-age ≥ 95th centile (or ≥ 2 SD WHO): screen for complications (blood pressure, lipids, glucose/HbA1c, ALT/NAFLD, sleep apnoea, PCOS, SCFE, depression, binge eating). Management: family-based behavioural intervention; pharmacotherapy (GLP-1 receptor agonists, e.g., liraglutide, semaglutide, in ≥ 12 years where licensed) and bariatric surgery in selected severe cases. Avoid weight stigma and focus on health behaviours.

### 5.8 Gender identity and sexual orientation

Many adolescents explore identity. Provide a safe, non-judgemental environment; use affirming language; assess mental health (higher rates of depression, self-harm and suicide in sexual and gender minority youth, largely due to stigma and rejection); support families; ensure access to appropriate multidisciplinary gender services where medical interventions (puberty blockers, hormones) are considered, following current evidence and national guidance. Screen for sexual health needs without assumption.

## 6. Mental health

### 6.1 Depression and anxiety

Prevalence rises sharply in mid-adolescence, particularly in girls. Presentation: irritability, withdrawal, school refusal, somatic complaints (headache, abdominal pain, fatigue), changes in sleep and appetite, drop in grades, substance use, risky behaviours, self-harm. Use PHQ-A and HEEADSSS; always assess suicide risk (see below). Treatment: psychoeducation, family involvement, school liaison; **psychological therapy first-line** for mild–moderate (CBT, interpersonal therapy, family therapy); **fluoxetine** (± CBT) for moderate–severe depression (first-line SSRI in adolescents; monitor closely in first weeks for activation and suicidal thoughts; sertraline/escitalopram as alternatives); avoid tricyclics and venlafaxine as first-line. Anxiety: CBT with exposure; SSRI if severe. Screen for bullying (including cyberbullying), abuse, learning disability, ADHD, autism, substance use, medical contributors (thyroid, anaemia, sleep apnoea).

### 6.2 Self-harm and suicide

Non-suicidal self-injury (cutting, burning, hitting) affects 10–20% of adolescents; suicide is a leading cause of death in this age group, and attempts are far more frequent in girls but deaths more frequent in boys (more lethal means). Risk factors: depression, previous attempts, family history, abuse, bullying, substance use, LGBTQ+ stigma, access to means, social contagion, recent loss or conflict, impulsivity, academic pressure.

Ask directly using validated tools (ASQ; C-SSRS). **Assess**: ideation, intent, plan, means, previous attempts, protective factors, current stressors, mental state, substance use. Use a **collaborative safety plan** with the young person and parents, remove or lock away means (medications, firearms, ligatures), arrange rapid follow-up (within 24–72 hours after emergency visit), involve child and adolescent mental health services (CAMHS), treat the underlying disorder. Overdose (paracetamol particularly) requires medical treatment regardless of stated intent (see chapter 56, case 5). Do not use "no-harm contracts". Provide crisis line information. Parents need guidance on communication, safety at home and support.

### 6.3 Eating disorders

Anorexia nervosa peaks at 14–18 years; bulimia and binge-eating later. Early detection saves lives; boys, athletes and diabetic patients (insulin omission) are often missed. Warning signs: weight loss or failure to gain, amenorrhoea, food rituals, excessive exercise, body checking, social withdrawal, cold intolerance, dizziness, constipation, lanugo. Assessment: weight/BMI (adolescents: % of median BMI), orthostatic pulse and BP, temperature, ECG, electrolytes (K, phosphate, Mg), glucose, FBC, LFT, calcium, vitamin D, DEXA when prolonged. Admission criteria include HR < 50 (day) / < 45 (night), SBP < 90, orthostatic changes, K < 3.0, phosphate low, BMI < 75% median, acute food refusal, syncope, arrhythmia/prolonged QTc, failed outpatient treatment. **Family-based treatment (FBT/Maudsley)** is first-line for adolescent anorexia nervosa; refeeding with monitoring of phosphate, K, Mg, thiamine. See chapter 56.

### 6.4 ADHD, autism, learning difficulties, conduct problems

Underdiagnosed, especially in girls; commonly present with school difficulties, anxiety, emotional dysregulation, or burnout. Assess with collateral from school; treat ADHD with stimulants/atomoxetine plus behavioural support; screen for comorbid anxiety, depression, substance use. Conduct disorder: parent training, family therapy, multisystemic therapy; look for trauma, abuse, learning disability.

### 6.5 Psychosis

Typical onset late adolescence; prodrome of withdrawal, falling grades, odd beliefs, perceptual disturbance, sleep change. Cannabis use (especially high-potency) is a risk factor. Early intervention services improve outcomes; exclude organic causes including autoimmune encephalitis, drugs, and epilepsy (see chapter 56).

### 6.6 Trauma, bullying and online harm

Ask about bullying, cyberbullying, sexting and non-consensual image sharing, grooming, gambling, gaming addiction, pornography exposure, sextortion. Intervene by validating, ensuring safety, involving school and family, reporting crimes, and offering psychological support.

## 7. Substance use

### 7.1 Epidemiology and assessment

Alcohol, tobacco/nicotine (including vaping), and cannabis are the most commonly used substances; others: stimulants, MDMA, ketamine, opioids, benzodiazepines, inhalants, anabolic steroids, energy drinks, misuse of prescription drugs (stimulants, opioids). Early onset (<15 years) predicts dependence. Use **CRAFFT**: two or more "yes" = high risk; ask about driving, use alone, forgetting, family concern, trouble.

Classify use: abstinent, experimental, regular, problematic, dependent. Look for consequences: school, mood, relationships, legal issues, injury, sexual risk.

### 7.2 Management

- **Brief intervention and motivational interviewing** (chapter 56); offer harm reduction (no driving, never mix substances, hydration, avoid use alone, naloxone for opioids); address coexisting mental illness; family involvement; school-based programmes.
- **Tobacco/vaping**: counsel, nicotine replacement for dependence (adolescents ≥ 12–16 years per local guidance); behavioural support.
- **Intoxication and overdose**: assess airway, breathing, circulation, glucose; consider co-ingestion; toxicology; psychosocial assessment before discharge; observation after GHB, synthetic cannabinoids, etc.
- **Cannabis**: risk of dependence, cognitive effects, psychosis in vulnerable individuals; cannabis hyperemesis.
- **Alcohol**: binge drinking, injuries, sexual assault risk, blackouts, alcohol poisoning (hypoglycaemia, hypothermia, respiratory depression).

## 8. Sexual and reproductive health

### 8.1 Taking a sexual history (the "5 Ps")

**P**artners (number, sex, ages, consent), **P**ractices (types of sex: oral, vaginal, anal), **P**rotection from STIs (condoms, PrEP), **P**ast history of STIs, **P**regnancy prevention (contraception). Add: age of first sex, any coercion or pressure, exchange of sex for money/goods, online partners, power imbalance (older partner), alcohol/drug use during sex, HPV vaccination. Normalise questions; use neutral language; assure confidentiality.

### 8.2 Contraception

Offer **long-acting reversible contraception (LARC)**—implant and levonorgestrel IUD—as first-line options for adolescents (most effective, safe, reversible, high continuation); combined oral contraceptive (COC), patch, ring, progestogen-only pill, DMPA injection (reduces bone density, weight gain: consider counselling), condoms (for STI protection, always recommended in addition). Check medical eligibility (WHO/CDC MEC): migraine with aura, hypertension, VTE history/thrombophilia, SLE with antiphospholipid antibodies, active liver disease, breast cancer; interactions (enzyme inducers). **Emergency contraception**: ulipristal 30 mg (up to 120 h) or levonorgestrel 1.5 mg (up to 72 h; less effective if BMI ≥ 30), copper IUD (up to 5 days; most effective). Advance supply is allowed in many places. Recommend pregnancy test if delay.

### 8.3 Sexually transmitted infections and testing

- **Chlamydia** (most common, often asymptomatic): nucleic acid amplification test (NAAT) of first-void urine or vulvovaginal swab; annual screening of sexually active women under 25 (country dependent) and others at risk; treat with doxycycline 100 mg twice daily for 7 days (azithromycin 1 g alternative, especially in pregnancy), test of cure in pregnancy, partner notification, and retest at 3 months.
- **Gonorrhoea**: NAAT; ceftriaxone 1 g IM (resistance: culture and susceptibility); test oropharynx and rectum as indicated.
- **Syphilis, HIV, hepatitis B and C**: offer testing; HIV PrEP and post-exposure prophylaxis (within 72 hours); hepatitis B and HPV vaccination (ideally before sexual debut; 2 doses if started before 15 years).
- **Genital herpes, warts (HPV), trichomonas, bacterial vaginosis, Mycoplasma genitalium**.
- **Pelvic inflammatory disease** (lower abdominal pain, cervical motion tenderness, adnexal tenderness, discharge): treat empirically early (ceftriaxone IM + doxycycline + metronidazole for 14 days) to protect fertility. See chapter 53.
- **Partner notification** and expedited partner therapy where permitted; counselling on condom use, PrEP.

### 8.4 Pregnancy in adolescents

Pregnancy test promptly; discuss options (continuing pregnancy, abortion, adoption) non-directively with full information and respect for local law; early antenatal care, folic acid, screening for substance use, mental health, intimate partner violence; adolescent pregnancy carries higher risks of pre-eclampsia, preterm birth, anaemia, STIs, postpartum depression; support education and contraception postpartum (LARC). See chapter 57.

### 8.5 Sexual violence and exploitation

Screen for coercion, dating violence, trafficking, sexual exploitation, female genital mutilation (FGM) in at-risk communities. In suspected assault: ensure safety; offer forensic examination within 72–120 hours; consider post-exposure prophylaxis for HIV (within 72 hours), emergency contraception, STI prophylaxis/testing, hepatitis B vaccine, tetanus; psychological support; mandatory reporting depending on age and law. Avoid retraumatising repeated interviews; use trained professionals.

## 9. Chronic conditions and transition

- **Type 1 diabetes**: adolescents have the poorest glycaemic control (insulin resistance of puberty, psychosocial stress, adherence issues, diabulimia (insulin omission for weight control), DKA recurrent admissions). Use CGM/insulin pumps, psychological support, screening for complications (retinopathy, nephropathy from age 11-12), thyroid disease and coeliac disease.
- **Asthma**: peak fatal risk in adolescent age; adherence and smoking/vaping; teach spacer/inhaler technique, action plan, vaccination.
- **Epilepsy**: sleep deprivation, alcohol, and non-adherence trigger seizures; driving and safety counselling; valproate avoidance in girls; contraceptive interactions; JME.
- **Inflammatory bowel disease, cystic fibrosis, congenital heart disease, juvenile arthritis, sickle cell disease, cancer survivors, chronic kidney disease, transplant recipients**: non-adherence rises; support autonomy, mental health, school, and sexual and reproductive health (CF fertility, teratogenic drugs, fertility preservation after chemotherapy).
- **Transition to adult services**: begin planning by 12–14 years: gradually transfer responsibility, joint clinics, written transition plan, named coordinator, ensure first adult appointment attended; avoid "falling through the gap" which causes loss to follow-up and poor outcomes. Use readiness checklists (e.g., TRAQ).

## 10. Consent, confidentiality and safeguarding

- **Capacity and competence**: adolescents who can understand, retain, weigh and communicate (chapter 56) a decision may consent to treatment (Gillick competence in the UK; "mature minor" in many jurisdictions; statutory age thresholds elsewhere, such as 16 in England for medical treatment, and 14–18 depending on country/state for specific services). Refusal of life-saving treatment by a minor may be overridden by parents or courts. Assess capacity decision by decision.
- **Confidentiality**: the default; limits when there is risk of serious harm to self or others, abuse, or legal duty to report. Involve the adolescent in decisions on disclosure; encourage them to involve parents/carers where safe.
- **Safeguarding**: abuse, neglect, sexual exploitation, trafficking, radicalisation, forced marriage, FGM, domestic violence, online abuse. Follow local child protection procedures, document and consult a senior colleague. Recognise vulnerable groups: looked-after children, homeless youth, refugees/unaccompanied minors, those with disability, LGBTQ+ youth, those in custody.
- **Parents**: acknowledge their worries, provide information and boundaries, support parenting; help them to allow the young person a degree of privacy.

## Worked cases

### Case 1: "She's just tired all the time"

**Presentation.** Hannah, 15, comes with her mother for fatigue, headaches and stomach aches for 3 months, missing school. Mother is worried she has anaemia or glandular fever. Hannah looks pale, thin, quiet. BMI 17.8 (was 20.5 a year ago). Pulse 48, BP 90/55, cold hands, lanugo. Mother says Hannah "eats the same as the rest of us".

**Reasoning.** Fatigue plus weight loss, bradycardia, cool extremities and lanugo in an adolescent girl: eating disorder (anorexia nervosa) until proven otherwise; also consider hyperthyroidism, coeliac disease, IBD, diabetes, depression, malignancy.

**Action.** Speak with Hannah alone; HEEADSSS: she exercises two hours daily, avoids lunch, fears weight gain, stopped periods 4 months ago, feels low, no suicidal thoughts. Investigations: ECG (QTc 470 ms, sinus bradycardia), U&E (K 3.2), phosphate low-normal, glucose 3.4 mmol/L, FBC, TSH, coeliac serology, calcium, vitamin D, LFT, pregnancy test. Admission criteria met (HR 48, hypokalaemia, hypoglycaemia): urgent medical stabilisation with careful refeeding (monitor phosphate, K, Mg; thiamine), then family-based treatment via CAMHS. Explain diagnosis to Hannah and her parents without blame.

**Lessons.** See the adolescent alone; fatigue and non-specific somatic symptoms may hide an eating disorder; look for physiological instability.

### Case 2: A teenager's cough

**Presentation.** Ethan, 16, tall and thin, sudden left-sided pleuritic chest pain and breathlessness while sitting at his desk. HR 96, SpO₂ 96%, BP normal, reduced breath sounds on the left. He smokes cannabis and vapes.

**Reasoning.** Primary spontaneous pneumothorax (tall thin young male, smoker). Other causes: Marfan syndrome, asthma, cystic fibrosis, lung disease.

**Action.** Chest X-ray: 3 cm apical pneumothorax. Stable: oxygen; conservative management or needle aspiration/small-bore drain per size and symptoms; advise to stop smoking/vaping and cannabis; avoid flying and diving; counsel on recurrence (30% in 1–2 years; consider VATS pleurodesis after the second episode). Check for Marfan features. Offer brief intervention for substance use.

**Lessons.** Pleuritic chest pain in a tall thin teenage boy: pneumothorax. Use the consultation to address smoking.

### Case 3: "I just want to sleep and not wake up"

**Presentation.** Maya, 14, referred after her teacher noticed cuts on her arms. She admits to cutting weekly for months, feels worthless since being bullied on social media, and last night thought about taking her mother's tablets. She has no plan today. PHQ-A 19; ASQ positive.

**Reasoning.** Depression with self-harm and suicidal ideation; high risk given access to means (tablets), recent ideation, cyberbullying.

**Action.** See Maya alone; explain confidentiality limits; assess intent: no current plan, but ideation and access to medication. Involve parents with Maya's agreement (risk requires it); safety plan (warning signs, coping strategies, people, crisis line, means restriction: lock all medicines, knives); same-week CAMHS assessment (urgent if risk increases); treat depression with CBT ± fluoxetine; liaise with school about bullying and online reporting; wound care and tetanus; follow-up within 48–72 hours. If she had a plan and intent: emergency psychiatric assessment/admission.

**Lessons.** Ask directly about suicide; involve family while respecting the young person; means restriction saves lives.

### Case 4: Heavy periods since menarche

**Presentation.** Sofia, 13, menarche at 11, soaks a pad every 1–2 hours for 8 days each cycle, with clots; Hb 82 g/L, ferritin 6 µg/L, nosebleeds in childhood, mother had heavy periods and a hysterectomy at 35. Examination: pale, no bruising; normal Tanner stage 3.

**Reasoning.** Heavy menstrual bleeding with anaemia since menarche and family history: bleeding disorder (von Willebrand disease) likely; also anovulatory bleeding, thrombocytopenia, thyroid disease, pregnancy (if sexually active).

**Action.** Pregnancy test negative; FBC, ferritin, TSH, coagulation screen, VWF antigen/activity/factor VIII (timed, since levels vary with oestrogen, stress); haematology referral. Treat: iron (IV if intolerance or severe), tranexamic acid during menses, hormonal suppression (combined pill or levonorgestrel IUD/progestin), DDAVP for VWD if responsive; avoid NSAIDs/aspirin. Confirm VWD type 1. Educate on bleeding action plan and future surgery/delivery planning.

**Lessons.** Heavy bleeding from menarche is a bleeding disorder until shown otherwise; one in five adolescents presenting with severe bleeding has a coagulopathy.

### Case 5: Collapse in a footballer

**Presentation.** Marco, 17, collapsed during football practice for 20 seconds with spontaneous recovery; earlier he had palpitations on exertion and felt faint twice. His uncle died suddenly at 32. Examination: ejection systolic murmur increasing on Valsalva. ECG: left ventricular hypertrophy with deep T-wave inversions.

**Reasoning.** Exertional syncope with family history of sudden death and murmur: hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM) until proven otherwise, a leading cause of sudden death in young athletes.

**Action.** Stop sport immediately; echocardiogram (septal thickness 24 mm), cardiac MRI, Holter, exercise test; cardiology/inherited cardiac conditions clinic; genetic testing and family screening; risk stratification for ICD; counsel on avoidance of competitive/intense exercise per shared decision-making guidelines.

**Lessons.** Exertional syncope is cardiac until proven otherwise. A normal resting examination does not exclude structural heart disease.

### Case 6: Unprotected sex and a lower abdominal pain

**Presentation.** Chloe, 17, with 5 days of lower abdominal pain, vaginal discharge, intermenstrual bleeding and dyspareunia. Unprotected sex with a new partner 3 weeks ago. Temperature 38.0 °C, tenderness in lower abdomen, cervical motion tenderness, bilateral adnexal tenderness. Urine pregnancy negative. She asks that her parents not be told.

**Reasoning.** Pelvic inflammatory disease (PID) (chlamydia/gonorrhoea ± anaerobes); differential: ectopic pregnancy (excluded by test), appendicitis, ovarian torsion, UTI.

**Action.** Explain confidentiality; take cervical/vaginal swabs for NAAT (chlamydia, gonorrhoea, Mycoplasma genitalium), HIV, syphilis and hepatitis B tests; treat empirically same day (ceftriaxone 1 g IM, doxycycline 100 mg twice daily and metronidazole 400 mg twice daily for 14 days; follow national guideline); pain relief; advise abstinence until partner treated; partner notification (contact tracing); follow-up at 72 hours (admit if no improvement, severe illness, or tubo-ovarian abscess on ultrasound); discuss condoms, LARC, HPV and hepatitis B vaccination, rescreen at 3 months. Assess for coercion or exploitation (age of partner).

**Lessons.** Treat PID early to preserve fertility; assess coercion; confidential care enables access.

## Common pitfalls

1. **Speaking only to the parent**, or never seeing the adolescent alone.
2. **Promising unconditional confidentiality**; conversely, breaking confidentiality without explanation.
3. **Overlooking eating disorders** in boys, athletes, normal-weight patients and diabetics.
4. **Missing depression and suicide risk** when the presentation is somatic or behavioural.
5. **Relying on a normal BMI** to dismiss an eating disorder (physiological instability can occur at any weight).
6. **Not asking about sexual activity, substance use, safety and abuse** for fear of offending.
7. **Failing to test for pregnancy** in any girl with abdominal pain, amenorrhoea or before prescribing/imaging.
8. **Dismissing heavy periods** as normal; missing bleeding disorders and iron deficiency.
9. **Calling exertional syncope "faint"** without ECG and cardiac evaluation.
10. **Misdiagnosing hip pathology** (SCFE) as knee pain; ignoring back-pain red flags.
11. **Prescribing isotretinoin, valproate or teratogens** without contraception planning.
12. **Using antidepressants without close follow-up** or without psychotherapy.
13. **Missing grooming, exploitation, trafficking, FGM or dating violence.**
14. **Neglecting transition** in young people with chronic conditions.
15. **Shaming or lecturing** instead of engaging and collaborating.

---

## Key points

- See the adolescent alone, explain confidentiality and its limits, and use HEEADSSS to find the problem behind the presenting complaint.
- Assess puberty with Tanner staging and growth charts; know the limits for delayed and precocious puberty.
- Think of eating disorders, depression, substance use, abuse, and pregnancy in unexplained somatic symptoms.
- Ask directly about suicide and self-harm; build a safety plan; restrict means; arrange rapid follow-up.
- Menstrual problems are common; heavy bleeding from menarche warrants bleeding disorder screening; always test for pregnancy.
- Offer LARC; test for STIs routinely; treat PID early; give emergency contraception.
- Exertional syncope, family history of sudden death, or abnormal ECG require cardiac work-up before returning to sport.
- Adolescents with chronic illness need psychosocial support and structured transition.
- Know your local laws on consent, confidentiality and mandatory reporting.

7. A 17-year-old girl has severe lower abdominal pain, fever and adnexal tenderness. What is the diagnosis and treatment?
8. When can an adolescent consent to treatment without parental permission, and when may confidentiality be breached?
9. Which findings in an adolescent with syncope require urgent cardiac evaluation?
10. What are the admission criteria for anorexia nervosa in an adolescent?

**Answers**

1. Home, Education/Employment, Eating/Exercise, Activities/peers, Drugs, Sexuality, Suicide/depression/Safety. It is a structured psychosocial interview to identify risk and protective factors in a systematic, non-judgemental way.
2. Primary amenorrhoea with delayed puberty: growth chart, Tanner, family history, BMI, features of Turner syndrome, anosmia (Kallmann), chronic disease and eating disorder; bone age, FSH, LH, oestradiol, prolactin, TSH, IGF-1, coeliac serology, karyotype; pelvic ultrasound; high FSH suggests gonadal failure (Turner); low FSH suggests constitutional delay or hypogonadotropic hypogonadism.
3. Any four: morning vomiting/nocturnal waking; progressively worsening; occipital location; neurological signs (ataxia, diplopia, weakness); papilloedema; personality or school performance change; endocrine signs; head trauma; fever and neck stiffness; thunderclap onset; positional headache; seizures.
4. Pubertal gynaecomastia is common; reassure after examination (breast tissue, testes, signs of Klinefelter, liver disease, drug history: cannabis, anabolic steroids, antipsychotics); investigate if rapid growth, hard/eccentric lump, abnormal testes, or Tanner not consistent; review in 6 months; most resolve in 1–2 years.
5. Ask directly about ideation, intent, plan, means, prior attempts, protective factors; assess depression, substance use, bullying, family; develop a collaborative safety plan (warning signs, coping strategies, contacts, professionals, means restriction); involve parents; arrange rapid follow-up and CAMHS; treat underlying disorder; admission if high risk.
6. LARC (implant, levonorgestrel IUD): most effective, safe, reversible, independent of adherence. Combined contraception contraindications: migraine with aura, history of VTE or thrombophilia, uncontrolled hypertension, SLE with antiphospholipid antibodies, smoking over 35, breast cancer, active liver disease (any two).
7. Pelvic inflammatory disease; empirical antibiotic regimen (e.g., ceftriaxone IM + doxycycline + metronidazole for 14 days), swabs and tests, pregnancy test, partner treatment, admission if severe or abscess.
8. When competent (understands, retains, weighs, communicates) or per statute (e.g., contraception, STI services); confidentiality may be breached when there is serious risk of harm to self or others, abuse or legal duty to report; inform the adolescent where possible.
9. Exertional syncope, palpitations before syncope, family history of sudden death or cardiomyopathy/channelopathy, abnormal ECG (long QT, pre-excitation, Brugada, T-wave inversion, LVH), murmur increasing on standing/Valsalva, syncope while supine or during emotional/auditory trigger.
10. HR < 50 awake (< 45 asleep), SBP < 90, orthostatic changes, K < 3.0 or low phosphate/Mg, hypoglycaemia, QTc prolongation or arrhythmia, BMI < 75% median, acute food refusal, syncope, rapid weight loss, failure of outpatient treatment, or acute psychiatric risk.
