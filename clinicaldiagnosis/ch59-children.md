# Chapter 59. Children

## Learning objectives

After studying this chapter you should be able to:

1. Adapt history-taking and examination to the child's age, using the parent or carer, observation and play.
2. Assess growth and development, recognise delay and red flags, and use screening tools.
3. Apply the paediatric assessment triangle, age-specific vital signs and early warning scores to recognise the seriously ill child.
4. Use the WHO IMCI approach and the traffic-light system to classify fever, respiratory illness, diarrhoea and dehydration, malnutrition and anaemia in children, especially in low-resource settings.
5. Diagnose and manage common paediatric presentations: fever, respiratory infections (bronchiolitis, croup, pneumonia, asthma), gastroenteritis and dehydration, UTI, abdominal pain, rashes, limping, headache, seizures.
6. Recognise paediatric emergencies: meningitis/sepsis, anaphylaxis, diabetic ketoacidosis, status epilepticus, intussusception, foreign body, and non-accidental injury.
7. Calculate fluids and drug doses by weight safely and recognise common paediatric medication errors.
8. Communicate with children and families, assess capacity and consent in minors, and safeguard.

> **Note on thresholds and doses.** Paediatric reference values change with age and doses depend on weight. Use your national paediatric formulary (e.g., BNFc, Harriet Lane, WHO Pocket Book of Hospital Care for Children) and local protocols for every prescription. Figures here are illustrative.

## 1. Children are not small adults

Children differ in physiology (higher metabolic rate, larger surface area-to-weight, faster respiratory rate, compensating well until sudden collapse), in disease spectrum (viral infections, congenital and developmental conditions), in communication (history from a third party, limited self-report), and in vulnerability (dependence, safeguarding). They deteriorate quickly and recover quickly. Parents' concern is a valuable clinical sign: "he is not himself" deserves respect.

### 1.1 Approach to the consultation

- **Greet the child first**, at their level; introduce yourself; use their name; involve them in the conversation.
- **Observe before touching**: alertness, interaction, colour, work of breathing, posture, hydration, activity, cry. A well child plays, smiles, makes eye contact, and is hard to examine; a quiet, listless child is the concerning one.
- **History**: presenting complaint with parental account, onset and course, feeding and fluid intake, wet nappies/urine output, stools, vomiting, fever, behaviour, sleep, contacts, immunisations, birth history, development, past illnesses, medications, allergies, family history, social circumstances (who lives at home, nursery or school attendance), safeguarding concerns. Ask the child directly if old enough; with adolescents see them alone for part of the consultation (chapter 60).
- **Examination order**: start with the least invasive (observation, counting respiratory rate, heart auscultation while the child is calm), leave ears, throat and anything painful to the end. Use distraction, toys, parent's lap. Examine the whole child, undressed (with parental presence and respect for dignity), including genitalia in infants, hips, femoral pulses, skin, and fontanelle.
- **Weigh and measure** every child: weight, height/length, head circumference (< 2–3 years) and plot; for drug dosing use actual weight or length-based tape (Broselow).

### 1.2 Age-specific vital signs (awake, approximate normal ranges)

| Age | Heart rate (bpm) | Respiratory rate (/min) | Systolic BP lower limit (mmHg) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Newborn–3 months | 110–160 | 35–55 | 60 |
| 3–12 months | 100–150 | 30–45 | 70 |
| 1–2 years | 90–140 | 25–35 | 70 + 2 × age (years) |
| 3–5 years | 80–120 | 20–30 | 70 + 2 × age |
| 6–11 years | 70–110 | 18–25 | 70 + 2 × age |
| ≥ 12 years | 60–100 | 12–20 | 90 |

(Use the charts of your hospital's paediatric early warning score. Values in sleep are lower; fever raises heart rate by about 10 bpm per °C.) **Hypotension is a late sign of shock in children**: they maintain pressure by tachycardia and vasoconstriction until sudden decompensation. Capillary refill > 2 seconds (central), cool peripheries, tachycardia, altered consciousness, reduced urine output are the early signs.

### 1.3 The paediatric assessment triangle (PAT)

A 30-second visual assessment from the doorway:

1. **Appearance** (TICLS): Tone, Interactiveness, Consolability, Look/gaze, Speech/cry.
2. **Work of breathing**: abnormal sounds (stridor, grunting, wheeze), positioning (tripod, sniffing), retractions, nasal flaring, head bobbing.
3. **Circulation to skin**: pallor, mottling, cyanosis.

An abnormal appearance suggests central nervous system or global hypoxia/hypoperfusion; abnormal work of breathing indicates respiratory problem; abnormal skin suggests circulatory problem. Any two abnormal sides = critical child: proceed to ABCDE (chapter 37) and call for help.

## 2. Growth and development

### 2.1 Growth

Use WHO growth standards (0–5 years) and national charts; plot weight, length/height, head circumference and BMI; interpret trajectory, not a single point. Calculate mid-parental height.

- **Weight loss or faltering growth**: crossing two centile lines downward, below the 0.4th centile (UK) or < −2 z-score. Causes: inadequate intake (most), malabsorption (coeliac disease, cystic fibrosis, cow's milk protein allergy), chronic disease (congenital heart disease, renal disease, hypothyroidism, diabetes, chronic infection including HIV and TB), neglect.
- **Short stature**: height < 2nd centile or growth velocity < 25th centile. Familial short stature and constitutional delay are most common; pathological: growth hormone deficiency, hypothyroidism, Turner syndrome, coeliac disease, chronic disease, Cushing, skeletal dysplasias, malnutrition. Investigate with bone age, TSH, coeliac serology, FBC, ESR, U&E, bicarbonate, karyotype in girls.
- **Obesity**: BMI > 95th centile (or > 2 z-scores WHO > 5y); look for endocrine causes if short stature, developmental delay, dysmorphism or hyperphagia from infancy (Prader–Willi).
- **Microcephaly/macrocephaly**: measure head circumference, check parents, examine neurologically; macrocephaly with bulging fontanelle or sunsetting eyes = hydrocephalus.

### 2.2 Developmental milestones (approximate median ages, with "red-flag" limits)

| Domain | Milestone (typical) | Red flag (refer if not by) |
|---|---|---|
| **Gross motor** | Head control 3 months; sits unsupported 6–7 months; crawls 9 months; pulls to stand 9–10 months; walks independently 12–13 months; runs 18 months–2 years; jumps 2–3 years | No head control 4 months; not sitting 9–10 months; not walking 18 months; hand preference < 12 months |
| **Fine motor and vision** | Follows face 6 weeks; reaches and grasps 4–5 months; transfers 6 months; pincer grip 9–12 months; scribbles 15 months; tower of 3 blocks 18 months; copies circle 3 years | No visual fixation 6–8 weeks; squint persistent after 3 months; fisting beyond 4 months |
| **Language and hearing** | Coos 2–3 months; laughs 4 months; babbles (mama, dada) 6–8 months; first words 12 months; 2-word phrases 18–24 months; 3-word sentences 3 years; 50% intelligible by 2, 75% by 3 | No babble by 9–12 months; no words by 16–18 months; no 2-word phrases by 24 months; any loss of language or social skill at any age |
| **Social and emotional** | Social smile 6 weeks; stranger anxiety 8–9 months; waves bye-bye 10–12 months; points to show 12–14 months; parallel play 2 years; cooperative play 4 years | No social smile 3 months; no pointing/eye contact/response to name by 12 months; lack of pretend play by 18–24 months |

Correct for prematurity until 2 years (chronological age minus weeks premature). **Regression** (loss of previously acquired skills) is always abnormal and requires urgent evaluation (neurodegenerative, metabolic, epileptic encephalopathy, autism spectrum with regression, Rett syndrome, subacute sclerosing panencephalitis).

**Screening tools**: ASQ-3, Denver II, Bayley, PEDS, M-CHAT-R/F (autism screen at 16–30 months), Vanderbilt (ADHD). Recognise global developmental delay (≥ 2 domains): investigate with hearing and vision tests, thyroid function, FBC/ferritin, genetic testing (chromosomal microarray, fragile X), metabolic screen, MRI as indicated; refer to developmental paediatrics early. Always test **hearing** in speech delay.

**Cerebral palsy**: non-progressive motor disorder from early brain injury; early signs: hand preference before 12 months, persistent primitive reflexes, abnormal tone (hypo- then hypertonia), asymmetric movements, toe walking, delayed motor milestones; MRI brain; early physiotherapy.

**Autism spectrum disorder**: reduced social communication (eye contact, joint attention, response to name, pointing), repetitive behaviours, restricted interests, sensory sensitivity; screen, refer for multidisciplinary assessment, evaluate hearing; do not wait for diagnosis to begin therapy.

## 3. WHO IMCI and care in low-resource settings

The **Integrated Management of Childhood Illness** classifies children aged 2 months to 5 years using simple signs, with action color-coded **pink (urgent referral)**, **yellow (treat)** and **green (home care)**. Start by checking for **general danger signs**: unable to drink or breastfeed, vomits everything, convulsions (now or in this illness), lethargic or unconscious. Then ask about cough or difficult breathing, diarrhoea, fever, ear problem, malnutrition and anaemia, HIV, and immunisation status.

| Main symptom | Key signs | Classification and action |
|---|---|---|
| **Cough or difficult breathing** | Count breaths for a full minute in a calm child; fast breathing: ≥ 50/min (2–11 months), ≥ 40/min (1–5 years); chest indrawing; stridor in calm child; oxygen saturation < 90% | Severe pneumonia/very severe disease (danger sign, stridor, SpO₂ < 90%): oxygen, injectable antibiotic, refer. Pneumonia (fast breathing alone): oral amoxicillin 3–5 days. Cough/cold: home care, soothe, return signs |
| **Diarrhoea** | Duration; blood in stool; dehydration signs: lethargic or unconscious, sunken eyes, unable to drink or drinks poorly, skin pinch goes back very slowly (≥ 2 s) | **Severe dehydration** (2 of: lethargic, sunken eyes, unable to drink, very slow skin pinch): IV fluids (Plan C). **Some dehydration** (2 of: restless/irritable, sunken eyes, drinks eagerly, slow skin pinch): ORS (Plan B, 75 mL/kg over 4 h). **No dehydration**: Plan A (home fluids, zinc 10–20 mg daily for 10–14 days, continue feeding). Persistent (≥ 14 days) and dysentery need specific treatment. |
| **Fever** (malaria risk, measles) | Stiff neck, bulging fontanelle, rash, mouth ulcers, corneal clouding, runny nose, cough, ear pain; malaria rapid test or smear | Very severe febrile disease/meningitis: urgent referral and antibiotics (IM ceftriaxone), treat hypoglycaemia; malaria: artemisinin-based therapy; measles: vitamin A |
| **Ear problem** | Pus draining < 14 days, tender swelling behind ear | Mastoiditis: refer; acute ear infection: amoxicillin 5 days; chronic: dry wicking |
| **Malnutrition and anaemia** | Visible severe wasting, bilateral pitting oedema, MUAC < 115 mm (6–59 months), weight-for-height < −3 z-score; palmar pallor | Complicated severe acute malnutrition (SAM) (with oedema, danger signs, infection, no appetite): inpatient care. Uncomplicated SAM: RUTF (ready-to-use therapeutic food) at home. Moderate acute malnutrition (MUAC 115–124 mm): supplementary feeding. Severe pallor: refer; moderate pallor: iron. |
| **HIV** | Exposure, symptoms (oral thrush, chronic diarrhoea, parotid swelling, lymphadenopathy) | Test and treat |

**Severe acute malnutrition (SAM)** requires special care: hypoglycaemia, hypothermia and infection are the main killers; give 10% glucose or sugar-water; keep warm; give broad-spectrum antibiotics (amoxicillin); avoid IV fluids unless in shock (and then 15 mL/kg over 1 hour with close monitoring because of risk of cardiac failure); use ReSoMal (low-sodium ORS); feed with F-75, then F-100/RUTF; micronutrients; treat anaemia with iron only after stabilisation. Kwashiorkor: oedema, skin flaking, hair changes; marasmus: severe wasting.

**Vitamin A, zinc, ORS, and immunisation** are the cornerstones of preventive child health.

## 4. Fever in children

### 4.1 General principles

Fever is a physiological response; most febrile children have a viral self-limited infection. Goals: identify the minority with serious bacterial infection (SBI) — meningitis, bacteraemia/sepsis, pneumonia, UTI, bone and joint infection, Kawasaki disease — and avoid unnecessary tests and antibiotics.

**Measure** temperature with an electronic axillary thermometer (infants and children under 4–5), or tympanic/oral/rectal; fever = ≥ 38 °C. A child's ill appearance and trajectory matter more than the number. Fever response to antipyretics does not distinguish bacterial from viral infection.

### 4.2 Traffic-light system (NICE, children under 5)

| | **Green (low risk)** | **Amber (intermediate)** | **Red (high risk)** |
|---|---|---|---|
| Colour | Normal | Pallor reported by parent | Pale/mottled/ashen/blue |
| Activity | Responds normally, content/smiles, stays awake, strong normal cry | Not responding normally, no smile, wakes only with prolonged stimulation, decreased activity | No response, appears ill, doesn't wake or if roused doesn't stay awake, weak/high-pitched/continuous cry |
| Respiratory | Normal | Nasal flaring, tachypnoea (RR >50 at 6–12 months; >40 > 12 months), SpO₂ ≤ 95%, crackles | Grunting, tachypnoea RR > 60, moderate/severe chest indrawing |
| Hydration | Moist mucous membranes, normal skin and eyes | Dry mucous membrane, poor feeding in infants, CRT ≥ 3 s, reduced urine | Reduced skin turgor |
| Other | None | Fever ≥ 5 days, swelling of a limb/joint, non-weight-bearing, new lump > 2 cm, age 3–6 months with temperature ≥ 39 °C | Age < 3 months with temperature ≥ 38 °C, non-blanching rash, bulging fontanelle, neck stiffness, status epilepticus, focal neurological signs/seizures, bilious vomiting |

Green: manage at home with safety-net advice. Amber: safety-net with follow-up, or refer for observation depending on circumstances. Red: urgent referral/investigation/treatment, consider antibiotics.

### 4.3 Practical approach

1. Look for a **focus**: ears, throat, chest, urine, skin, joints, abdomen, meninges, CNS.
2. **Do not examine only the ear**: undress, check for rash, joint swelling, lymph nodes, hepatosplenomegaly, abdominal tenderness, genitalia, and perform neurological examination.
3. **No focus** in a child < 3 years with fever > 39 °C: consider urine testing (clean-catch, catheter, or suprapubic aspirate), and in the unvaccinated or very unwell: blood tests, blood culture, chest X-ray if respiratory signs or persistent fever and raised WCC.
4. **Fever ≥ 5 days**: consider Kawasaki disease (conjunctival injection without exudate, red cracked lips/strawberry tongue, cervical lymphadenopathy, rash, swelling/erythema of hands and feet, peeling later; echocardiography; IV immunoglobulin 2 g/kg and aspirin within 10 days to prevent coronary aneurysms), as well as UTI, pneumonia, osteomyelitis, EBV, juvenile idiopathic arthritis, inflammatory bowel disease, malignancy, abscess, TB. MIS-C: fever, multisystem inflammation after SARS-CoV-2 exposure.
5. **Antipyretics**: paracetamol (15 mg/kg every 4–6 hours, max 60–75 mg/kg/day) or ibuprofen (5–10 mg/kg every 6–8 hours) for comfort, not to prevent febrile seizures. Don't alternate routinely. Avoid aspirin (Reye syndrome) except in Kawasaki disease under specialist care. Avoid ibuprofen in dehydration, renal disease, chickenpox (severe skin infections).
6. **Safety-net**: written advice on warning signs and when to seek help (see chapter 73).
7. **Febrile seizures**: age 6 months–5 years, generalised, < 15 minutes, once in 24 hours; benign; after the first seizure, examine for source and meningitis signs; do not give antiepileptic prophylaxis. Complex features (focal, prolonged, repeated, neurological abnormality) require investigation; consider LP if meningeal signs, unvaccinated, or recent antibiotics.

### 4.4 Serious bacterial infection in children

- **Bacterial meningitis and meningococcal sepsis**: fever, irritability, drowsiness, vomiting, headache, photophobia, neck stiffness (often absent < 18 months), bulging fontanelle, seizures, non-blanching rash, cold hands and feet, leg pain, abnormal skin colour. Septic shock may precede meningitis. **Give IV/IM ceftriaxone 80 mg/kg (max 4 g) immediately** (in the community: benzylpenicillin IM), resuscitate, dexamethasone 0.15 mg/kg before or with the first antibiotic (not in septic shock or < 3 months in some guidelines; follow local), then LP when safe (contraindicated if shocked, GCS < 9/falling, focal signs, coagulopathy, raised ICP signs). Do not wait for LP. Notify public health, chemoprophylaxis for close contacts (ciprofloxacin or rifampicin), vaccination review.
- **Sepsis** in children: see chapter 39; paediatric sepsis screening; fluid boluses 10–20 mL/kg (in resource-limited settings with caution, e.g., FEAST trial showed harm from boluses in African children without hypotension), early antibiotics (within 1 hour), inotropes (adrenaline for cold shock, noradrenaline for warm shock).
- **Septic arthritis and osteomyelitis**: refusal to bear weight, limp, fever, pain on joint movement (septic joint holds the hip flexed, abducted, externally rotated), raised CRP/ESR; *S. aureus*, *Kingella kingae*, streptococci, Hib in unvaccinated. Kocher criteria for septic hip (non-weight-bearing, ESR ≥ 40, temperature > 38.5 °C, WBC > 12): 0–4 predicts probability. Aspiration/drainage and IV antibiotics. MRI/ultrasound.
- **Urinary tract infection**: see below.
- **Periorbital/orbital cellulitis, mastoiditis, epiglottitis (rare now), toxic shock syndrome, staphylococcal scalded skin syndrome, necrotising fasciitis.**

## 5. Respiratory illness in children

### 5.1 Upper respiratory conditions

- **Common cold**, **pharyngotonsillitis** (viral in most; GAS in 20–30% of 5–15-year-olds; use Centor/McIsaac or FeverPAIN; penicillin V for 10 days if confirmed), **acute otitis media** (chapter 55), **sinusitis**, **croup** (chapter 55), **epiglottitis**, **bacterial tracheitis** (toxic, "croup that doesn't respond"), **retropharyngeal abscess**.
- **Foreign body aspiration**: toddler with sudden choking, cough, stridor or wheeze; unilateral signs; may be silent for days or present as recurrent pneumonia. Back blows/chest thrusts in infants, abdominal thrusts in older children; bronchoscopy.

### 5.2 Bronchiolitis

Viral lower respiratory infection (RSV in most) in infants < 12 months (peak 2–6 months): 1–3 days of coryza and cough followed by tachypnoea, wheeze, fine inspiratory crackles, recession, feeding difficulty, apnoea in young babies. Risk factors for severe disease: prematurity, chronic lung disease, congenital heart disease, age < 3 months, immunodeficiency, Down syndrome.

*Management is supportive*: oxygen if SpO₂ < 90–92%, feeding support (NG feeding or IV fluids when intake < 50%), nasal suction, high-flow nasal cannula for moderate–severe disease, CPAP/ventilation for apnoea or fatigue. Don't routinely give bronchodilators, steroids, antibiotics, chest physiotherapy or hypertonic saline in ward patients; consider nirsevimab/RSV prophylaxis in high-risk infants and maternal RSV vaccine. Admit if: apnoea, SpO₂ < 92%, feeding < 50% of normal, severe respiratory distress, age < 3 months, parental concerns.

### 5.3 Pneumonia

Fever, cough, tachypnoea (best sign in children), chest indrawing, grunting, nasal flaring, focal crackles or bronchial breathing, hypoxia; abdominal pain may mimic appendicitis (lower lobe). Viral causes predominate in < 5 years; *S. pneumoniae* (commonest bacterial), *Mycoplasma pneumoniae* (school age: gradual onset, dry cough, wheeze), *S. aureus*, GAS, Hib (unvaccinated).

*Investigation*: chest X-ray is not needed for mild uncomplicated disease; do it if severe, hypoxic, complicated (effusion, empyema), or diagnostic doubt; blood culture in moderate–severe; pulse oximetry in every child with respiratory illness.

*Treatment*: oral amoxicillin (high dose, e.g., 40–90 mg/kg/day) for 3–5 days for non-severe pneumonia in children < 5 years; macrolide for atypical infection in school-age children or penicillin allergy; IV ampicillin/ceftriaxone for severe disease, plus oxygen. Complications: parapneumonic effusion/empyema (persistent fever, dull percussion, pleuritic pain; ultrasound, drainage), pneumatocele, pneumothorax.

### 5.4 Wheeze and asthma

- **Preschool wheeze**: viral-triggered; episodic (viral) vs multiple-trigger; inhaled bronchodilator (salbutamol via spacer), consider ICS or montelukast trial for frequent episodes. Atopy, parental asthma, eczema increase the chance of persistent asthma.
- **Asthma** in children ≥ 5 years: recurrent cough (especially at night/exercise), wheeze, chest tightness, breathlessness; diagnosis by history, response to treatment, spirometry (reversibility ≥ 12%), peak flow variability, FeNO. Management ladder (GINA): as-needed low-dose ICS-formoterol (≥ 12 y) or SABA plus regular ICS; step-up with LABA, LTRA; inhaler technique, spacer, written action plan, adherence, triggers, comorbidity (rhinitis, GORD, obesity).
- **Acute asthma** severity: moderate (SpO₂ ≥ 92%, PEF > 50%, speaks sentences); severe (SpO₂ < 92%, PEF 33–50%, can't complete sentences, RR > 30 (>5y) or >40 (2–5y), HR > 125/>140); life-threatening (silent chest, cyanosis, poor effort, exhaustion, confusion, SpO₂ < 92% with normal PaCO₂). Treat with oxygen, salbutamol (10 puffs via spacer or 2.5–5 mg nebulised every 20 minutes), ipratropium bromide, oral prednisolone 1–2 mg/kg (max 40 mg) or IV hydrocortisone, IV magnesium sulphate 40 mg/kg (max 2 g) for severe cases, IV salbutamol/aminophylline in ICU. Chest X-ray only if suspected pneumothorax or pneumonia.
- **Differential of chronic cough/wheeze in children**: cystic fibrosis, protracted bacterial bronchitis, foreign body, tracheomalacia/bronchomalacia, vascular ring, GORD with aspiration, primary ciliary dyskinesia, TB, post-infectious, habit (psychogenic) cough, immunodeficiency, congenital heart disease, vocal cord dysfunction.
- **Whooping cough (pertussis)**: paroxysmal cough with whoop (absent in infants), post-tussive vomiting, apnoea, lymphocytosis; PCR; macrolide; unvaccinated and young infants at risk: admit.
- **Cystic fibrosis**: persistent wet cough, recurrent chest infections, failure to thrive, steatorrhoea, nasal polyps, finger clubbing; sweat chloride test; newborn screening.

## 6. Gastrointestinal illness, dehydration and abdominal pain

### 6.1 Acute gastroenteritis and dehydration

Most commonly viral (rotavirus, norovirus, adenovirus); bacterial (Salmonella, Campylobacter, Shigella, *E. coli*) causes blood in stool and fever. **Assess dehydration clinically** (weight loss is the gold standard; typical categories: none < 3–5%, some 3–9%, severe > 9–10%):

- *Early signs*: thirst, restlessness/irritability, reduced urine, dry mucous membranes.
- *Later signs*: sunken eyes and fontanelle, reduced skin turgor, tachycardia, cool peripheries, prolonged CRT, deep (acidotic) breathing, lethargy, hypotension (late).
- Best predictors in children: prolonged capillary refill, abnormal skin turgor, abnormal respiratory pattern; no single sign is perfect. Use a dehydration score (Gorelick/Clinical Dehydration Scale).

*Management*:

1. **Oral rehydration solution (ORS)** (low-osmolarity: Na 75 mmol/L, glucose 75 mmol/L): 50 mL/kg over 4 hours for mild–moderate dehydration plus maintenance and ongoing losses (10 mL/kg per loose stool); small frequent sips, nasogastric if refusing but not shocked. Continue breastfeeding and normal diet early; avoid fruit juices and fizzy drinks.
2. **IV fluids** for shock or severe dehydration or failure of ORS: isotonic bolus 10–20 mL/kg (0.9% saline or balanced crystalloid), reassess, then rehydrate; use isotonic maintenance fluids with dextrose (e.g., 0.9% saline with 5% glucose) to reduce hyponatraemia. Check glucose, electrolytes.
3. **Ondansetron** (single dose 0.1–0.15 mg/kg) can reduce vomiting and facilitate oral rehydration.
4. **Zinc** in low- and middle-income countries (10–20 mg/day for 10–14 days).
5. **No antibiotics for most**; consider in toxic children, immunocompromise, *Shigella*, cholera, severe *Campylobacter*; do not give anti-motility drugs.
6. **Red flags**: bile-stained vomit, blood with severe pain (intussusception), severe abdominal pain or distension, neurological symptoms, persistent vomiting without diarrhoea (consider other cause: UTI, DKA, raised ICP, pyloric stenosis, meningitis), diabetic child.

*Hypernatraemic dehydration* (thirsty, doughy skin, irritable) must be corrected slowly over 48 h; *hyponatraemia* occurs with hypotonic fluids.

### 6.2 Abdominal pain by age

| Age | Common causes | Don't miss |
|---|---|---|
| **Infant** | Colic, gastroenteritis, constipation, UTI | Intussusception, volvulus, incarcerated hernia, testicular torsion |
| **Preschool** | Gastroenteritis, constipation, UTI, mesenteric adenitis, viral illness | Intussusception, appendicitis (atypical, perforation rate high), HUS, diabetic ketoacidosis |
| **School age** | Constipation, gastroenteritis, functional abdominal pain, UTI, mesenteric adenitis, appendicitis, pneumonia (lower lobe), Henoch–Schönlein purpura, tonsillitis | Appendicitis, DKA, IBD, testicular torsion, Meckel diverticulum, ovarian torsion |
| **Adolescent** | Dysmenorrhoea, functional pain, constipation, appendicitis, IBD, coeliac, STI/PID | Ectopic pregnancy, ovarian torsion, testicular torsion, appendicitis, DKA |

- **Intussusception** (peak 3 months–3 years; 6–9 months): intermittent severe colicky pain with drawing up of legs, pallor between episodes, vomiting (may become bilious), "redcurrant jelly" stool (late), sausage-shaped mass; lethargy. Diagnosis by ultrasound (target/doughnut sign); treatment: air or hydrostatic enema reduction (stable, no peritonitis), surgery if failure or perforation. Resuscitate first.
- **Appendicitis**: periumbilical pain migrating to the right iliac fossa, anorexia, vomiting, low-grade fever, tenderness with guarding and rebound; may be atypical in young children (diffuse pain, diarrhoea, perforation in 50–70% of < 5 years). Use pediatric appendicitis score (PAS); ultrasound; CT if equivocal; surgery or non-operative management with antibiotics in selected uncomplicated cases.
- **Constipation**: the most common cause of recurrent abdominal pain; hard stools, withholding, overflow soiling, palpable faecal mass; treat with macrogol (polyethylene glycol) disimpaction then maintenance, behavioural measures. Red flags: onset in neonatal period with delayed meconium (Hirschsprung), failure to thrive, abdominal distension, bilious vomiting, abnormal lumbosacral exam, absent anal wink, vomiting, blood in stool.
- **Functional abdominal pain** (Rome IV): chronic, periumbilical, non-progressive, no alarm features; reassurance and biopsychosocial approach.
- **Henoch–Schönlein purpura (IgA vasculitis)**: palpable purpura on buttocks and legs, arthralgia/arthritis, colicky abdominal pain (can cause intussusception), nephritis (monitor urine and BP for 6–12 months).
- **Haemolytic uraemic syndrome (HUS)**: bloody diarrhoea (Shiga toxin *E. coli* O157), pallor, oliguria, haemolytic anaemia, thrombocytopenia, AKI. Check FBC, film, U&E, urine. Supportive care and dialysis; avoid antibiotics and antimotility drugs.
- **Coeliac disease, inflammatory bowel disease** (growth failure, chronic diarrhoea, blood, mouth ulcers, perianal disease, arthritis, fatigue; faecal calprotectin), **cow's milk protein allergy**, **lactose intolerance**.
- **Testicular torsion**: boys with lower abdominal pain must have their testes examined.

### 6.3 Vomiting in older children

Consider gastroenteritis, UTI, appendicitis, DKA (vomiting with abdominal pain, deep breathing, polyuria and weight loss), raised ICP (morning vomiting, headache, papilloedema), migraine/cyclic vomiting, food poisoning, poisoning, pyloric stenosis (infants), eating disorder, pregnancy, hepatitis.

## 7. Urinary tract and renal conditions

- **UTI** is the most common serious bacterial infection in febrile infants and children < 2 years; symptoms may be non-specific (fever, vomiting, poor feeding, irritability, jaundice in neonates), older children have dysuria, frequency, abdominal pain, enuresis relapse. Diagnose with clean-catch urine or catheter/suprapubic sample (bag specimens are unreliable for culture). Dipstick (leukocyte esterase + nitrite positive: highly likely; both negative: UTI unlikely); culture confirms. Treat with oral antibiotics (cefalexin, amoxicillin–clavulanate, trimethoprim, per local resistance) for 3–5 days for lower tract, 7–10 days for pyelonephritis; IV for ill or < 3 months. Renal ultrasound (during acute illness if atypical/recurrent; otherwise at 4–6 weeks), DMSA scan for atypical or recurrent UTI, VCUG for recurrent or abnormal ultrasound. Prophylaxis not routine.
- **Nocturnal enuresis**: primary (never dry) vs secondary (dry > 6 months, new onset: exclude UTI, diabetes, constipation, stress, sleep apnoea, abuse); alarms, desmopressin, fluid regulation, treat constipation.
- **Nephrotic syndrome**: periorbital oedema, frothy urine, hypoalbuminaemia, heavy proteinuria; minimal change disease in most; prednisolone 60 mg/m²/day (chapter 49).
- **Acute nephritis**: post-streptococcal GN, IgA nephropathy, HSP: hypertension, haematuria, oedema.
- **Hypertension in children**: measure BP with correct cuff size; causes renal, renovascular, coarctation, endocrine, drugs; need systematic evaluation.
- **Wilms tumour**: asymptomatic abdominal mass in child 2–5 years; ultrasound; do not palpate vigorously.

## 8. Neurological and musculoskeletal presentations

### 8.1 Headache

Most are primary: tension-type, migraine (with or without aura; bilateral frontal in children, shorter duration 1–72 h), and infection-related. Red flags: morning/ nocturnal headache with vomiting, worsening pattern, focal deficits, papilloedema, ataxia, personality or school performance change, head tilt, growth failure or visual disturbance (pituitary/craniopharyngioma), onset < 3 years, severe thunderclap headache, fever with neck stiffness, post-trauma, neurocutaneous stigmata, associated seizures. Posterior fossa tumours (medulloblastoma, ependymoma, pilocytic astrocytoma) present with raised ICP and ataxia. Check BP, visual acuity, fundoscopy; MRI for red flags. Idiopathic intracranial hypertension in adolescent girls with obesity. Also screen for sleep, eye strain (rarely), caffeine, dehydration, mood, bullying.

### 8.2 Seizures and epilepsy

- **First seizure**: confirm it is a seizure (mimics: syncope, breath-holding spells, reflex anoxic seizures, night terrors, tics, migraine variants, cardiac arrhythmias such as long QT, pseudoseizures). Check glucose, electrolytes if prolonged or unwell, ECG (long QT) if provoked by exertion/emotion or collapse; EEG; MRI for focal features, regression or age < 2 years.
- **Status epilepticus** (convulsion ≥ 5 min or recurrent without recovery): ABC, oxygen, glucose; 1st-line: IV lorazepam 0.1 mg/kg (or buccal midazolam 0.3–0.5 mg/kg, rectal diazepam 0.5 mg/kg, IM midazolam 0.2 mg/kg); repeat once after 5–10 min; 2nd-line (after 20 min): levetiracetam 40–60 mg/kg, phenytoin 20 mg/kg, or valproate 40 mg/kg (avoid in suspected metabolic disease); 3rd-line: rapid sequence induction with thiopental/propofol, ICU. Treat cause (hypoglycaemia, meningitis, hyponatraemia).
- **Epilepsy syndromes**: childhood absence epilepsy (3–10 years, staring spells, 3 Hz spike-wave; ethosuximide), juvenile myoclonic epilepsy (adolescence, morning myoclonic jerks, valproate avoided in girls), benign rolandic epilepsy (nocturnal facial seizures, often no treatment), infantile spasms (West syndrome: flexor spasms in clusters at 3–7 months, hypsarrhythmia on EEG, developmental regression; **emergency**: ACTH, prednisolone or vigabatrin; tuberous sclerosis).
- **Breath-holding spells**: toddler, provoked by anger/pain, cyanotic or pallid, brief loss of consciousness; benign; check iron deficiency.

### 8.3 The limping child

Stratify by age. **Toddler (1–3 years)**: toddler's fracture (spiral tibial), septic arthritis, transient synovitis, osteomyelitis, DDH, juvenile idiopathic arthritis, non-accidental injury, malignancy. **Child (4–10 years)**: transient synovitis (most common cause of hip pain, afebrile child after viral illness; diagnosis of exclusion), **Perthes disease** (avascular necrosis of femoral head, boys 4–8, insidious limp, knee pain referred from hip), septic arthritis, osteomyelitis, JIA, leukaemia. **Adolescent (10–16 years)**: **slipped capital femoral epiphysis (SCFE)** (obese adolescent, hip or knee pain, externally rotated leg, limited internal rotation; urgent orthopaedics, non-weight bearing), Osgood–Schlatter, stress fractures, sports injuries, bone tumours (osteosarcoma, Ewing sarcoma), spondylolysis.

*Approach*: always examine the hip in a limping child with knee pain; assess fever, ability to bear weight, joint effusion, night pain, systemic symptoms, spinal tenderness. *Investigations*: FBC, CRP/ESR, blood culture, plain X-ray (AP and frog-leg lateral of hips), ultrasound for effusion, MRI if osteomyelitis suspected. Use Kocher criteria for hip aspiration decisions. Persistent bone pain with night pain, pallor, bruising or fever: consider leukaemia and malignancy.

### 8.4 Other neurological presentations

- **Floppy child, ataxia (acute cerebellar ataxia post-viral, medication ingestion, brain tumour, Guillain–Barré syndrome with ascending weakness, opsoclonus–myoclonus with neuroblastoma), acute weakness, encephalitis (fever, altered consciousness, seizures, behavioural change; start aciclovir), hydrocephalus**, school failure and learning disability (screen hearing, vision, sleep apnoea, thyroid, anaemia, epilepsy/absence).
- **Tics and Tourette syndrome**: usually benign; reassure.
- **Head injury**: apply PECARN or CHALICE rules for CT; observe; abuse in infants.

## 9. Haematological and oncological pointers

- **Anaemia**: iron deficiency (toddlers with milk-heavy diets, adolescent girls), thalassaemia, sickle cell disease, G6PD deficiency, lead, chronic disease, blood loss, leukaemia.
- **Bruising/bleeding**: ITP (post-viral, well child with petechiae; platelets < 20), HSP, leukaemia, haemophilia (joint bleeds), von Willebrand disease, vitamin K deficiency bleeding, meningococcaemia, abuse. Check FBC, film, coagulation; platelet count < 10–20 with bleeding needs treatment (IVIG, steroids).
- **Sickle cell disease**: dactylitis in infants, vaso-occlusive pain, acute chest syndrome (fever, chest pain, hypoxia, new infiltrate), splenic sequestration (acute pallor, abdominal pain, big spleen, shock), stroke (focal deficits), aplastic crisis (parvovirus), infection (pneumococcal sepsis; penicillin prophylaxis from 2–3 months; vaccines; fever ≥ 38.5 °C = emergency: blood culture and IV ceftriaxone).
- **Childhood cancer warning signs** (CHILDREN'S CANCER acronym variants): persistent unexplained fever, pallor, bruising/bleeding, bone pain with night waking, limp, headaches with vomiting, neurological change, abdominal mass, lymphadenopathy (hard, > 2 cm, supraclavicular, persistent), leukocoria/squint, testicular mass, weight loss, fatigue. Leukaemia (ALL is most common, peak 2–5 years), brain tumours, lymphoma, neuroblastoma, Wilms, retinoblastoma, bone tumours. Urgent FBC and film; refer same day for suspected leukaemia; do not start steroids before diagnosis (can mask leukaemia/lymphoma).
- **Lymphadenopathy**: reactive nodes common; concerning: > 2 cm, firm/fixed, supraclavicular, progressive, with B symptoms or hepatosplenomegaly.

## 11. Paediatric emergencies and resuscitation

### 11.1 Recognising and managing the seriously ill child

Use ABCDE (chapter 37) with paediatric modifications:

- **Airway**: head-neutral in infants, jaw thrust, suction, oropharyngeal airway; avoid blind finger sweeps.
- **Breathing**: oxygen 100% via mask with reservoir, bag–valve–mask (5 rescue breaths), assess RR, SpO₂, work of breathing, auscultate.
- **Circulation**: HR, CRT, pulses, BP; IV/IO access (IO within 90 s if IV fails); **fluid bolus 10 mL/kg (up to 20 mL/kg in septic/hypovolaemic shock in settings with ICU; 10 mL/kg for cardiogenic or malnourished children)** of isotonic crystalloid; reassess after each bolus; early vasoactive drugs.
- **Disability**: AVPU, GCS, pupils, glucose (**hypoglycaemia: 2 mL/kg 10% glucose IV**), posture.
- **Exposure**: temperature, rash.
- **Drug and fluid calculation**: use weight-based tools; estimated weight: (age in years + 4) × 2 kg (1–10 years), (3 × age + 7) for others; infants: (0.5 × age in months) + 4. Adrenaline 10 µg/kg (0.1 mL/kg of 1:10,000) IV/IO in cardiac arrest; defibrillation 4 J/kg; amiodarone 5 mg/kg. Paediatric cardiac arrest is usually asphyxial: start with 5 breaths then 15:2 compressions:ventilations (30:2 for lone rescuer).

### 11.2 Specific emergencies

- **Anaphylaxis**: IM adrenaline 0.01 mg/kg (0.1 mg/10 kg; <6 months 0.15 mg; 6 months–6 years: 0.15 mg; 6–12 years: 0.3 mg; ≥ 12: 0.5 mg) in the anterolateral thigh, repeat after 5 minutes if no improvement, high-flow oxygen, fluid bolus, chlorphenamine and hydrocortisone are secondary; observe 6–12 h; adrenaline auto-injector prescription and allergy referral (chapter 37, 40).
- **Diabetic ketoacidosis**: new-onset type 1 diabetes in a child with polyuria, polydipsia, weight loss, vomiting, abdominal pain, deep sighing breathing. Diagnose with glucose > 11, pH < 7.3 or bicarbonate < 15, ketonaemia/ketonuria. Treat: cautious rehydration (10 mL/kg bolus only if shocked; then deficit replaced over 48 h), insulin infusion 0.05–0.1 U/kg/h starting 1 hour after fluids, potassium replacement, monitor for **cerebral oedema** (headache, bradycardia, irritability, decreasing GCS: mannitol or hypertonic saline). Avoid bicarbonate.
- **Acute severe asthma, croup, bronchiolitis**: see above.
- **Poisoning**: common in toddlers (iron, paracetamol, tricyclic antidepressants, button batteries (urgent removal if lodged in the oesophagus), magnets, opioids, sulfonylureas, calcium channel blockers, beta-blockers, clonidine ("one pill can kill")); contact poison centre; paracetamol level at 4 hours; N-acetylcysteine. Activated charcoal only within 1 hour in selected cases.
- **Burns and scalds**: cool running water for 20 minutes, cover with cling film, assess depth and TBSA with Lund and Browder chart; analgesia; consider non-accidental injury (stocking/glove distribution, patterned burns).
- **Drowning, choking, foreign body, head injury, trauma**: follow ATLS/APLS principles, with abuse awareness.
- **Anaphylactoid reaction to bee sting**, **acute scrotum**, **hypoglycaemia**, **adrenal crisis (in steroid-dependent child: hydrocortisone 4 mg/kg IV/IM)**.

## 12. Immunisation, safeguarding, consent and communication

- **Immunisation**: know the national schedule (BCG, hepatitis B, DTaP-IPV-Hib, pneumococcal, rotavirus, MMR, varicella, MenACWY/B, HPV, influenza, COVID-19). Check at every contact. Vaccine-preventable diseases (measles, pertussis, Hib, meningococcus, pneumococcus, polio) should be considered in the unvaccinated; contraindications are few (anaphylaxis to previous dose or component; live vaccines in severe immunosuppression). Fever and local reactions are expected.
- **Safeguarding and child abuse**: physical abuse (bruising in babies, fractures, burns, head injury), neglect (growth faltering, poor hygiene, missed appointments), sexual abuse (disclosures, genital injury, STI, behavioural change), emotional abuse, fabricated or induced illness. Be alert to inconsistent histories, delayed presentation, repeated injuries, parental behaviour, domestic violence, substance abuse, parental mental illness. **Act**: document verbatim words, body maps, photographs where policy allows; discuss with a senior; report to child protection services; do not interrogate the child or promise secrecy; do not delay urgent medical care. In many countries, reporting is mandatory.
- **Consent and capacity in minors**: those with parental responsibility consent for young children; adolescents may be competent (Gillick competence in the UK; "mature minor" and statutory rights elsewhere) to consent to contraception, mental health care or testing; confidentiality limits include risk of serious harm. In emergencies, treat in the child's best interests. Children should be involved in decisions at their level.
- **Communication**: use simple language; explain what you will do; honesty about pain; allow play; ask the child "what do you think is wrong?"; support parents' anxiety; elicit "what are you most worried about?". Use trained interpreters, not the child, for translation. Provide written safety-net advice.
- **Chronic illness and transition**: involve schools, plan transfer to adult services.
- **Child death**: delivered with compassion; refer for review and bereavement support.

## Worked cases

### Case 1: The quiet toddler with a high temperature

**Presentation.** Jamal, 18 months, has had 2 days of fever up to 39.5 °C, now is irritable and drowsy, vomiting twice, refusing food, and has cold feet. Parents say "he is not himself". HR 178, RR 48, CRT 4 s, temperature 38.9 °C, mottled limbs, a few small non-blanching spots on his legs. Not vaccinated against meningococcus.

**Reasoning.** Red flags: mottled skin, reduced activity, prolonged CRT, non-blanching rash. Septic shock/meningococcal disease until proven otherwise.

**Action.** Call for help, high-flow oxygen, IV/IO access, glucose, cultures, gas, **ceftriaxone 80 mg/kg IV immediately** (do not wait), fluid bolus 10–20 mL/kg 0.9% saline repeated; consider adrenaline/noradrenaline infusion if fluid-refractory; avoid LP (shock, rash); intensive care; notify public health; prophylaxis for contacts. Recovery after 48 h with antibiotics for 7 days; hearing test and follow-up.

**Lessons.** Parental concern, mottling and prolonged CRT are the earliest signs of shock. Do not wait for LP or results.

### Case 2: Wheezing infant

**Presentation.** Ayesha, 4 months, born at 35 weeks, 3 days of cold, now fast breathing, poor feeding (taking half usual), intermittent apnoeas reported by mother. RR 70, subcostal recession, SpO₂ 89% in air, widespread fine crackles and wheeze.

**Reasoning.** Bronchiolitis (RSV) with hypoxia, apnoea risk and poor feeding: admission criteria.

**Action.** Admit; oxygen via nasal cannula/high-flow to keep SpO₂ ≥ 92%; NG feeds; gentle suction; monitor for apnoeas; no bronchodilators/antibiotics/steroids; escalate to high-flow/CPAP if increasing work of breathing or rising CO₂; test for RSV and pertussis if apnoea. Discharge when SpO₂ stable in air, feeding > 75% of normal and parents confident; safety-net advice. Palivizumab/nirsevimab consideration.

**Lessons.** Treatment is supportive; recognise those at risk of apnoea and exhaustion. Avoid unnecessary drugs and investigations.

### Case 3: Vomiting and diarrhoea with drowsiness

**Presentation.** Lucas, 2 years, 3 days of watery diarrhoea (8 episodes/day) and vomiting. Weighs 11.2 kg (was 12.0 kg 1 month ago). Sunken eyes, dry mouth, lethargic, drinks poorly, skin pinch goes back slowly, RR 38 (deep), HR 160, CRT 4 s.

**Reasoning.** Severe dehydration (about 7% weight loss) with early shock (IMCI: lethargy, sunken eyes, unable to drink, slow pinch). Deep breathing = metabolic acidosis.

**Action.** IV access, check glucose (3.5 mmol/L: treat), gas, electrolytes; fluid bolus 20 mL/kg 0.9% saline, repeat if perfusion not restored; once perfusion improves, replace deficit (≈ 70 mL/kg over 24 h in addition to maintenance and ongoing losses) with isotonic fluids with dextrose; start ORS via NG when tolerated; zinc if in resource-limited setting; monitor sodium. Stool culture only if bloody or persistent. Improves in 12 hours; discharged after oral intake established.

**Lessons.** Assess dehydration by multiple signs; treat shock first; use isotonic fluids and monitor sodium.

### Case 4: The limping boy with knee pain

**Presentation.** Ben, 13, BMI 31, 3 weeks of left knee pain and limp; no injury; afebrile. Walks with an externally rotated left leg; knee exam normal; hip: limited internal rotation, obligatory external rotation on flexion; pain on hip movement.

**Reasoning.** Hip pathology presenting as knee pain; obese adolescent: slipped capital femoral epiphysis (SCFE).

**Action.** Non-weight bearing (crutches/wheelchair), urgent AP and frog-leg lateral X-rays of both hips: left slip (Klein line abnormal). Orthopaedic surgery for in-situ pinning; examine contralateral hip (bilateral in 20–40%); screen for endocrine causes (hypothyroidism, growth hormone), weight management; warn about avascular necrosis.

**Lessons.** Always examine the hip when the child complains of knee pain. SCFE is an orthopaedic urgency.

### Case 5: A baby with bruises

**Presentation.** A 4-month-old girl brought for "fussiness". On examination: bruising on the left cheek, ear and torso, a torn frenulum. Parents attribute the bruises to a sibling's play; she cannot roll. Anterior fontanelle full; irritable.

**Reasoning.** Bruising in a non-mobile infant on face/ear/torso, torn frenulum, inconsistent history, bulging fontanelle: possible abusive head trauma.

**Action.** Admit and notify senior paediatrician and child protection team, avoid confronting the parents; document injuries (body map), photograph per policy; skeletal survey, CT/MRI head (subdural haemorrhage), dilated ophthalmoscopy (retinal haemorrhages), coagulation screen, FBC, bone profile, LFT, amylase; exclude medical mimics; police and social services as per procedures; discharge only with a safe plan. Multidisciplinary strategy meeting.

**Lessons.** Pattern and developmental stage matter. A normal-looking baby may have intracranial bleeding. Safeguarding is a clinical duty.

### Case 6: Persistent fever and a rash

**Presentation.** Sara, 3, has had fever 39–40 °C for 6 days, bilateral non-purulent conjunctivitis, cracked red lips, strawberry tongue, cervical lymph node 2 cm, red swollen hands and a polymorphous rash. CRP 120, platelets 520, ALT mildly raised, sterile pyuria.

**Reasoning.** Complete Kawasaki disease (fever ≥ 5 days plus ≥ 4 principal features).

**Action.** Admit; echocardiogram (baseline coronary dimensions: z-scores); IVIG 2 g/kg over 10–12 hours and aspirin (per local protocol: high-dose or low-dose); repeat IVIG or add steroids/infliximab if fever persists 36 hours after IVIG; follow-up echocardiograms at 1–2 and 4–6 weeks; avoid live vaccines for 11 months; low-dose aspirin until coronary normal; annual cardiology review if aneurysm.

**Lessons.** Fever ≥ 5 days in a young child with mucocutaneous signs needs Kawasaki consideration; treat within 10 days.

## Common pitfalls

1. **Not weighing the child** before prescribing; decimal point errors (10-fold) in doses.
2. **Relying on blood pressure** to detect shock in a child; ignoring tachycardia and CRT.
3. **Ignoring parental concern** ("not himself").
4. **Treating fever** without looking for a source and without undressing the child.
5. **Missing meningitis/sepsis** because of absent neck stiffness in infants or "blanching" rash early on.
6. **Overuse of antibiotics** for viral infections; or under-treatment of pneumonia in low-resource settings.
7. **Bolus fluids in cardiogenic shock or severe malnutrition** without monitoring.
8. **Hypotonic maintenance fluids**, causing hyponatraemia.
9. **Missing the hip** in a child with knee pain; assuming "growing pains" without exclusion of red flags.
10. **Calling bilious vomiting reflux** or missing intussusception in a pale, lethargic infant.
11. **Attributing abdominal pain to constipation** without ruling out appendicitis, DKA, torsion.
12. **Not testing urine** in a febrile child without a source.
13. **Delay in recognising developmental regression** or autism.
14. **Failing to ask about safeguarding** or to consider abuse in unexplained injuries.
15. **Giving aspirin, codeine, or ibuprofen inappropriately**.
16. **Discharging without safety-net advice** or without checking that parents understand.

## Key points

- Observe first, undress the child, and listen to parents; use PAT and age-specific vitals; hypotension is a late sign.
- Assess growth and development at every opportunity; recognise regression and red flags.
- IMCI and traffic-light systems help classify risk; hypoxia (SpO₂), fast breathing, danger signs and dehydration drive action.
- Fever: find the source; treat sepsis and meningitis fast; consider Kawasaki when fever lasts ≥ 5 days.
- Bronchiolitis and viral wheeze need supportive care; asthma follows a stepwise plan; pneumonia is diagnosed by tachypnoea and treated with amoxicillin.
- Gastroenteritis: ORS first; IV isotonic fluids for shock; zinc in low-income settings.
- Abdominal pain: always consider appendicitis, intussusception, torsion, DKA and UTI.
- Weight-based dosing and checking are essential; know emergency drug doses.
- Safeguarding, consent and communication are clinical skills.

## Self-assessment

**Questions**

1. Name the three components of the paediatric assessment triangle and what an abnormal appearance implies.
2. A 3-year-old has RR 52/min and chest indrawing in a clinic without oxygen. According to IMCI, what is the classification and the action?
3. List five red features in the NICE traffic-light system for febrile children.
4. A child has bloody diarrhoea, pallor, and falling urine output. What condition is suspected and which investigations/treatment are appropriate (and which are not)?
5. When is a fluid bolus potentially harmful in children, and what is the usual bolus volume?
6. Describe the clinical picture and first-line imaging for intussusception.
7. A 10-year-old boy with fever has a hot, swollen knee and won't bear weight. What are the likely diagnosis, key investigations and treatment?
8. What are the first-line drugs for a prolonged seizure in a child?
9. Which developmental milestones should prompt urgent evaluation if absent by 12 months and by 18 months?
10. Give three features that raise suspicion of child abuse in an infant.

**Answers**

1. Appearance, work of breathing, circulation to skin. Abnormal appearance (poor tone, poor interactiveness, inconsolability, abnormal gaze or cry) suggests hypoxia, hypoperfusion, or CNS dysfunction and indicates a seriously ill child.
2. Severe pneumonia (chest indrawing; fast breathing ≥ 40 for age 1–5 years), referral to hospital/urgent treatment; give first dose of antibiotic (e.g., amoxicillin or injectable ampicillin/gentamicin), oxygen if SpO₂ < 90%.
3. Pale/mottled/ashen/blue; no response to social cues or doesn't wake; weak, high-pitched or continuous cry; grunting; RR > 60; moderate or severe chest indrawing; reduced skin turgor; age < 3 months with temperature ≥ 38 °C; non-blanching rash; bulging fontanelle; neck stiffness; status epilepticus; focal neurological signs; focal seizures; bilious vomiting (any five).
4. Haemolytic uraemic syndrome (Shiga-toxin *E. coli*). Investigations: FBC and film (schistocytes), platelets, U&E, creatinine, LDH, stool culture/PCR. Treatment: supportive (fluid balance, BP control, dialysis if needed, transfusion as required). Avoid antibiotics, anti-motility agents, NSAIDs and excessive fluid.
5. In fluid-overloaded states: cardiogenic shock, severe acute malnutrition, severe anaemia, and in some resource-limited settings without hypotension (FEAST). Usual bolus 10–20 mL/kg isotonic crystalloid, reassess after each (10 mL/kg in cardiac/malnourished).
6. Intermittent severe colic with drawing up legs, pallor/lethargy between episodes, vomiting (may be bilious), redcurrant jelly stool (late), sausage mass, age 3 months–3 years; ultrasound (target/doughnut sign); treat with air/hydrostatic enema after resuscitation; surgery if perforation or failure.
7. Septic arthritis (until excluded). Investigations: FBC, CRP/ESR, blood culture, ultrasound of the joint, urgent orthopaedic review for aspiration/lavage; IV antibiotics (flucloxacillin or cefuroxime/ceftriaxone, plus considering *Kingella* in younger and MRSA coverage as needed) after samples; switch to oral after clinical improvement.
8. Benzodiazepine: IV/IO lorazepam 0.1 mg/kg (or buccal/IM midazolam, rectal diazepam when no IV); repeat once after 5–10 minutes; second-line: levetiracetam, phenytoin or valproate; correct hypoglycaemia; protect airway.
9. By 12 months: no babble, no pointing/waving, no response to name, no weight bearing on legs, no transfer, not sitting. By 18 months: not walking, no single words, no pretend play, loss of skills.
10. Bruising in a non-mobile infant or on ears/neck/torso; inconsistent or changing history; delayed presentation; torn frenulum; fractures (rib, metaphyseal); retinal haemorrhages; unexplained apnoea or encephalopathy; failure to thrive/neglect; parents' behaviour (any three).
