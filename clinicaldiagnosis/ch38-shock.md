## Chapter 38. Shock

### Learning objectives

After studying this chapter you should be able to:

1. Define shock as inadequate tissue oxygen delivery relative to demand, and explain how cardiac output, preload, contractility, afterload and oxygen content determine perfusion.
2. Recognise the clinical features of shock, including the early compensated stage, and the limits of blood pressure as a marker.
3. Distinguish the four types of shock (hypovolaemic, cardiogenic, obstructive, distributive) using bedside findings (temperature of extremities, JVP, chest findings, pulse character) and point-of-care ultrasound.
4. Describe the first-line resuscitation for each type, including fluids, vasopressors, inotropes, blood products and cause-specific treatment.
5. Use monitoring (lactate, capillary refill, urine output, arterial pressure, echocardiography) and dynamic indices to guide therapy and recognise over-resuscitation.
6. Identify special forms of shock: haemorrhagic, anaphylactic, neurogenic, adrenal, toxic, drug-induced, and septic.
7. Anticipate complications (multi-organ failure, ARDS, AKI, DIC) and recognise when to escalate to intensive care.

---

### 38.1 What shock is

**Shock** is a life-threatening state of **acute circulatory failure in which tissue oxygen delivery is inadequate to meet metabolic demand,** leading to cellular hypoxia, anaerobic metabolism, lactic acidosis, and, if uncorrected, organ dysfunction and death. It is a **clinical diagnosis** based on evidence of tissue hypoperfusion, **not** on a particular blood pressure.

Hypotension is a **late and unreliable** sign, especially in children, young adults, athletes, pregnant women and patients on vasoactive drugs; a patient can be profoundly shocked with a "normal" blood pressure (compensated shock) and **a low blood pressure is not always shock** (e.g. a healthy young woman with a baseline of 90/60).

#### Physiology: oxygen delivery

> **DO₂ = cardiac output (CO) × arterial oxygen content (CaO₂)**
> **CO = heart rate × stroke volume**
> **Stroke volume depends on preload, contractility, and afterload**
> **CaO₂ = (Hb × 1.34 × SaO₂) + (0.003 × PaO₂)**

Each component can fail:

| Component | Failure | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Preload** (venous return, filling) | Low | Haemorrhage, dehydration, vasodilatation (venous pooling), tension pneumothorax, tamponade, PE, pregnancy (IVC compression) |
| **Contractility** | Low | Myocardial infarction, cardiomyopathy, myocarditis, septic cardiomyopathy, drug toxicity (beta-blockers, calcium channel blockers), acidosis |
| **Rate and rhythm** | Too fast or too slow | Tachyarrhythmias, bradycardia, heart block |
| **Afterload (systemic vascular resistance, SVR)** | Too low | Sepsis, anaphylaxis, neurogenic shock, adrenal insufficiency, drugs |
| **Oxygen content** | Low | Severe anaemia/haemorrhage, hypoxaemia, carbon monoxide, methaemoglobinaemia |

**Mean arterial pressure (MAP)** = diastolic + one-third of pulse pressure (approximately 2/3 diastolic + 1/3 systolic); organ perfusion depends on MAP (a threshold of at least 60–65 mmHg for most organs; higher in chronic hypertension).

#### Compensation

The body initially maintains perfusion of vital organs by:

- **Sympathetic activation:** tachycardia, vasoconstriction (skin, gut, kidney), venoconstriction, increased contractility.
- **Neuro-humoral responses:** renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system (sodium and water retention), vasopressin (ADH), catecholamines, cortisol.
- **Fluid shifts:** movement of interstitial fluid into the vasculature.
- **Increased oxygen extraction** by tissues.

When these mechanisms are exhausted, **decompensation** follows with hypotension, lactic acidosis, organ failure, and **irreversible shock** (cell death, vasoplegia, refractory to treatment).

#### Stages

| Stage | Features |
|---|---|
| **Compensated (early)** | Tachycardia, tachypnoea, cool peripheries (in hypovolaemia), anxious, narrow pulse pressure, normal or near-normal BP, reduced urine output, raised lactate |
| **Decompensated** | Hypotension, confusion or drowsiness, oliguria, mottling, metabolic acidosis, rising lactate |
| **Irreversible / refractory** | Multi-organ failure, vasoplegia, myocardial depression, DIC, death despite treatment |

### 38.2 Recognition

#### Symptoms and history

Anxiety or sense of doom, weakness, dizziness, thirst, dyspnoea, chest or abdominal pain, palpitations, reduced urine, confusion. History of the **cause:** bleeding (haematemesis, melaena, trauma, vaginal bleeding), vomiting/diarrhoea, burns, fever and infection, chest pain, allergen exposure, drug overdose, spinal injury, steroid therapy or withdrawal, recent surgery, anticoagulation, pregnancy.

#### Signs

| System | Findings |
|---|---|
| **General** | Pale, sweaty, restless or drowsy; mottled, cyanosed; hypothermia or fever |
| **Pulse** | **Tachycardia (>100–120);** thready (hypovolaemic, cardiogenic) or bounding (distributive); bradycardia (neurogenic shock, beta-blocker, heart block, late hypovolaemia in young patients, increased intracranial pressure) |
| **Blood pressure** | Narrow pulse pressure early (hypovolaemia), low systolic and low diastolic (distributive), low MAP (<65 mmHg) |
| **Skin and perfusion** | **Capillary refill >2–3 seconds** (central), cool clammy skin (hypovolaemic, cardiogenic, obstructive) or warm flushed skin (early distributive); **mottling** (knee and periphery: associated with mortality) |
| **Respiratory** | Tachypnoea (compensating for metabolic acidosis, Kussmaul), hypoxia |
| **Neck veins** | **Low JVP** (hypovolaemia, distributive), **raised JVP** (cardiogenic, obstructive) |
| **Lungs** | Crackles (cardiogenic), absent breath sounds (tension pneumothorax), clear lungs (obstructive, hypovolaemic) |
| **Heart** | Murmurs (acute valve lesion, VSD), muffled sounds (tamponade), S3 |
| **Abdomen** | Distension, tenderness (haemorrhage, perforation, pancreatitis), pulsatile mass (aneurysm) |
| **Urine output** | **<0.5 mL/kg/hour** |
| **Mental state** | Agitation, confusion, drowsiness |

#### Laboratory and monitoring markers of hypoperfusion

- **Lactate** (arterial or venous): >2 mmol/L abnormal, >4 mmol/L severe; **lactate trend** is more useful than a single value (clearance). Causes of raised lactate other than shock: seizures, liver failure, metformin, thiamine deficiency, adrenaline infusion (type B), exercise.
- **Base deficit/bicarbonate, anion gap.**
- **Central or mixed venous oxygen saturation** (ScvO₂ <70% suggests inadequate delivery or high extraction; a high value in sepsis may reflect impaired extraction).
- **Venous-to-arterial CO₂ gap** (>6 mmHg suggests inadequate flow).
- **Creatinine, urea, troponin, LFTs, coagulation, FBC (Hb, platelets), glucose, calcium,** ABG, ECG, cultures, toxicology.
- **Shock index** (HR/SBP), **modified shock index** (HR/MAP).

### 38.3 Classification and bedside differentiation

#### The four types

| Type | Primary problem | Haemodynamic pattern | Common causes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Hypovolaemic** | ↓ Preload (intravascular volume) | ↓CO, ↑SVR, ↓CVP/PCWP | **Haemorrhage** (trauma, GI, obstetric, ruptured aneurysm, ectopic pregnancy), **fluid loss** (vomiting, diarrhoea, burns, DKA/HHS, diuretics, adrenal crisis, heat illness), third-space loss (pancreatitis, bowel obstruction, peritonitis) |
| **Cardiogenic** | Pump failure | ↓CO, ↑SVR, ↑CVP/PCWP | **Acute MI** (extensive LV or RV infarction, mechanical complications: papillary muscle rupture, VSD, free wall rupture), **arrhythmia** (VT, rapid AF, complete heart block), **acute valve failure** (endocarditis, prosthetic), **cardiomyopathy, myocarditis,** end-stage heart failure, Takotsubo, drug toxicity, post-cardiac surgery, septic cardiomyopathy |
| **Obstructive** | Extracardiac obstruction to flow or filling | ↓CO, ↑SVR, ↑CVP (↑ or ↓ PCWP) | **Tension pneumothorax, cardiac tamponade, massive pulmonary embolism,** constrictive pericarditis, severe auto-PEEP (asthma, COPD with ventilation), aortic dissection (with tamponade/occlusion), severe pulmonary hypertension, abdominal compartment syndrome |
| **Distributive** | Vasodilatation, ↓SVR, maldistribution | Usually ↑CO (↓ in late stages), ↓SVR, variable CVP | **Sepsis/septic shock** (most common), **anaphylaxis, neurogenic shock,** adrenal crisis, toxic shock syndrome, liver failure, pancreatitis, burns (early), vasodilator drugs, post-cardiopulmonary bypass vasoplegia, thyrotoxicosis/myxoedema, carbon monoxide/cyanide |

**Mixed shock is common** (septic shock may also have hypovolaemia and cardiac depression; trauma may combine haemorrhagic, obstructive, and neurogenic components; cardiac arrest with PE).

#### Bedside differentiation (the "cold or warm, wet or dry" approach)

| Finding | Hypovolaemic | Cardiogenic | Obstructive | Distributive |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Extremities** | **Cold,** pale, clammy | **Cold,** clammy, possibly cyanosed | **Cold,** clammy | **Warm,** flushed (early); cold if late or hypodynamic |
| **Pulse** | Fast, thready | Fast (or slow), weak | Fast, weak | Fast, **bounding** (wide pulse pressure) |
| **Pulse pressure** | Narrow | Narrow | Narrow (tamponade: narrow; pulsus paradoxus) | **Wide** (low diastolic) |
| **JVP** | **Low (flat)** | **Raised** | **Raised** (tamponade, PE, tension pneumothorax) | Low or normal |
| **Lungs** | Clear | **Crackles** (pulmonary oedema) | Clear (PE, tamponade) or **absent breath sounds** (tension pneumothorax) | Clear (or pneumonia/ARDS) |
| **Heart sounds** | Normal | S3, murmur, gallop | Muffled (tamponade), loud P₂ (PE) | Normal |
| **Clues** | Bleeding, vomiting, dehydration, burns, abdominal signs | Chest pain, ECG changes, history of heart disease, pulmonary oedema | Trauma, dyspnoea, DVT, central line, pacemaker, recent surgery | **Fever or hypothermia,** infection source, rash (urticaria, petechiae), allergen exposure, spinal injury, steroid history |
| **Treatment priority** | Volume, blood, stop bleeding | Reperfusion, inotropes, avoid excessive fluid | **Relieve the obstruction** (decompress, drain, lyse) | Treat cause, fluids, vasopressors |

Exceptions: **septic shock** may be cold in late stages or with cardiomyopathy; **anaphylaxis** can combine distributive and hypovolaemic components; **neurogenic shock** is warm with bradycardia.

#### Point-of-care ultrasound: the RUSH protocol (Rapid Ultrasound in SHock)

Rapid bedside ultrasound (Chapter 67) can quickly identify the shock type:

- **Pump (heart):** LV function (hyperdynamic: hypovolaemia or early sepsis; poor: cardiogenic), RV dilatation (PE: McConnell sign, septal flattening), pericardial effusion and tamponade (RA/RV diastolic collapse), regional wall motion abnormalities.
- **Tank (volume and pressure):** IVC size and collapsibility (small, collapsing: hypovolaemia; plethoric, non-collapsing: raised right atrial pressure), free fluid (FAST: peritoneal, pericardial, pleural), lung ultrasound (B-lines: pulmonary oedema; absent lung sliding: pneumothorax; pleural effusion).
- **Pipes (vessels):** abdominal aorta (aneurysm), thoracic aorta (dissection flap, dilated root), DVT (compression ultrasound of the leg veins).

Ultrasound **supplements, but does not replace,** clinical assessment.

### 38.4 Principles of initial management

All forms of shock are managed by **simultaneously assessing, resuscitating and treating the cause.** The principles below follow the ABCDE approach (Chapter 37).

1. **Airway and breathing:** oxygen to maintain SpO₂ ≥94% (consider early intubation and ventilation in refractory shock or exhaustion, but induction can precipitate cardiovascular collapse: resuscitate before intubation, use haemodynamically stable agents, vasopressors ready).
2. **Circulation access:** **two large-bore cannulae or intraosseous access;** central venous access for vasopressors when needed (peripheral norepinephrine may be used short-term through a good proximal vein).
3. **Monitoring:** continuous ECG, SpO₂, **non-invasive BP every few minutes (arterial line for unstable patients),** urinary catheter, temperature, lactate and blood gas, glucose; consider **cardiac output monitoring** and **echocardiography.**
4. **Identify the type of shock** by the clinical and ultrasound approach and **start cause-directed therapy at once.**
5. **Fluids:** judicious (see 38.5).
6. **Vasoactive drugs** when perfusion remains inadequate (38.6).
7. **Correct reversible contributors:** hypoxia, acidosis, electrolytes (potassium, calcium, magnesium), glucose, temperature, anaemia, coagulopathy, drugs.
8. **Early senior and ICU involvement.**
9. **Targets and reassessment:** MAP ≥65 mmHg; capillary refill normalising; mental state improving; urine output ≥0.5 mL/kg/hour; falling lactate; adequate ScvO₂; no signs of overload. **Reassess frequently.**

### 38.5 Fluid resuscitation

#### General principles

- **Fluids are drugs:** use for a purpose (restore preload in volume-responsive patients), in a **fluid challenge** (e.g. 250–500 mL over 10–15 minutes in adults; 10–20 mL/kg in children), and reassess; stop when there is no further haemodynamic benefit or evidence of overload.
- **Choose balanced crystalloids** (Ringer's lactate, Plasma-Lyte, Hartmann's) in most situations; normal saline carries a risk of hyperchloraemic acidosis and kidney injury with large volumes; albumin may be considered in septic shock after large crystalloid volumes or in cirrhosis; **avoid hydroxyethyl starches and gelatins** (renal injury, mortality).
- **Predict fluid responsiveness** (dynamic measures: **passive leg raising** with change in cardiac output or pulse pressure; **pulse pressure variation or stroke volume variation** in ventilated patients without arrhythmia; IVC variation; fluid challenge) rather than relying on CVP.
- **Beware over-resuscitation:** positive fluid balance is associated with worse outcomes (pulmonary oedema, ARDS, abdominal compartment syndrome, impaired renal and cardiac function).

#### Haemorrhagic shock: damage-control resuscitation

- **Stop the bleeding** (direct pressure, tourniquet, pelvic binder, surgical or radiological control: the definitive treatment).
- **Minimal crystalloid;** **early balanced blood product resuscitation** (red cells : plasma : platelets in roughly 1:1:1 via a **massive transfusion protocol**), warmed products; **tranexamic acid** (1 g IV over 10 minutes within 3 hours of injury, then 1 g over 8 hours; also obstetric haemorrhage); **calcium** replacement (citrate toxicity); avoid hypothermia (<35 °C), acidosis, and coagulopathy ("lethal triad"); **permissive hypotension** (target systolic ~80–90 mmHg or MAP 50–60 until bleeding is controlled, except in traumatic brain injury and spinal cord injury where higher perfusion pressure is required); point-of-care coagulation testing (TEG/ROTEM), fibrinogen replacement (cryoprecipitate or fibrinogen concentrate) and prothrombin complex concentrate as guided.
- **Non-trauma haemorrhage:** GI bleeding (Chapter 27), ruptured aortic aneurysm, obstetric haemorrhage (uterotonics, tranexamic acid, uterine massage, balloon tamponade, surgery), ruptured ectopic pregnancy: see relevant chapters.

#### Non-haemorrhagic hypovolaemia

- Replace losses with crystalloid (DKA, gastroenteritis: Chapters 26, 43), correct electrolytes; **burns:** formula-based (Parkland) resuscitation; **adrenal crisis:** saline with hydrocortisone.

#### Cardiogenic shock

- **Avoid large fluid loads;** a small challenge (250 mL) is reasonable if there is no pulmonary congestion and the filling pressures are low (e.g. right ventricular infarction: raised JVP, clear lungs, hypotension: fluid, avoid nitrates and diuretics).

### 38.6 Vasopressors and inotropes

**Indications:** MAP <65 mmHg despite adequate preload (fluid), or **life-threatening hypotension** while fluids are being given (do not wait for fluid completion in septic or anaphylactic shock).

| Drug | Mechanism | Dose (adult, typical) | Use and cautions |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Noradrenaline (norepinephrine)** | α₁ >> β₁: vasoconstriction, modest inotropy | **0.05–0.5 µg/kg/min** (titrate to MAP) | **First-line vasopressor** in septic, cardiogenic, and most other shock; central line preferred, peripheral short-term acceptable |
| **Adrenaline (epinephrine)** | α, β₁, β₂ | 0.01–0.5 µg/kg/min | **Anaphylaxis (IM first),** refractory shock, cardiac arrest; raises lactate (type B); arrhythmias |
| **Vasopressin** | V1 receptor vasoconstriction | **0.03 units/min** (fixed) | Add to noradrenaline in septic shock to reduce noradrenaline requirements; caution in cardiac and mesenteric ischaemia |
| **Dobutamine** | β₁ inotrope (β₂ vasodilator) | **2.5–20 µg/kg/min** | **Low cardiac output with adequate MAP** (cardiogenic shock, septic cardiomyopathy with persistent hypoperfusion); tachyarrhythmias; hypotension if volume depleted |
| **Dopamine** | Dose-dependent (D, β, α) | 5–20 µg/kg/min | Less favoured (more arrhythmias); may be used in bradycardic hypotension |
| **Phenylephrine** | Pure α₁ | | Hypotension with tachyarrhythmia, anaesthesia, neurogenic shock; reflex bradycardia; decreases cardiac output in heart failure |
| **Milrinone, levosimendan** | PDE-III inhibitor, calcium sensitiser (inodilators) | | Cardiogenic shock with high vascular resistance, right heart failure; vasodilatation; renal dosing |
| **Angiotensin II** | | | Refractory vasodilatory shock |
| **Methylene blue, hydroxocobalamin** | Nitric oxide pathway | | Vasoplegia (selected) |
| **Hydrocortisone** | Glucocorticoid | 200 mg/day | **Septic shock requiring escalating vasopressors; adrenal crisis** |
| **Calcium** | | | Hypocalcaemia, calcium channel blocker toxicity, massive transfusion |

**Practical points:** start vasopressors **early** (do not delay for large-volume resuscitation), titrate to MAP and **perfusion markers,** monitor for extravasation (central access), watch for arrhythmias, and **wean slowly.** In **chronic hypertension,** a higher MAP target (e.g. 70–80) may be considered; in **uncontrolled haemorrhage,** vasopressors are generally avoided in favour of volume and haemostasis.

### 38.7 Specific forms of shock

#### Septic shock

Chapter 39. In brief: **sepsis with persistent hypotension requiring vasopressors to maintain MAP ≥65 and lactate >2 mmol/L despite adequate fluid.** Early antibiotics (within 1 hour), source control, fluid, noradrenaline, steroids in refractory shock.

#### Anaphylactic shock

Adrenaline IM, fluids, oxygen, airway; refractory: adrenaline infusion, vasopressin, glucagon in patients on beta-blockers, methylene blue (Chapter 37).

#### Cardiogenic shock

- **Definition:** persistent hypotension (SBP <90 mmHg or vasopressors needed) with evidence of hypoperfusion and **elevated filling pressures** (despite adequate volume) due to primary cardiac dysfunction; mortality about 40–50%.
- **Evaluate with:** ECG, troponin, echocardiography (LV/RV function, mechanical complications), chest X-ray, lactate.
- **Treatment:**
  - **Emergency revascularisation (primary PCI)** for acute MI complicated by shock (SHOCK trial).
  - **Haemodynamic support:** noradrenaline for hypotension, dobutamine (or milrinone) for low output, careful diuretics for congestion, avoid excess fluids (except RV infarct), correct arrhythmias (cardioversion, antiarrhythmics, pacing), correct acidosis and electrolytes.
  - **Mechanical circulatory support:** intra-aortic balloon pump (limited evidence), **Impella** (percutaneous LV assist), **VA-ECMO** in refractory shock (bridge to recovery, transplant, or durable LVAD).
  - **Mechanical complications:** surgery (papillary muscle rupture with severe MR, ventricular septal rupture, free-wall rupture with tamponade).
  - **Valvular emergencies:** acute aortic or mitral regurgitation, prosthetic valve thrombosis, endocarditis: urgent surgery.
  - **Myocarditis/stress cardiomyopathy:** supportive; inotropes with caution (catecholamines can worsen Takotsubo).
  - **Pulmonary embolism:** see below; **tamponade:** drainage.

#### Obstructive shock

| Cause | Key treatment |
|---|---|
| **Tension pneumothorax** | Needle decompression → chest drain |
| **Cardiac tamponade** | Fluid bolus as a bridge, **pericardiocentesis** (echo-guided) or surgical drainage; avoid positive-pressure ventilation and sedation if possible |
| **Massive pulmonary embolism** | **Systemic thrombolysis** (alteplase 100 mg over 2 hours or 50 mg bolus in arrest), catheter-directed therapy or surgical embolectomy; anticoagulation; vasopressors (noradrenaline) with careful fluids (avoid overloading RV); VA-ECMO in refractory cases |
| **Auto-PEEP / dynamic hyperinflation** (ventilated asthmatics) | Disconnect from the ventilator and allow exhalation, reduce rate and tidal volume, bronchodilators |
| **Abdominal compartment syndrome** | Decompression |
| **Aortic dissection with tamponade or occlusion** | Surgery |

#### Neurogenic shock

- **Loss of sympathetic tone from spinal cord injury above T6** (or high spinal anaesthesia, brainstem lesion): **hypotension with bradycardia, warm, dry extremities, wide pulse pressure,** loss of temperature regulation (poikilothermia).
- **Do not assume** neurogenic shock in a trauma patient with hypotension: **exclude haemorrhage first** (the most common cause); tachycardia in a trauma patient suggests haemorrhage, but bradycardia may be absent in polytrauma.
- **Distinguish from spinal shock** (flaccid paralysis and areflexia below the lesion).
- **Treatment:** IV fluids (modest, to avoid pulmonary oedema), **vasopressors (noradrenaline or phenylephrine)** to maintain MAP ≥85 mmHg for 5–7 days in spinal cord injury (to maintain cord perfusion), **atropine** or pacing for symptomatic bradycardia, immobilisation, temperature maintenance.

#### Adrenal crisis

- **Features:** shock with hypotension unresponsive to fluids, hypoglycaemia, hyponatraemia, hyperkalaemia, abdominal pain, vomiting, fever, confusion; occurs in patients with adrenal insufficiency (Addison disease), after abrupt steroid withdrawal, with stress (infection, surgery, trauma) in patients on long-term steroids, bilateral adrenal haemorrhage (sepsis, anticoagulation), hypopituitarism.
- **Treatment (immediately, before test results):** **hydrocortisone 100 mg IV (or IM) bolus, then 200 mg/24 hours** (infusion or 50 mg every 6 hours), **IV 0.9% saline** (1 L over the first hour, then as required), **glucose** if hypoglycaemic; treat precipitant; later fludrocortisone and education on sick-day rules.

#### Toxic shock syndrome

- Toxin-mediated (staphylococcal: tampons, wound infection; streptococcal: necrotising fasciitis): fever, diffuse macular erythroderma, hypotension, multi-organ involvement, desquamation after 1–2 weeks. **Source control, antibiotics (flucloxacillin or equivalent plus clindamycin as anti-toxin; vancomycin if MRSA), IVIG** in severe cases.

#### Drug and toxin-induced shock

- **Calcium channel blocker and beta-blocker overdose:** profound hypotension, bradycardia, hypoglycaemia (beta-blocker), hyperglycaemia (CCB); **calcium, high-dose insulin euglycaemia therapy (HIET),** glucagon (beta-blocker), vasopressors, atropine, lipid emulsion, ECMO.
- **Sepsis mimics and others:** sodium channel blockers (tricyclics), opioids, clonidine, digoxin, cyanide (lactic acidosis with normal SaO₂: hydroxocobalamin), carbon monoxide, anaesthetic reactions, local anaesthetic toxicity (lipid emulsion), serotonin syndrome, anaphylactoid reactions to contrast and drugs, vancomycin infusion reaction.

#### Other

- **Severe anaemia and haemolysis,** **acute pancreatitis** (third-space losses and inflammation), **hepatic failure** (vasodilatation), **myxoedema coma, thyroid storm,** **post-cardiac-surgery vasoplegia,** **burns.**

### 38.8 Monitoring, endpoints and complications

#### Monitoring

- **Clinical:** mental state, capillary refill, skin temperature, mottling, urine output (hourly), blood pressure (arterial line), heart rate.
- **Biochemical:** serial lactate (every 2–4 hours until improving), blood gases, creatinine, electrolytes, glucose, haemoglobin, coagulation, troponin.
- **Haemodynamic:** echocardiography (repeat), cardiac output monitoring (pulse contour analysis, thermodilution, oesophageal Doppler), central venous pressure (limited value), ScvO₂, venous-arterial CO₂ gap.
- **Fluid balance:** daily weights, fluid balance charts.

#### Resuscitation endpoints (guidance only)

MAP ≥65 mmHg, capillary refill ≤3 seconds, urine output >0.5 mL/kg/hour, improving mental state, **falling lactate (≥10–20% per 2 hours),** no mottling, ScvO₂ >70% where measured. **Do not chase normal numbers** (blood pressure, lactate) at the expense of harm.

#### Complications

- **Acute kidney injury** (renal replacement as required), **ARDS and respiratory failure** (lung-protective ventilation), **DIC and coagulopathy,** **ischaemic hepatitis,** **ischaemic bowel,** **stress ulceration and GI bleeding,** **myocardial ischaemia,** **adrenal insufficiency,** **hyperglycaemia,** **delirium,** **ICU-acquired weakness,** **secondary infection,** **multi-organ dysfunction syndrome (MODS),** and long-term sequelae (post-intensive care syndrome).
- **Mortality** depends on the cause, delay to treatment, age, and comorbidity (septic shock about 30–40% in-hospital; cardiogenic shock 40–50%; haemorrhagic shock depends on control; anaphylaxis low if treated promptly).

### 38.9 Special populations

- **Children:** compensate extremely well until sudden collapse; **tachycardia, poor perfusion, altered behaviour** are early signs; **hypotension is a pre-terminal event.** Use age-appropriate vital signs and weight-based treatment: fluid bolus 10–20 mL/kg isotonic crystalloid, reassess; early vasoactive support (adrenaline for cold shock, noradrenaline for warm shock); treat hypoglycaemia; consider sepsis, dehydration, anaphylaxis, cardiogenic (myocarditis, congenital heart disease), non-accidental trauma (Chapter 59).
- **Pregnancy:** blood volume is expanded by about 50%, so **up to 30–35% blood loss may occur before maternal signs;** the fetus is compromised early. **Left lateral tilt;** early blood and tranexamic acid; consider obstetric causes: ectopic, abruption, placenta praevia, postpartum haemorrhage, uterine rupture, amniotic fluid embolism, eclampsia; cardiac causes (peripartum cardiomyopathy), PE.
- **Older adults:** reduced physiological reserve, blunted heart rate response (beta-blockers), frequent comorbidities, delayed recognition; hypotension may be relative to baseline hypertension; cognitive changes as early sign; consider abdominal catastrophe, MI, sepsis; discuss goals of care.
- **Athletes and young adults:** can maintain a near-normal BP until >30% blood loss; **tachycardia and narrow pulse pressure** are clues.
- **Patients on beta-blockers or pacemakers:** absent tachycardia; rely on other signs.
- **Obesity:** difficult access and monitoring; BP cuffs inaccurate.

### 38.10 Worked cases

#### Case 1: Haemorrhagic shock

*A 28-year-old man is stabbed in the thigh. HR 138, BP 80/50, cold and clammy, RR 30, anxious. Femoral wound pulsatile bleeding.*

**Reasoning.** **Class III–IV haemorrhagic shock** (hypovolaemic).

**Action.** **Direct pressure and tourniquet** to stop bleeding, oxygen, two large-bore IVs, activate the massive transfusion protocol (balanced blood products), tranexamic acid, permissive hypotension, warm the patient, call surgery and anaesthesia, damage-control surgery; monitor lactate and coagulation.

#### Case 2: Cardiogenic shock

*A 63-year-old woman with 6 hours of chest pain presents with BP 78/50, HR 110, cold clammy skin, raised JVP, bibasal crackles, and anterior ST elevation. Lactate 5.2.*

**Reasoning.** **Cardiogenic shock** complicating anterior STEMI.

**Action.** Oxygen if hypoxic, emergency **primary PCI** with revascularisation, noradrenaline for MAP ≥65, dobutamine if low output persists, cautious diuretics, echocardiography, mechanical circulatory support (Impella or ECMO) if refractory, avoid large fluid volumes.

#### Case 3: Obstructive shock: tamponade

*A 55-year-old man 3 days after cardiac surgery is hypotensive (BP 75/50), tachycardic, JVP raised, heart sounds muffled, with a falling urine output and reduced drain output.*

**Reasoning.** **Post-operative tamponade** (obstructive shock).

**Action.** Urgent echocardiography (loculated effusion), call the cardiothoracic team: **re-exploration and drainage** (pericardiocentesis may be inadequate for clot), volume loading as a bridge, avoid vasodilators and positive pressure.

#### Case 4: Septic (distributive) shock

*A 70-year-old woman with a urinary source has fever, confusion, RR 28, HR 118, BP 82/38, warm peripheries, lactate 4.1 despite 30 mL/kg crystalloid.*

**Reasoning.** **Septic shock.**

**Action.** Antibiotics already given within the hour, source control (relieve obstruction), **noradrenaline** to MAP ≥65, vasopressin if escalating, hydrocortisone, ICU, repeat lactate, cultures (Chapter 39).

#### Case 5: Neurogenic shock

*A 24-year-old man falls from a ladder with a cervical spine fracture, is flaccid with sensory loss below C6. BP 76/40, HR 48, warm dry skin. FAST scan and chest and pelvic X-rays negative.*

**Reasoning.** **Neurogenic shock** (haemorrhage excluded).

**Action.** Spinal immobilisation, modest fluids, **noradrenaline** for MAP ≥85 mmHg, atropine for bradycardia, prevent hypothermia, ICU, spinal surgery assessment.

#### Case 6: Adrenal crisis

*A 52-year-old woman with rheumatoid arthritis on prednisolone 10 mg/day, who stopped it 5 days ago, develops vomiting, abdominal pain, confusion, BP 70/40, glucose 2.8, Na 126, K 5.9.*

**Reasoning.** **Adrenal crisis** following abrupt steroid withdrawal.

**Action.** **Hydrocortisone 100 mg IV immediately,** IV saline, glucose, monitor potassium and ECG, treat precipitating infection, endocrine input, stress dosing and steroid card.

### 38.11 Common pitfalls

- **Waiting for hypotension** to diagnose shock.
- **Treating shock as one entity** (e.g. giving large fluid boluses to a patient in cardiogenic shock or tamponade).
- **Missing the cause** (bleeding, tension pneumothorax, tamponade, adrenal crisis, anaphylaxis).
- **Under-resuscitating (inadequate blood in haemorrhage) or over-resuscitating** (excessive crystalloid dilutes clotting factors, worsens acidosis and oedema).
- **Using only CVP** to guide fluids.
- **Delaying vasopressors** in profound hypotension until "enough fluid."
- **Using dopamine as first-line** or high-dose adrenaline as routine.
- **Intubating an unresuscitated patient** (induction-induced arrest).
- **Assuming that bradycardia means the patient is stable** (neurogenic shock, beta-blockers, late hypovolaemia, heart block).
- **Missing mixed shock.**
- **Assuming trauma hypotension is neurogenic** without excluding haemorrhage.
- **Not looking for the source of sepsis** or delaying antibiotics.
- **Withholding steroids** in suspected adrenal crisis while awaiting cortisol.
- **Neglecting to re-assess** and adjust.
- **Failing to escalate to ICU** or discuss goals of care.

---

### 38.12 Key points

- Shock = inadequate tissue oxygen delivery; recognise it by perfusion (mental state, skin, capillary refill, urine output, lactate), not by blood pressure alone.
- Four types: hypovolaemic, cardiogenic, obstructive, distributive; use temperature, JVP, lungs, pulse character, history and ultrasound (RUSH) to differentiate.
- Treat as you assess: oxygen, access, monitoring, cause-directed therapy, fluids with dynamic reassessment, early vasopressors (noradrenaline first-line), inotropes for low output.
- Haemorrhage: stop the bleeding, balanced blood products, tranexamic acid, permissive hypotension, avoid hypothermia and coagulopathy.
- Obstructive shock needs relief of the obstruction; cardiogenic shock needs reperfusion and support; avoid excessive fluids.
- Neurogenic shock (warm, bradycardic) after spinal injury is a diagnosis of exclusion in trauma; adrenal crisis requires immediate hydrocortisone.
- Monitor lactate, urine output, perfusion; target MAP ≥65; avoid over-resuscitation; call ICU early.

### 38.13 Self-assessment

1. Write the equation for oxygen delivery and name three ways it can fail.
   *Answer: DO₂ = cardiac output × arterial oxygen content (Hb × 1.34 × SaO₂ + dissolved O₂); it fails through low cardiac output (preload, contractility, rate/rhythm, afterload), low haemoglobin or hypoxaemia, or both.*

2. Differentiate the JVP and extremities in hypovolaemic, cardiogenic, obstructive, and distributive shock.
   *Answer: Hypovolaemic: low JVP, cold; cardiogenic: high JVP, cold, crackles; obstructive: high JVP, cold, clear lungs (or absent breath sounds); distributive: low/normal JVP, warm with bounding pulse (early).*

3. What is the first-line vasopressor in most shock states, and what MAP target?
   *Answer: Noradrenaline; MAP ≥65 mmHg (adjusted for chronic hypertension and clinical context).*

4. Outline damage-control resuscitation in haemorrhagic shock.
   *Answer: Haemorrhage control, balanced blood products (≈1:1:1), tranexamic acid, calcium, avoid hypothermia/acidosis/coagulopathy, limited crystalloid, permissive hypotension (except head or spinal injury), early surgery.*

5. How is neurogenic shock distinguished from haemorrhagic shock, and treated?
   *Answer: Neurogenic shock: warm dry skin, bradycardia, wide pulse pressure, spinal injury above T6; haemorrhage must be excluded first; treated with modest fluids, noradrenaline/phenylephrine (MAP ≥85 in spinal injury), atropine for bradycardia.*

6. What immediate treatment is required in suspected adrenal crisis?
   *Answer: Hydrocortisone 100 mg IV/IM at once, IV saline, correction of hypoglycaemia and electrolyte disturbances, treatment of the precipitant.*
