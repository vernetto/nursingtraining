# Chapter 74. Case-Based Learning

## Learning objectives

After studying this chapter you should be able to:

1. Use cases as a deliberate learning method: unfold a case in stages, commit to a hypothesis at each stage, and compare your reasoning with the actual outcome.
2. Build and use illness scripts, problem representations (semantic qualifiers) and "one-liners" to organise knowledge for retrieval.
3. Work an unfolding case through the full cycle: problem representation → differential → test selection → interpretation → revision → management → follow-up → reflection.
4. Recognise how cognitive biases shape a case, and apply debiasing steps at the points where errors typically occur.
5. Analyse eight complete unfolding cases that integrate content from earlier parts (history, examination, tests, scoring rules, red flags, safety-netting).
6. Write your own case reflection (structured learning log) and a case presentation suitable for teaching, morbidity and mortality review or publication.
7. Use "case-to-concept" mapping to identify gaps in knowledge and plan deliberate practice.

> **How to use this chapter.** Cover the text below each "Pause" heading and write your own answer before reading further. The cases are fictional composites; investigation results and doses are illustrative. Verify against current guidelines before applying them to patients.

---

## 1. Why cases?

Clinical expertise is largely **pattern knowledge organised around cases**. Experts do not recall isolated facts; they retrieve **illness scripts** (enabling conditions, pathophysiological fault, clinical consequences) and compare the patient against stored exemplars. Case-based learning (CBL) develops:

- **Pattern recognition** (system 1) with feedback.
- **Analytic reasoning** (system 2): hypothesis generation, probability, test choice.
- **Metacognition**: noticing when a conclusion is arrived at too fast.
- **Transfer**: using a principle (e.g., "hyponatraemia needs osmolality, volume and urine studies") across conditions.

Learning improves when the case is **unfolded**, you **predict** before seeing the next data ("generation effect"), you **receive feedback**, and you **revisit** the case after a delay (spaced retrieval).

## 2. Tools for case work

### 2.1 Problem representation and the one-liner

A strong problem representation uses **semantic qualifiers**: acute vs chronic, unilateral vs bilateral, constant vs intermittent, young vs old, healthy vs immunocompromised.

> *"A previously healthy 27-year-old woman with 3 days of acute, severe, pleuritic chest pain and dyspnoea, tachycardia, on the combined oral contraceptive pill."*

The qualifiers (acute, pleuritic, tachycardia, oestrogen) immediately prime PE, pneumothorax, pericarditis and pneumonia rather than "chest pain".

**Template**: [Age] [sex] with [relevant risk factors/comorbidities] presenting with [tempo: acute/subacute/chronic] [key symptom with qualifiers] and [key positives/negatives].

### 2.2 Illness scripts

Each diagnosis is stored as: **Enabling conditions** (risk factors, setting) → **Fault** (pathophysiology) → **Consequences** (symptoms, signs, test results, course). For example, *infective endocarditis*: enabling (valve disease, IV drug use, prosthesis, dental or skin portals, indwelling lines) → fault (bacteraemia seeding a damaged endocardium, vegetations) → consequences (fever, new murmur, embolic phenomena, immune phenomena, positive cultures, vegetation on echo).

### 2.3 Structure of a case discussion

1. **Summarise** the problem (one-liner).
2. **Generate** a differential with likelihood and "can't-miss" diagnoses.
3. **Discriminating features**: which data would separate the top diagnoses?
4. **Plan tests**, anticipating how results change probabilities.
5. **Integrate** results; revise.
6. **Decide and treat**; define safety net and follow-up.
7. **Reflect**: what drove the diagnosis, what was nearly missed, which bias nearly misled you.

### 2.4 Cognitive checkpoints in a case

| Checkpoint | Question |
|---|---|
| After the first impression | What else could this be? What is the worst case? |
| After initial tests | Did the tests move my probability, or did I just confirm my view? |
| Before closing | Does one diagnosis explain *all* the findings? What does not fit? |
| After treatment | Is the response as expected? If not, reconsider |
| At handover | What did I assume? What is still unresolved? |

## 3. Eight unfolding cases

### Case A: Fever and a new murmur

**Stage 1 – Presentation.** Mr A., 31, 10 days of fever, sweats, malaise and low back pain. He has a history of intravenous drug use ("not for a year", he says). Temperature 38.7 °C, HR 108, BP 116/68. Examination: dental caries, a soft systolic murmur at the left sternal edge, no peripheral stigmata at first glance.

> **Pause.** Write a one-liner and the top five diagnoses. Which two "can't-miss" diagnoses? Which tests first?

**Discussion.** A young adult with prolonged fever and a history of injection drug use: **infective endocarditis** (right- or left-sided), vertebral osteomyelitis/discitis (back pain), epidural abscess, HIV, TB, abscess elsewhere, viral illness. Murmur is new or undocumented. The first tests: **three sets of blood cultures** from separate sites before antibiotics (ideally ≥ 3 sets over 1 hour if stable), FBC, CRP, U&E, LFT, urinalysis (haematuria), ECG (PR prolongation suggests root abscess), CXR (septic emboli), HIV/hepatitis serology.

**Stage 2 – Results.** Blood cultures: *Staphylococcus aureus* in 3/3 bottles (MSSA). CRP 180, WBC 15, Hb 105, microscopic haematuria. CXR: multiple peripheral nodules, some cavitating. Echocardiogram (TTE): 12 mm vegetation on the tricuspid valve, moderate regurgitation.

> **Pause.** Apply the modified Duke criteria. What is the likely valve and why the lung nodules? What about the back pain?

**Discussion.** Major criteria: typical organism in cultures; echocardiographic evidence. Minor: predisposition (IVDU), fever, vascular/immunological phenomena (haematuria). **Definite endocarditis.** Right-sided endocarditis causes septic pulmonary emboli. Back pain with fever and bacteraemia needs MRI spine to exclude vertebral osteomyelitis or epidural abscess (haematogenous seeding).

**Stage 3 – Management and outcome.** Treat with IV anti-staphylococcal therapy (flucloxacillin/cloxacillin or equivalent, per guideline; vancomycin if MRSA), typically 2–6 weeks depending on the site, vegetation size and complications; MRI spine confirms L3–L4 discitis; infectious diseases and cardiology/cardiothoracic involvement; addiction services (opioid agonist therapy, harm reduction, long-term options); screen for HIV, HBV, HCV; consider surgery for heart failure, large vegetations with emboli, uncontrolled infection. Follow-up echo and cultures after treatment; dental review.

**Learning points.** Obtain cultures before antibiotics; murmurs and stigmata may be absent in right-sided disease; multiple sites of infection occur; address the substance use disorder as part of treatment.

### Case B: The confused woman with a "normal" chest X-ray

**Stage 1 – Presentation.** Mrs B., 71, smoker, 4 days of confusion, nausea and lethargy. Her daughter says she has lost 5 kg in two months. No headache. On examination: euvolaemic, no focal deficit, BP 130/80. Sodium 118 mmol/L.

> **Pause.** What data do you need to classify the hyponatraemia? What are the most likely causes in this patient?

**Discussion.** Order **serum osmolality** (exclude pseudo/hypertonic), **urine osmolality and sodium**, glucose, TSH, cortisol; assess volume status. Likely: SIADH (malignancy, pulmonary disease, drugs, CNS disease), hypothyroidism, adrenal insufficiency, thiazide use, low solute intake. In a smoker with weight loss, **small cell lung cancer** is high on the list.

**Stage 2 – Results.** Serum osmolality 248 mOsm/kg; urine osmolality 480 mOsm/kg, urine Na 64 mmol/L; TSH and cortisol normal; urea low; CXR: right hilar prominence (read as "prominent vascular markings" by the first reader). CT chest: 4 cm right hilar mass with mediastinal nodes; adrenal and liver lesions.

> **Pause.** What is your management of the sodium, and what is the diagnosis and next step?

**Discussion.** Hypotonic euvolaemic hyponatraemia with concentrated urine and high urine Na: **SIADH**. Moderate symptoms (confusion, nausea) without seizure: cautious treatment—fluid restriction, consider hypertonic saline bolus if symptoms are severe or deteriorating, limit correction to ≤ 8–10 mmol/L per 24 hours. Treat the cause: tissue diagnosis (bronchoscopy/EBUS or biopsy of an accessible lesion) → small cell lung cancer, extensive stage; oncology and palliative care input.

**Learning points.** Hyponatraemia has a differential that is solved with three data points (osmolality, volume, urine studies). The first CXR read did not exclude malignancy; hilar abnormality warranted CT. Weight loss + smoker = cancer until excluded.

### Case C: Abdominal pain in a young man

**Stage 1 – Presentation.** Mr C., 22, a student, 2 days of nausea, vomiting and diffuse abdominal pain, plus thirst and urinating frequently over the last 2 weeks; he lost 5 kg. Temperature 37.4 °C, HR 118, BP 100/62, RR 30 and deep, dry mucous membranes, abdominal tenderness without peritonism.

> **Pause.** Which investigation do you do within five minutes? What are your top diagnoses?

**Discussion.** Deep, rapid breathing (Kussmaul), polyuria/polydipsia and weight loss: **new-onset type 1 diabetes with DKA** until proven otherwise, though abdominal pathology (appendicitis, pancreatitis) can coexist or be precipitated. Immediate: capillary glucose and ketones, venous blood gas, U&E, lactate, ECG, urinalysis, cultures if febrile.

**Stage 2 – Results.** Glucose 32 mmol/L; blood β-hydroxybutyrate 6.2 mmol/L; venous pH 7.08; bicarbonate 7 mmol/L; K⁺ 5.4 mmol/L; Na⁺ 131; lipase mildly raised (2 × ULN); WBC 18; CRP 12.

> **Pause.** Outline your first hour. What would make you worry about hypokalaemia? Why not blame the abdominal pain on pancreatitis?

**Discussion.** Management: IV 0.9% saline (e.g., 1 L over 1 hour, then per protocol), fixed-rate IV insulin infusion (0.1 units/kg/h) once potassium ≥ 3.5 mmol/L, add potassium to fluids when K < 5.5 and urine output established (K will fall with insulin and correction of acidosis), replace glucose when < 14 mmol/L, monitor hourly glucose/ketones, 2–4-hourly K/venous gas; search for the trigger (infection, new diagnosis, missed insulin); continue basal insulin; watch for cerebral oedema (rare in young adults, more in children) and hypoglycaemia. Abdominal pain in DKA is common (up to 30–40%) and resolves with treatment; mild lipase elevations occur without pancreatitis. If pain persists after the acidosis resolves, reassess for appendicitis, pancreatitis, or other pathology.

**Learning points.** Ketones and a blood gas within minutes; diagnose DKA by the triad (hyperglycaemia, ketosis, acidosis); treat fluid, insulin, potassium; educate; arrange diabetes team and education before discharge.

### Case D: Headache and blurred vision in a woman on the pill

**Stage 1 – Presentation.** Ms D., 29, BMI 34, takes the combined oral contraceptive. 10 days of progressively worsening headache, worse lying down and in the morning, vomiting twice, and transient visual obscurations on standing. Neurological examination: no focal deficit; fundoscopy shows bilateral blurred disc margins.

> **Pause.** What does papilloedema tell you? List your differential and the first investigation.

**Discussion.** Papilloedema = raised intracranial pressure. Differential: **cerebral venous thrombosis (CVT)** (oestrogen, obesity), idiopathic intracranial hypertension (IIH), mass lesion, hydrocephalus, meningitis, drug-induced (tetracyclines, vitamin A, steroid withdrawal). **Neuroimaging before lumbar puncture**: urgent MRI/MR venography (or CT venography); CT head first if MRI not available to exclude mass.

**Stage 2 – Results.** CT head: normal. MR venography: filling defect in the superior sagittal and right transverse sinus; no infarct.

> **Pause.** What is the diagnosis, treatment and what additional work-up?

**Discussion.** **Cerebral venous sinus thrombosis.** Treatment: anticoagulation (LMWH then warfarin or a DOAC per guideline) even in the presence of haemorrhagic transformation (if stable); stop oestrogen contraception permanently; ophthalmic assessment (visual fields, acetazolamide if visual threat); treat seizures if present; thrombophilia testing selectively (not during the acute phase for some tests); consider ICP-lowering measures; neurologist/haematologist; counselling on future contraception (progestogen-only, copper IUD), pregnancy and risk.

**Learning points.** The cause of papilloedema must be found; in a young woman with oestrogen, think of CVT; MRV/CTV is essential; LP before imaging is dangerous.

### Case E: Back pain that wasn't mechanical

**Stage 1 – Presentation.** Mr E., 66, 3 months of worsening mid-back pain, worse at night, fatigue, constipation and recurrent chest infections. BP 128/76. Examination: spinal tenderness at T9, no neurology.

> **Pause.** Which red flags are present? What basic tests?

**Discussion.** Age > 50, night pain, weight loss/fatigue and recurrent infections raise suspicion for malignancy (myeloma, metastases) and infection. Constipation + fatigue + back pain suggest **hypercalcaemia**. Basic tests: FBC, film, ESR, U&E/creatinine, calcium (corrected), albumin, ALP, LDH, serum protein electrophoresis with immunofixation and serum free light chains, urine Bence Jones protein or free light chains, spine imaging (MRI whole spine).

**Stage 2 – Results.** Hb 92 g/L, normocytic; ESR 118; creatinine 190 µmol/L (baseline unknown); corrected calcium 2.95 mmol/L; total protein 94 g/L with albumin 31 (raised globulin gap 63 g/L); serum electrophoresis: IgG kappa paraprotein 42 g/L; free light chains markedly elevated; MRI spine: multiple lytic lesions and a T9 vertebral compression fracture without cord compression; skeletal survey: "punched-out" lesions.

> **Pause.** Which CRAB features are present? What are the immediate actions?

**Discussion.** **Multiple myeloma** with CRAB features (hyper**C**alcaemia, **R**enal impairment, **A**naemia, **B**one lesions). Immediate: IV hydration, avoid nephrotoxins (NSAIDs, contrast), treat hypercalcaemia (fluids, bisphosphonate with caution for renal function or denosumab), analgesia, bone protection, urgent haematology referral for marrow biopsy and cytogenetics, start anti-myeloma therapy; vigilance for cord compression (neurology check), hyperviscosity, infection; vaccination, VTE prophylaxis based on regimen; palliative and supportive care in parallel.

**Learning points.** A "back pain" with constitutional symptoms needs blood tests; hypercalcaemia and renal impairment are clues; the total protein/albumin gap can hint at a paraprotein.

### Case F: Syncope during a football match

**Stage 1 – Presentation.** Mr F., 19, collapsed 20 minutes into a match while sprinting, recovered within a minute, but was confused for a short time. His father died suddenly at 41 "of a heart attack". Examination: normal; BP 118/72; murmur: systolic ejection murmur that increases on standing.

> **Pause.** Is this vasovagal? Which features are high-risk? What should be done before he returns to sport?

**Discussion.** Exertional syncope and a family history of sudden death are high-risk features; the murmur that increases with Valsalva/standing suggests **hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM)** with outflow obstruction. Other causes: arrhythmogenic RV cardiomyopathy, long QT/catecholaminergic polymorphic VT, congenital coronary anomalies, myocarditis, WPW, Brugada. Initial investigations: 12-lead ECG, echocardiography, ambulatory rhythm monitoring, exercise test, cardiac MRI; genetic counselling.

**Stage 2 – Results.** ECG: LVH voltage with deep inferior and lateral T-wave inversion and deep narrow Q waves. Echo: asymmetric septal hypertrophy 24 mm, systolic anterior motion of the mitral valve, LVOT gradient 60 mmHg on provocation. Holter: non-sustained VT.

> **Pause.** What is the management and the risk of sudden death? What about the family?

**Discussion.** HCM with high-risk features (exertional syncope, non-sustained VT, family history of sudden death, massive hypertrophy ≥ 30 mm): **ICD for primary prevention** is considered (use the HCM Risk-SCD calculator); avoid competitive sport and intense exertion in line with expert guidance (shared decision-making is increasingly emphasised), beta-blocker for symptom relief, avoid dehydration and vasodilators; consider myosin inhibitors (mavacamten) or septal reduction for refractory obstruction; **cascade screening of first-degree relatives** (ECG, echo, genetic testing); psychological support.

**Learning points.** Do not label exertional syncope "vasovagal"; take a family history of sudden death; ECG is abnormal in about 90% of HCM; screen family members.

### Case G: The confident wrong label

**Stage 1 – Presentation.** Mrs G., 84, lives alone, found on the floor at home, confused, with a urine dipstick positive for leukocytes and nitrite. The ambulance crew handover: "UTI, probably septic, falls." In the ED, a trainee starts IV antibiotics. T 37.2 °C, HR 78, BP 150/90. She is drowsy; GCS 13.

> **Pause.** Which cognitive biases are already operating? What would you do differently?

**Discussion.** Biases: **anchoring and diagnostic momentum** (the handover label "UTI"), **premature closure**, **availability** (UTI as the default cause of confusion in the elderly), **framing**. Asymptomatic bacteriuria is common in older women, and a positive dipstick does not explain a fall with reduced GCS. A fall plus drowsiness requires considering intracranial haemorrhage (subdural), stroke, hypoglycaemia, hypotension, arrhythmia, fracture, dehydration, medication effects, and rhabdomyolysis after a "long lie".

**Stage 2 – The review.** A senior doctor re-takes the history: she is on apixaban; her daughter reports a head injury a week ago. Examination: scalp haematoma, mild left-sided weakness, fluctuating GCS. CT head: right subacute-on-acute subdural haematoma with 8 mm midline shift. INR/anti-Xa confirms anticoagulant effect.

> **Pause.** What are the immediate steps?

**Discussion.** Reverse anticoagulation (andexanet alfa or four-factor PCC per local protocol), neurosurgical referral, ICU/HDU monitoring, blood pressure control, seizure prophylaxis if indicated, stop antibiotics unless true infection is evident; capacity and goals of care discussions with family, rehabilitation plan; falls assessment; reassess the need for anticoagulation (shared decision).

**Learning points.** A label received at handover is a hypothesis; re-take the history and examine; the combination "fall + anticoagulant + drowsiness" mandates CT head; avoid treating asymptomatic bacteriuria.

### Case H: Fatigue, joint pain and a red rash

**Stage 1 – Presentation.** Ms H., 26, 6 weeks of fatigue, symmetric joint pain in hands and wrists, a facial rash after sun exposure, mouth ulcers, and hair thinning. She noticed ankle swelling this week. BP 148/92. Dipstick: protein ++, blood ++.

> **Pause.** What is your leading diagnosis and what makes the picture urgent?

**Discussion.** The pattern—photosensitive malar rash, oral ulcers, inflammatory arthritis, fatigue, and now **hypertension with proteinuria and haematuria**—suggests **systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) with lupus nephritis**. Urgent features: renal involvement (can lead to irreversible damage), possible CNS or haematological involvement. Investigations: FBC (cytopenias), creatinine/eGFR, urine PCR/ACR and microscopy (red cell casts), ANA, anti-dsDNA, anti-Sm, ENA, complement C3/C4 (low in active disease), antiphospholipid antibodies, direct Coombs, CRP/ESR (ESR high, CRP often normal), hepatitis/HIV screen before immunosuppression, pregnancy test; renal ultrasound; refer for renal biopsy.

**Stage 2 – Results.** ANA 1:1280 homogeneous; anti-dsDNA strongly positive; C3 and C4 low; Hb 98 g/L with positive DAT; lymphocytes 0.8; platelets 110; creatinine 130 µmol/L; urine PCR 480 mg/mmol; RBC casts. Biopsy: class IV lupus nephritis.

> **Pause.** What treatment and what monitoring?

**Discussion.** Induction with high-dose glucocorticoids (IV methylprednisolone pulses then oral prednisolone) plus mycophenolate or cyclophosphamide (or combination with belimumab/voclosporin per guideline), hydroxychloroquine for all patients with SLE (annual eye screening), ACE inhibitor/ARB for proteinuria and blood pressure, contraception counselling (avoid teratogens such as mycophenolate in pregnancy; plan pregnancy when disease quiet), vaccinations, bone protection, infection prophylaxis (PJP if high-dose therapy), sun protection, screening for cardiovascular risk and antiphospholipid syndrome. Monitor disease activity (SLEDAI), urine PCR, complement, dsDNA, creatinine regularly.

**Learning points.** Classification criteria (EULAR/ACR 2019) integrate ANA entry criterion and weighted domains; renal involvement drives urgency; early treatment reduces long-term damage.

## 4. Reflecting on cases

### 4.1 Structured reflection (learning log template)

1. **Case summary** (one-liner, outcome).
2. **My initial hypotheses** (and probabilities).
3. **Key discriminating data** and when they arrived.
4. **Where my reasoning was right / wrong / fortunate.**
5. **Cognitive biases or system factors involved.**
6. **Knowledge gaps** discovered (list with learning actions).
7. **What I would do differently.**
8. **Patient impact and communication** (what patient/family understood).
9. **Teaching point** in one sentence.

### 4.2 Presenting a case

- **Brief**: one-liner, relevant data, problem list, assessment, plan.
- **Teaching case**: unfold in stages; invite predictions; reveal the diagnosis late; end with take-home points.
- **Morbidity and mortality review**: factual timeline; contributing factors (patient, task, individual, team, environment, organisation); non-blaming; actions with owners and dates.
- **Case report** (publication): consent, anonymisation, novelty, learning value, references (CARE guidelines).

### 4.3 Spaced and interleaved practice

Return to the same cases after 1 week, 1 month and 3 months, with a **different framing** (e.g., change one detail: the patient is 80 and on steroids). Mix cases from different systems in one session (interleaving) to practise discrimination between look-alike diagnoses.

## Common pitfalls in case-based learning

1. **Reading the answer too early** instead of committing to a hypothesis.
2. **Learning only classic presentations**; neglecting atypical ones and mimics.
3. **Hindsight bias**: believing "I would have known".
4. **Focusing on the diagnosis only**; skipping management, communication, follow-up.
5. **Not revisiting** cases (no spacing).
6. **Ignoring psychosocial and ethical dimensions.**
7. **Using only cases from one specialty**; lack of interleaving.
8. **Failing to translate** lessons into practice change (checklist, personal rule, further reading).

---

## Key points

- Cases build illness scripts and reasoning when you commit to predictions before feedback.
- A precise one-liner with semantic qualifiers drives better differentials.
- At each step, ask what else, what is the worst case, and what does not fit.
- The eight unfolding cases illustrate common diagnostic traps: missed endocarditis, hyponatraemia with occult cancer, abdominal pain from DKA, CVT with papilloedema, myeloma behind back pain, exertional syncope in HCM, anchoring on UTI, SLE nephritis.
- Reflection, spaced retrieval and interleaving convert experience into expertise.
- Document learning actions and follow through.

**Answers**

1. A semantic qualifier is a paired descriptive term (acute/chronic, unilateral/bilateral, young/old) used to abstract the case; it improves pattern matching and narrows the differential.
2. "A 66-year-old man with 3 months of progressive nocturnal mid-back pain, fatigue, constipation, recurrent chest infections and spinal tenderness, suggesting a systemic cause such as malignancy."
3. Bacteraemia in endocarditis is continuous, but multiple sets increase sensitivity and help distinguish true infection from contamination; antibiotics sterilise cultures and reduce yield.
4. Serum osmolality, volume status, urine osmolality and urine sodium (plus glucose, lipids/protein, TSH, cortisol).
5. Ketosis, dehydration, gastric stasis and electrolyte shifts cause abdominal pain in 30–40% of DKA patients; suspect separate pathology if pain persists or worsens after acidosis resolves, if localised peritonism, fever or very high lipase/amylase develop.
6. MR venography or CT venography (with MRI or CT head); imaging precedes LP because raised ICP from a mass or CVT can cause herniation and LP may also alter management.
7. Hypercalcaemia, Renal impairment, Anaemia, Bone lesions (lytic lesions, fractures).
8. Exertional syncope, family history of sudden death, murmur increasing with provocation, ECG with LVH and deep T inversions, non-sustained VT, massive hypertrophy; screen first-degree relatives with ECG, echo and genetic counselling/testing.
9. Anchoring/diagnostic momentum (handover label), premature closure, availability (UTI as default), framing, and possibly confirmation bias.
10. Hypertension, proteinuria, haematuria, oedema with SLE features (rash, arthritis, ulcers); investigations: urine PCR/microscopy, creatinine, complement, dsDNA, renal biopsy to determine class and guide treatment.
