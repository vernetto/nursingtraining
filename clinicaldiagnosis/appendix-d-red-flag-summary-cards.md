# Appendix D: Red Flag Summary Cards

> **How to use these cards.** Each card is a one-page prompt for the moment of uncertainty: *what must I not miss, what do I ask or examine, what do I do next?* Cards support reasoning; they do not replace it. A red flag raises the probability of serious disease; its absence never excludes it (Chapters 72 and 73). Thresholds and management steps are teaching-level; follow current local protocols and escalate early.

**Universal rules that sit above every card**

1. **Look at the patient before the numbers.** "Looks unwell" is a finding (Chapters 10, 11 and 37).
2. **Trend beats snapshot.** Two abnormal observations that are worsening matter more than one fixed value.
3. **New, sudden, severe, progressive, or "worst ever"** are red-flag words in nearly every presentation.
4. **Extremes of age, immunosuppression, pregnancy, and recent surgery or procedures lower the threshold** for concern and for investigation.
5. **If you are worried, say so, escalate, and document the reason.** Safety-net every discharge (Chapter 73).
6. **Reassess after treatment.** Failure to respond is a diagnostic clue.

## Card 1: The sick patient (ABCDE) — Chapters 37, 38, 44

**Red flags:** Airway noise, stridor, drooling; RR ≥25 or <8; SpO₂ <92% (or below the patient's target); HR >130 or <40; SBP <90 or fall >40 from baseline; shock index >1; cold, mottled, prolonged capillary refill (>3 s); new confusion or drowsiness; GCS drop ≥2; glucose <3.0 mmol/L; no urine for 6–8 h; temperature <36 °C or >39 °C with rigors; non-blanching rash; "I feel like I'm going to die."

**Do now:** Call for help; treat life threats as you find them (A before B before C); oxygen to target; IV/IO access; bloods including lactate, gas, glucose, cultures; ECG; fluids if hypovolaemic; early senior or critical-care review.

**Don't:** Assume tachycardia is anxiety; wait for a result before treating obvious shock; rely on a normal BP in young, fit or compensating patients.

## Card 2: Chest pain — Chapter 22

**Can't-miss:** Acute coronary syndrome, aortic dissection, pulmonary embolism, tension pneumothorax, oesophageal rupture, pericarditis with tamponade.

**Red flags:** Pain with exertion or radiation to arm/jaw; sweating, nausea, breathlessness; syncope; tearing pain to the back; pulse or BP differential between arms (>20 mmHg); new aortic regurgitation murmur; haemodynamic compromise; hypoxia; unilateral calf swelling; recent surgery, immobilisation or cancer; Boerhaave setting (forceful vomiting); known coronary disease with a change in pattern.

**Do now:** ECG within 10 minutes; repeat if symptoms change; high-sensitivity troponin per pathway; chest X-ray; bedside echo if unstable; pre-test probability (Wells/PERC, Chapter 70); consider CT aortography/CTPA by risk.

**Don't:** Dismiss "atypical" pain in women, diabetics and older adults; stop at one normal ECG; anchor on reproducible chest wall tenderness.

---

## Card 3: Dyspnoea and cough — Chapter 23, 40

**Can't-miss:** Pulmonary embolism, acute heart failure, tension pneumothorax, severe asthma, anaphylaxis, pneumonia/sepsis, airway obstruction, ACS.

**Red flags:** Unable to complete sentences; silent chest; accessory muscle use, tripod position; RR ≥30; SpO₂ <92%; cyanosis; stridor; drowsiness or confusion (CO₂ retention or hypoxia); haemoptysis (massive >100–200 mL/24 h); unilateral absent breath sounds with tracheal deviation; orthopnoea with raised JVP; chronic cough >3 weeks with weight loss, night sweats or TB contact.

**Do now:** Sit upright, oxygen to target (88–92% if at risk of hypercapnia), peak flow if asthma, ECG, chest X-ray, blood gas, bedside ultrasound.

**Don't:** Withhold oxygen from a hypoxic patient because of COPD; use a normal PaCO₂ in severe asthma as reassurance.

---

## Card 4: Headache and dizziness — Chapter 24, 41

**Can't-miss:** Subarachnoid haemorrhage, meningitis/encephalitis, stroke (including posterior circulation), giant cell arteritis, raised intracranial pressure, cerebral venous thrombosis, carbon monoxide poisoning, acute angle-closure glaucoma.

**Red flags (headache):** Thunderclap onset (peak <1 min); worst ever; fever and neck stiffness; focal deficit; papilloedema; new headache >50 years with jaw claudication or scalp tenderness; onset with exertion, coitus or Valsalva; pregnancy/postpartum; immunosuppression; cancer; head injury; progressive over weeks; positional (worse lying, on waking).

**Red flags (dizziness):** Inability to walk or sit unaided; new severe headache or neck pain; vertical or direction-changing nystagmus; negative head impulse test with acute vestibular syndrome (central pattern, "HINTS"); diplopia, dysarthria, limb ataxia; vascular risk factors with abrupt onset.

**Do now:** Neurological examination and fundi; CT head (non-contrast) within 6 hours for suspected SAH, then lumbar puncture/CT angiography per local pathway; urgent bloods (ESR/CRP for GCA); treat suspected bacterial meningitis immediately.

**Don't:** Delay antibiotics for imaging in suspected meningitis; diagnose "migraine" for a first, sudden, severe headache without excluding SAH; trust a normal CT beyond 6 hours without further evaluation.

---

## Card 5: Abdominal pain — Chapter 25, 42

**Can't-miss:** Ruptured abdominal aortic aneurysm, perforation, mesenteric ischaemia, bowel obstruction/volvulus, appendicitis, ectopic pregnancy, ovarian/testicular torsion, pancreatitis, DKA, ACS (inferior MI), strangulated hernia.

**Red flags:** Peritonism (rigidity, guarding, rebound, pain on movement/coughing); shock; pain out of proportion to examination (mesenteric ischaemia); known AF or vasculopathy; pulsatile mass or flank/back pain in an older adult; absolute constipation with distension and vomiting; blood PR; fever with rigors; positive pregnancy test or missed period; irreducible tender hernia; elderly, steroid or immunosuppressed (muted signs).

**Do now:** Vital signs and shock index; examine hernial orifices and genitalia; pregnancy test in all women of reproductive age; bloods incl. lactate, lipase, group-and-screen; imaging by presentation (ultrasound, CT); early surgical review.

**Don't:** Withhold analgesia for fear of masking signs (adequate analgesia does not obscure the diagnosis); assume gastroenteritis in an older adult with abdominal pain; discharge a first-time "colic" without considering aortic pathology.

---

## Card 6: Vomiting, diarrhoea, constipation — Chapter 26

**Red flags:** Bilious or faeculent vomiting; projectile vomiting in an infant (pyloric stenosis); haematemesis; blood or mucus in stool with fever; signs of dehydration (hypotension, reduced urine, sunken eyes); severe abdominal pain; diarrhoea after antibiotics, hospital stay or PPI (C. difficile); recent foreign travel; new constipation >50 years with weight loss, anaemia or rectal bleeding; absent flatus; neurological signs (spinal cord compression); hypercalcaemia; opioids.

**Do now:** Hydration status, electrolytes, renal function, glucose, ketones, lactate; stool cultures when indicated; imaging for obstruction; stop offending drugs.

**Don't:** Forget DKA, adrenal crisis, raised ICP and pregnancy as causes of vomiting.

---

## Card 7: GI bleeding and jaundice — Chapter 27

**Red flags (bleeding):** Haematemesis, melaena, large-volume fresh blood PR; haemodynamic instability; known varices, liver disease or anticoagulant use; syncope; age >60 with comorbidity; Hb fall >20 g/L; rising urea (upper GI source).

**Red flags (jaundice):** Fever and rigors (cholangitis); confusion or flap; coagulopathy; painless progressive jaundice with weight loss (malignancy); pale stools/dark urine; recent paracetamol or new drugs; pregnancy; sickle cell disease; haemolysis (low Hb, raised reticulocytes).

**Do now:** Two large-bore IV lines, bloods with cross-match, restore volume with blood early if unstable (restrictive transfusion otherwise), stop anticoagulants, urgent endoscopy per risk; liver ultrasound; sepsis bundle for cholangitis.

**Don't:** Rely on initial haemoglobin (it lags); omit rectal examination.

---

## Card 8: Palpitations, syncope, collapse — Chapter 28

**Can't-miss:** Ventricular arrhythmia, complete heart block, aortic stenosis, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, pulmonary embolism, long-QT/Brugada, aortic dissection, ruptured ectopic pregnancy, GI bleed, hypoglycaemia.

**Red flags:** Syncope during exertion or lying down; absent prodrome; palpitations before syncope; family history of sudden death <40 years; known structural heart disease or heart failure; ECG abnormalities (QTc >480 ms, Brugada pattern, pre-excitation, bifascicular block, Q waves, T-wave inversion V1–V3, bradycardia <40); chest pain, dyspnoea or severe headache with syncope; age >60; systolic BP <90; haematocrit <30%; new murmur; injury with syncope.

**Do now:** 12-lead ECG for every syncope; lying and standing BP; glucose; echo for structural disease; monitoring; apply risk rule (Chapters 70 and 28).

**Don't:** Label "vasovagal" without a typical trigger and prodrome; ignore the ECG because the patient is young.

---

## Card 9: Back pain and joint pain — Chapter 29

**Can't-miss:** Cauda equina syndrome, spinal cord compression, vertebral infection, fracture, aortic aneurysm/dissection, septic arthritis, malignancy, giant cell arteritis/polymyalgia.

**Red flags (back):** Urinary retention/incontinence, saddle anaesthesia, bilateral leg weakness, new bowel incontinence, reduced anal tone; progressive neurological deficit; fever, injecting drug use, immunosuppression; known cancer, weight loss, night pain; trauma, osteoporosis, steroid use; age <20 or >55 at onset; thoracic pain.

**Red flags (joint):** Hot, swollen, single joint with fever (septic arthritis until proven otherwise); inability to bear weight or move passively; recent joint surgery or prosthesis; rash, uveitis, urethritis (reactive/spondyloarthropathy); tophi or acute gout in the first MTP; prolonged morning stiffness >1 h; systemic features (lupus, vasculitis).

**Do now:** Examine neurology including perianal sensation; urgent MRI for suspected cord/cauda equina; joint aspiration before antibiotics when septic arthritis is suspected; cultures, CRP, ESR, urate.

**Don't:** Miss urinary retention (check bladder scan); give intra-articular steroid in suspected infection.

---

## Card 10: Weight loss, fatigue, lumps, oedema — Chapters 30 and 31

**Red flags:** Unintentional weight loss >5% in 6 months or >10% ever; night sweats; fever; dysphagia; change in bowel habit; iron-deficiency anaemia in men or post-menopausal women; hard, fixed, painless lump; lymph nodes >2 cm, supraclavicular or generalised; hepatosplenomegaly; new unilateral leg swelling; oedema with breathlessness, orthopnoea, raised JVP; periorbital oedema with haematuria/proteinuria; ascites; testicular lump.

**Do now:** Full examination including lymph nodes, breast, rectal, testes; bloods incl. FBC, ferritin, CRP, LFTs, TSH, glucose; urine dipstick; targeted imaging; urgent referral under suspected-cancer pathways.

**Don't:** Attribute weight loss to depression or ageing without investigating.

---

## Card 11: Urinary symptoms and haematuria — Chapter 32

**Red flags:** Painless visible haematuria (any age; cancer until proven otherwise); haematuria with loin pain and fever (obstructed infected kidney: urgent decompression); anuria; urinary retention; flank pain in an older adult with hypotension (aortic aneurysm); testicular pain in young males (torsion); pregnancy with UTI symptoms; fever in a child with urinary symptoms; recurrent infections in males; haematuria with hypertension and oedema (glomerular).

**Do now:** Dipstick, culture, renal function, bladder scan, ultrasound, CT KUB as indicated; urology review.

**Don't:** Treat asymptomatic bacteriuria in non-pregnant adults; attribute haematuria to anticoagulants alone.

---

## Card 12: Altered consciousness, confusion, seizures — Chapter 33, 41

**Can't-miss:** Hypoglycaemia, stroke, CNS infection, status epilepticus, intracranial haemorrhage, sepsis, hypoxia/hypercapnia, overdose or poisoning, Wernicke's encephalopathy, hyponatraemia, hepatic encephalopathy, non-convulsive status, delirium from drugs or retention.

**Red flags:** Reduced GCS or fluctuating attention; focal deficits; fever or neck stiffness; head injury; seizure >5 minutes or repeated without recovery; first seizure in an adult; age >65 with new confusion; alcohol misuse; antiepileptic withdrawal; pregnancy (eclampsia); immunosuppression/HIV; anticoagulant use.

**Do now:** Capillary glucose first; ABCDE; naloxone if opioid toxidrome; IV thiamine before glucose in at-risk drinkers when feasible; blood gas, electrolytes, ammonia, toxicology; CT head; LP if infection suspected and safe; benzodiazepine for seizure >5 minutes.

**Don't:** Attribute drowsiness to alcohol until other causes are excluded.

---

## Card 13: Rash and skin — Chapter 34

**Red flags:** Non-blanching purpura with fever (meningococcal sepsis); painful, dusky, or blistering skin with mucosal involvement (SJS/TEN); skin pain out of proportion with erythema (necrotising fasciitis); rapidly spreading erythema with systemic illness; widespread erythroderma; urticaria with angioedema/airway symptoms (anaphylaxis); new pigmented lesion with change in size, colour or border (ABCDE of melanoma); drug exposure in the last 2–8 weeks; target-like lesions; purpura with abdominal pain/joint pain (vasculitis).

**Do now:** Glass test, full vital signs, check mucosae; stop suspected drug; urgent antibiotics for sepsis or purpura; dermatology review; biopsy.

**Don't:** Wait for the rash to "declare itself" in a febrile, unwell patient.

---

## Card 14: Bleeding, bruising, anaemia — Chapter 35

**Red flags:** Platelets <20 × 10⁹/L or falling quickly; spontaneous mucosal bleeding; haemoglobin falling with haemodynamic effect; pancytopenia; blasts on film; haemolysis (jaundice, dark urine, low haptoglobin, raised LDH); fever with neutropenia; haemarthrosis; unexplained bleeding in children (consider non-accidental injury and bleeding disorders); anticoagulants with trauma; thrombocytopenia with neurological signs, renal failure and haemolysis (TTP: emergency).

**Do now:** FBC and film, coagulation, fibrinogen, group and screen, reticulocytes, DIC screen; reverse anticoagulants as appropriate; urgent haematology advice.

**Don't:** Transfuse platelets in TTP without specialist advice.

---

## Card 15: Sepsis — Chapter 39

**Red flags:** Suspected infection plus any of: RR ≥22, SBP ≤100, altered mentation, lactate >2 mmol/L, HR >90, new oxygen requirement, oliguria, mottling, high-risk host (age extremes, immunosuppression, post-partum, post-surgery, lines, splenectomy).

**Do now (within 1 hour):** Lactate, blood cultures before antibiotics, broad-spectrum antibiotics, 30 mL/kg crystalloid for hypotension or lactate ≥4 (adjust to cardiac and renal state), source control, reassess. Use your local bundle.

**Don't:** Wait for a fever; forget the source (urine, chest, abdomen, skin, CNS, lines, joint, pelvic).

---

## Card 16: Diabetic and metabolic emergencies — Chapter 43

**Red flags:** Glucose >11 mmol/L with ketones ≥3.0 mmol/L or pH <7.3 (DKA); glucose >30 mmol/L with osmolality >320 and no ketosis (HHS); glucose <3.0 mmol/L; K⁺ <3.0 or >6.5 mmol/L (or ECG changes); Na⁺ <120 mmol/L with symptoms; Ca²⁺ >3.0 mmol/L; Addisonian crisis (hypotension, hyponatraemia, hyperkalaemia, hypoglycaemia); thyroid storm; myxoedema coma; toxic ingestion with unexplained anion gap acidosis.

**Do now:** Fluids, insulin and potassium per protocol; ECG; correct hypoglycaemia (10–20 g glucose oral, or IV/IM glucagon); hydrocortisone for suspected adrenal crisis without waiting for a test; toxicology advice.

**Don't:** Give insulin before checking potassium; correct sodium too fast (≤8–10 mmol/L per 24 h in chronic hyponatraemia).

---

## Card 17: Children and infants — Chapter 56, 57, 58

**Red flags:** Age <3 months with fever ≥38 °C; poor feeding, lethargy, floppy or inconsolable; grunting, recession, RR >60 (infant); bile-stained vomit; bulging fontanelle; non-blanching rash; capillary refill >3 s; weak or high-pitched cry; dehydration; persistent unilateral limp or inability to bear weight; injuries inconsistent with history (non-accidental injury); failure to thrive; parental concern ("something is not right" is a valid red flag).

**Do now:** Use paediatric early warning scores and the traffic-light system; full exposure examination; glucose; sepsis treatment; senior paediatric review; safeguarding assessment.

**Don't:** Interpret vitals without age-normal values (Appendix C); sedate a distressed child before ruling out hypoxia.

---

## Card 18: Pregnancy and gynaecology — Chapter 59

**Red flags:** Positive pregnancy test with pain or bleeding (ectopic); heavy bleeding with shock; hypertension ≥160/110 or ≥140/90 with symptoms (severe headache, visual disturbance, epigastric pain, hyperreflexia); reduced fetal movements; trauma; fever in pregnancy; postpartum haemorrhage; new unilateral leg swelling or breathlessness; postmenopausal bleeding; pelvic pain with fever (PID/tubo-ovarian abscess); suspected torsion; sudden severe lower abdominal pain.

**Do now:** Pregnancy test in any woman of reproductive age; Rh status; obstetric early warning score; senior obstetric review; avoid delay for imaging that exposes the fetus when the mother is unstable.

**Don't:** Attribute dyspnoea or palpitations to pregnancy without exclusion of PE, peripartum cardiomyopathy and pre-eclampsia.

---

## Card 19: Older adults — Chapter 61

**Red flags:** Acute confusion (delirium) — look for infection, drugs, retention, constipation, pain, metabolic disturbance; falls with anticoagulants (CT head); new incontinence; unexplained weight loss; atypical presentation of MI, sepsis and abdominal catastrophe; sudden functional decline; polypharmacy with Beers/STOPP criteria; elder abuse or neglect; pressure injury.

**Do now:** Collateral history; baseline function; medication review; screen for delirium (4AT); examine feet, skin and pressure areas; consider capacity and goals of care.

**Don't:** Attribute confusion to dementia without excluding delirium.

---

## Card 20: Mental health — Chapter 20, 60

**Red flags:** Suicidal ideation with plan, intent or access to means; recent attempt; hopelessness with agitation; psychosis with command hallucinations or risk to others; catatonia; manic episode with poor judgment; severe self-neglect; intoxication or withdrawal (alcohol seizures, delirium tremens); first-episode psychosis (screen for organic causes); eating disorder with bradycardia, syncope, electrolyte disturbance; postnatal psychosis; abuse or coercive control.

**Do now:** Ask directly about suicide; assess risk and protective factors; remove means; secure safe environment and observation; medical clearance (glucose, bloods, toxicology, head injury); involve mental health services; consider capacity and legal framework.

**Don't:** Assume that asking about suicide plants the idea (it does not); attribute new psychiatric symptoms to a "functional" cause before medical causes are excluded.

---

## Card 21: Immunocompromised and palliative patients — Chapters 62 and 63

**Red flags (immunocompromised):** Fever with neutrophils <0.5 × 10⁹/L (medical emergency: antibiotics within 60 minutes); subtle presentations in transplant and steroid-treated patients; new respiratory symptoms (Pneumocystis, fungal, CMV); CNS symptoms in HIV (toxoplasmosis, cryptococcus, lymphoma, PML); relapse of underlying disease; drug interactions.

**Red flags (palliative):** Spinal cord compression, superior vena cava obstruction, hypercalcaemia, uncontrolled pain, bowel obstruction, delirium, haemorrhage, neutropenic sepsis, and treatment-related emergencies. Reversible causes of distress should be sought before attributing symptoms to the dying process.

**Do now:** Treat early and empirically per protocol; involve oncology/haematology/palliative teams; explicitly record goals of care and resuscitation status; consider what the patient wants.

**Don't:** Wait for fever in a neutropenic or steroid-treated patient; assume everything is "just the cancer".

---

## Card 22: Safety-netting script — Chapter 73

Use at the end of consultations where diagnosis remains uncertain:

1. **What I think is going on** (and what I am not sure about).
2. **What to expect** (usual course and timeframe).
3. **What to look out for** — specific, observable symptoms.
4. **When and how to seek help** — who to call, how quickly, what to say.
5. **Check understanding** — "Can you tell me what you will do if…?"
6. **Document** the advice, the follow-up arranged and who is responsible.

*Example:* "I think this is a viral infection and I expect it to settle within a week. If the fever lasts beyond three days, if you develop a stiff neck, a rash that doesn't fade under a glass, or can't keep fluids down, or if you feel much worse at any point, call the practice—or 112/999/911—immediately."
