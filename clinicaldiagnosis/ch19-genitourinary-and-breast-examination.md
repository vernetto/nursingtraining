## Chapter 19. Genitourinary and Breast Examination

### Learning objectives

After studying this chapter you should be able to:

1. Explain the principles of consent, privacy, chaperones and trauma-informed practice for intimate examinations.
2. Perform and interpret a breast and axillary examination, and apply the triple assessment to a breast lump.
3. Examine the male genitalia and scrotum, and distinguish testicular torsion, epididymo-orchitis, hydrocele, varicocele and testicular tumour.
4. Describe the steps of the female pelvic examination (inspection, speculum, bimanual) and know its indications.
5. Interpret urinalysis and examine the kidneys and bladder.
6. Recognise the emergencies of this region: testicular torsion, ruptured ectopic pregnancy, Fournier gangrene, paraphimosis, priapism, acute urinary retention, and obstructed infected kidney.
7. Examine sensitively in children, transgender and gender-diverse patients, and patients who have experienced abuse or trauma.

---

### 19.1 Principles of intimate examination

Intimate examinations (breast, genital, rectal) are among the most sensitive that clinicians perform. Done well, they are quick, informative and respectful; done badly, they cause distress, deter patients from future care, and may constitute a breach of professional standards.

#### Before the examination

- **Indication:** examine only when it is necessary to answer a clinical question. Explain *why.*
- **Explanation and consent:** describe in plain language what you will do, what it feels like, and how long it will take. Obtain **explicit verbal consent** (and written where required by local rules). Tell the patient they may stop at any time, and honour that.
- **Chaperone:** offer a chaperone for every intimate examination, whatever the gender of the examiner. The chaperone should be a trained, impartial person (not a relative), who stays for the whole examination, at a position where they can see what is happening. Record the chaperone's name and role, or that the patient declined. A patient may prefer a relative or a clinician of a particular gender: accommodate where possible.
- **Interpreter:** use a professional interpreter if necessary (Chapter 8).
- **Privacy and dignity:** a closed room, a curtain or screen for undressing, a gown or sheet. Leave the room while the patient undresses. Expose only what is necessary at each stage. Avoid unnecessary comments or interruptions.
- **Preparation:** wash and warm your hands; gloves; lubricant; a good light; equipment ready and at hand.
- **History first:** the relevant history (Chapter 6–7) before examining.

#### During the examination

- Keep talking: signpost what comes next ("I'm going to place my hand on your abdomen now").
- Watch the patient's face for discomfort; ask for feedback.
- Use a gentle, unhurried technique.
- Avoid comments about the patient's body, or sexual remarks.

#### After the examination

- Leave the room or turn away while the patient dresses.
- Explain the findings in plain language and next steps.
- Document: consent, chaperone, findings, what you told the patient.

#### Trauma-informed care

Many patients have experienced sexual violence, childhood abuse, genital mutilation, or have had previous distressing medical experiences. Signs of distress or flashbacks can occur during intimate examination. **Principles:** safety, choice, control and collaboration. Give the patient control over positioning, pace and the right to say "stop." If the patient becomes distressed, pause, ask what they need, and consider deferring or modifying the examination. Do not assume a history of trauma but be prepared.

#### Special groups

- **Children and adolescents:** the parent or carer is usually present; the child's wishes and comfort matter; adolescents may prefer to be examined without the parent. Examination of the genitalia in children should be limited to what is clinically necessary. Suspected abuse should be examined by trained paediatric or forensic clinicians (Chapters 59–60).
- **Transgender and gender-diverse patients:** use the patient's chosen name and pronouns; ask what terms they use for their body parts; examine anatomy relevant to symptoms and risks (a transgender man may need cervical screening; a transgender woman may require prostate and breast assessment); acknowledge that the examination can be a source of dysphoria; offer choices.
- **Patients with disability or cognitive impairment:** adapt communication, involve carers, ensure that consent or best-interests decisions are properly documented.
- **Female genital mutilation (FGM):** recognise and respond sensitively; know local reporting duties.
- **Cultural and religious considerations:** same-gender examiners, modesty requirements.

### 19.2 Breast examination

#### Indications

A breast lump, pain, nipple discharge or retraction, skin changes, axillary swelling, a family history of breast or ovarian cancer, symptoms in men (gynaecomastia, lump), and as part of a general examination where indicated. Clinical breast examination is **not** a substitute for screening mammography in asymptomatic women, and its routine role as a screening test is limited and debated.

#### History

- **Lump:** how and when noticed, change in size, relation to the menstrual cycle, pain, skin or nipple change, previous lumps, biopsies.
- **Nipple discharge:** unilateral or bilateral, spontaneous or on squeezing, colour (clear, milky, green, bloody), from a single duct or several.
- **Pain (mastalgia):** cyclical (linked to the menstrual cycle) or non-cyclical; musculoskeletal or chest wall pain commonly mimics breast pain.
- **Risk factors for breast cancer:** age, family history (first-degree relatives; BRCA1/2 carriers; age at diagnosis; ovarian cancer; male breast cancer), early menarche, late menopause, nulliparity, late first pregnancy, hormone replacement therapy (combined), oral contraceptives, obesity, alcohol, radiation to the chest (e.g. for lymphoma), previous atypical hyperplasia or lobular carcinoma in situ.
- **Menstrual history, pregnancy, lactation, contraception, HRT.**

#### Inspection

Seated, upright, arms by the sides, then raised above the head, then hands pressed on the hips (contracting the pectoral muscles), and leaning forward. Compare the two sides.

Look for:

- **Symmetry, size and shape** (some asymmetry is normal).
- **Skin:** erythema (mastitis, inflammatory cancer), **peau d'orange** (oedema with prominent skin pores: lymphatic obstruction by cancer), dimpling or tethering (fibrosis from a cancer pulling on Cooper's ligaments), ulceration, visible veins, scars, rash.
- **Nipple:** inversion (new retraction is suspicious; long-standing inversion is usually benign), deviation, discharge, **scaling, crusting or eczema of the nipple and areola (Paget disease of the breast,** usually unilateral and not responsive to topical steroids: associated with underlying carcinoma), ulceration.
- **Contour changes** with arm movement (dimpling brought out by raising the arms).

#### Palpation

1. **Position:** supine, with the ipsilateral arm behind the head (spreads the breast tissue evenly across the chest wall). Examine the whole breast, from the clavicle to the inframammary fold and from the sternum to the mid-axillary line, including the axillary tail.
2. **Technique:** use the pads of the three middle fingers, with light, medium and deep pressure in small circular motions, systematically (e.g. vertical strips, spiral or wedge pattern). **Start with the normal breast** (if there is a known lump). Include the **nipple and the area beneath it.**
3. **Describe any lump:** **site** (quadrant, clock-face position, distance from the nipple), **size** (cm), **shape,** **surface** (smooth, irregular), **edge** (well-defined or ill-defined), **consistency** (soft, firm, hard, cystic), **mobility** (mobile, fixed to skin, fixed to the pectoral muscle or chest wall: test with hand on hip contracting pectoralis), **tenderness,** relation to the skin and nipple, **overlying skin changes.**
4. **Nipple discharge:** gently compress the areola in a radial pattern to identify the duct; note the colour and test for blood if necessary.
5. **Axillae:** examine with the patient sitting, supporting the arm, and palpating the central, lateral, anterior, posterior and apical groups; the **supraclavicular and infraclavicular fossae.** Axillary nodes that are hard, fixed or enlarged are suspicious.

#### Interpretation

| Feature | More suggestive of benign | More suggestive of malignant |
|---|---|---|
| Age | Younger | Older, post-menopausal |
| Consistency | Soft, firm rubbery | Hard, stony |
| Edge | Smooth, well-defined, mobile ("breast mouse") | Irregular, ill-defined |
| Fixity | Mobile | Fixed to skin or chest wall |
| Skin | Normal | Dimpling, peau d'orange, ulceration |
| Nipple | Normal | New retraction, bloody discharge, Paget changes |
| Axilla | Soft nodes, absent | Hard, fixed nodes |
| Pain | Often cyclical pain (not a reliable discriminator) | Often painless (but pain does not exclude cancer) |

**These features are not reliable enough to exclude cancer clinically.** Any new, discrete lump in an adult needs formal assessment.

**Common benign conditions:** fibroadenoma (young women: firm, mobile, smooth), cysts (perimenopausal women, may be tender and fluctuant), fibrocystic change, mastitis and abscess (lactation: hot, tender, erythematous), fat necrosis (after trauma), duct ectasia (nipple discharge, retraction in older women), intraductal papilloma (bloody discharge from a single duct), lipoma, gynaecomastia in men.

#### Triple assessment

All suspicious breast lumps are evaluated using **triple assessment:**

1. **Clinical** (history and examination),
2. **Imaging** (ultrasound for women <40 years or <30 in some guidelines; mammography ± ultrasound for older women),
3. **Pathology** (core needle biopsy preferred to fine-needle aspiration).

Concordance of the three methods gives a high negative predictive value. Refer urgently under the suspected cancer pathway in the presence of: age ≥30 with an unexplained persistent lump, age ≥50 with nipple discharge, retraction or other changes in one nipple, skin changes suggestive of cancer, or an unexplained axillary lump.

#### Men

Breast examination in men is needed for **gynaecomastia** (soft, symmetrical, subareolar glandular tissue: drugs such as spironolactone, cimetidine, digoxin, anabolic steroids, marijuana; liver disease; hypogonadism; testicular tumour; hyperthyroidism; puberty; obesity), and for any hard eccentric lump, nipple retraction or bloody discharge (male breast cancer is rare, associated with BRCA2 and Klinefelter syndrome).

### 19.3 The male genitalia

#### History

Pain (location, onset, severity, relation to activity), swelling, lumps, discharge, dysuria, haematuria, sexual dysfunction, trauma, fever, urinary symptoms, sexual history, previous STIs, vasectomy, history of undescended testes, hernia, infertility, family history of testicular cancer.

#### Inspection

With the patient **standing** (and later supine), well-lit room, exposed from the waist down.

- **Pubic hair, skin:** infestations, rash, lesions (warts, ulcers: syphilis, herpes, chancroid), scars, erythema, swelling.
- **Penis:** foreskin (phimosis: cannot retract; **paraphimosis:** retracted foreskin that cannot be replaced, with swelling of the glans: **urological emergency**), balanitis (inflammation of the glans), plaques (Peyronie), **meatus** (position: hypospadias; discharge; stenosis), lesions on the glans (carcinoma: persistent ulcer or lump, especially in uncircumcised men).
- **Scrotum:** size, symmetry, swelling, erythema, oedema, skin lesions, visible veins (varicocele: "bag of worms"). The left testis normally hangs lower.

#### Palpation

- **Each testis separately,** using both hands: size (volume ~15–25 mL in adults; 4–5 cm long), shape, consistency (firm, smooth), tenderness, nodules, position (high-riding, horizontal lie: torsion), mobility.
- **Epididymis:** lies posterolaterally; palpable as a soft comma-shaped structure. Swelling and tenderness: epididymitis; cystic swelling: epididymal cyst (spermatocele).
- **Spermatic cord and vas deferens:** thickening, a "bag of worms" (varicocele, upright), tenderness; absence of the vas (cystic fibrosis).
- **Scrotal swelling: the questions to ask:**
  1. Can you **get above** the swelling? (Yes: scrotal origin; no: inguinal hernia extending into the scrotum.)
  2. Can you feel the **testis separately** from the swelling?
  3. Does it **transilluminate?** (Hydrocele and spermatocele transilluminate; hernia, haematocele and tumour do not.)
  4. Is there a **cough impulse?** (Hernia.)
  5. Is it **tender?**

| Swelling | Features |
|---|---|
| **Hydrocele** | Fluid around the testis; smooth, fluctuant, **transilluminates,** testis cannot be felt separately; may be primary, or secondary to infection, tumour, trauma (always examine the testis, and ultrasound if it cannot be felt) |
| **Epididymal cyst (spermatocele)** | Separate from the testis, transilluminates, above and behind |
| **Varicocele** | "Bag of worms" above the testis, more apparent standing and on Valsalva, usually left-sided; sudden onset of a right-sided or non-reducing varicocele suggests a retroperitoneal mass |
| **Testicular tumour** | Hard, painless, irregular lump in the testis, young men (15–45); may present with a heavy sensation or acute pain from haemorrhage; gynaecomastia; back pain from retroperitoneal nodes; supraclavicular node |
| **Epididymo-orchitis** | Tender, swollen epididymis and testis, fever, erythema; pain relieved by elevation (Prehn sign is unreliable); urethritis (young: chlamydia, gonorrhoea), urinary pathogens (older), mumps orchitis |
| **Inguinal hernia** | Cough impulse, cannot get above |
| **Testicular torsion** | See below |
| **Haematocele (trauma)** | Painful swelling after injury, does not transilluminate; **ultrasound** to look for rupture |
| **Fournier gangrene** | See below |

#### Testicular torsion

**Torsion of the spermatic cord** is a **surgical emergency**; the testis is viable for 6 hours (high salvage), with decreasing viability thereafter (salvage falls substantially after 12 hours).

- **Presentation:** sudden, severe unilateral testicular pain, often waking the patient from sleep or after exertion/trauma, **nausea and vomiting,** lower abdominal pain, with peak incidence in adolescence (12–18), but it can occur at any age, including neonates.
- **Examination:** a swollen, tender, **high-riding** testis with a **horizontal lie,** **absent cremasteric reflex** (stroke the medial thigh: the testis normally rises; absence has high sensitivity for torsion), tender spermatic cord, erythematous scrotum later.
- **Do not delay for imaging** if torsion is clinically likely: urgent surgical exploration and fixation of both testes. Doppler ultrasound may help if the diagnosis is uncertain and does not delay treatment. Manual detorsion ("open book" outward rotation) may be tried while arranging surgery, but surgery must still follow.
- **Mimics:** torsion of the testicular appendage (blue dot sign), epididymitis, hernia, trauma, acute idiopathic scrotal oedema, referred pain from the abdomen or ureter. **In any boy with abdominal pain, examine the testes.**

#### Other urological emergencies

- **Paraphimosis:** reduce under analgesia (compression, ice, granulated sugar, or dorsal slit).
- **Priapism:** painful prolonged erection >4 hours (sickle cell disease, drugs including erectile dysfunction treatments and antipsychotics, trauma); urgent urological care (ischaemic priapism).
- **Fournier gangrene:** necrotising fasciitis of the perineum and genitals; severe pain, swelling, crepitus, dusky skin, sepsis; risk factors: diabetes, immunosuppression, alcohol use; **urgent surgical debridement and broad-spectrum antibiotics.**
- **Acute urinary retention:** painful inability to void, palpable bladder (see 19.5).
- **Penile fracture:** sudden "snap" during intercourse with immediate detumescence, haematoma ("aubergine" deformity).

#### Prostate

The prostate is examined by digital rectal examination (Chapter 14). **Prostate cancer is often asymptomatic;** a hard nodular prostate warrants further investigation (PSA with appropriate counselling, MRI, urology referral). **Acute prostatitis:** fever, perineal pain, dysuria, a boggy, extremely tender prostate; avoid vigorous palpation.

### 19.4 The female genitalia and pelvic examination

#### Indications for pelvic examination

Abnormal vaginal bleeding (including postmenopausal), pelvic pain, vaginal discharge, suspected STI, cervical screening (as part of national programmes), suspected pelvic mass, prolapse, contraception procedures, fertility evaluation, and in the investigation of suspected ectopic pregnancy. **Routine "annual pelvic examination" in asymptomatic women is not recommended** by most guidelines because of poor benefit and potential harm.

#### History

Menstrual history (age at menarche, cycle length, duration, heaviness, intermenstrual or postcoital bleeding, last menstrual period), **sexual history** (Chapter 7), contraception, pregnancies and outcomes, **pregnancy possibility,** discharge (colour, odour, itch), pain (dyspareunia, dysmenorrhoea, pelvic pain), urinary and bowel symptoms, menopausal symptoms, cervical screening history and results, history of STIs, previous gynaecological surgery, FGM, sexual violence.

#### Examination sequence

1. **General and abdominal examination** first: pregnancy, masses, tenderness, peritonism, hernias, lymph nodes.
2. **Position:** supine with knees flexed and apart (**dorsal**), or the **left lateral (Sims)** position, with a sheet to cover.
3. **External genitalia (inspection):** hair distribution, skin (lichen sclerosus: white atrophic plaques, vulval cancer), ulcers (herpes, syphilis), warts, discharge, swelling (Bartholin cyst or abscess: a swelling at the posterior introitus), clitoral enlargement, scars, FGM, prolapse (ask the patient to cough or strain).
4. **Speculum examination:** warm and lubricate the speculum (water or a minimal amount of water-based lubricant if cytology is planned). Insert gently at an angle, open, and visualise the **cervix:** colour, surface, os (parous: slit-like; nulliparous: round), discharge, bleeding, polyps, ectropion (red area around the os: a normal variant), growth, ulcer, cervical motion. Take samples as indicated: **cervical screening (cytology/HPV testing), high vaginal and endocervical swabs, nucleic acid amplification tests for chlamydia and gonorrhoea** (self-taken vulvovaginal swabs are acceptable for NAATs).
   - **Vaginal wall:** atrophy, prolapse (cystocele, rectocele, uterine prolapse: Sims speculum), lesions.
5. **Bimanual examination:** with a lubricated gloved index and middle finger in the vagina and the other hand on the lower abdomen, assess:
   - **Cervix:** position, consistency (firm; soft in pregnancy: Goodell sign), mobility, **cervical motion tenderness** (pain on moving the cervix: pelvic inflammatory disease, ectopic pregnancy; "chandelier sign"), os.
   - **Uterus:** size, shape, position (anteverted or retroverted), consistency, mobility, tenderness (an enlarged uterus: pregnancy, fibroids, adenomyosis; enlarged, boggy uterus in adenomyosis; fixed retroverted uterus in endometriosis).
   - **Adnexa:** palpate each side for masses and tenderness (ovarian cysts, tumours, tubo-ovarian abscess, ectopic pregnancy; normal ovaries are often not palpable in postmenopausal women: a palpable ovary after menopause is suspicious).
   - **Pouch of Douglas** (nodularity, tenderness: endometriosis, cancer deposits).
6. **Rectal or rectovaginal examination** where indicated.

#### Patterns

| Presentation | Typical findings |
|---|---|
| **Pelvic inflammatory disease (PID)** | Lower abdominal pain, fever, mucopurulent cervical discharge, cervical motion tenderness, adnexal tenderness; chlamydia/gonorrhoea; may lead to infertility and ectopic pregnancy |
| **Ectopic pregnancy** | Amenorrhoea/abnormal bleeding, lower abdominal pain, shoulder tip pain, adnexal tenderness or mass, cervical motion tenderness; **pregnancy test positive;** hypovolaemia in rupture |
| **Miscarriage** | Bleeding, pain, open or closed cervical os, products of conception |
| **Fibroids** | Heavy periods, pressure symptoms, enlarged irregular firm uterus |
| **Endometriosis** | Dysmenorrhoea, deep dyspareunia, cyclical symptoms, fixed retroverted uterus, uterosacral nodules |
| **Ovarian cyst/torsion** | Adnexal mass, sudden pain with nausea (torsion) |
| **Ovarian cancer** | Persistent bloating, early satiety, pelvic or abdominal pain, urinary frequency; ascites; adnexal mass; raised CA-125 (not specific) |
| **Cervical cancer** | Postcoital or intermenstrual bleeding, discharge, a friable, irregular cervix |
| **Endometrial cancer** | Postmenopausal bleeding (urgent investigation) |
| **Vaginal candidiasis** | Thick, white, curd-like, itchy discharge, erythema |
| **Bacterial vaginosis** | Thin, grey, fishy-smelling discharge, no itch, pH >4.5 |
| **Trichomoniasis** | Frothy green-yellow discharge, "strawberry cervix," pH >4.5 |
| **Atrophic vaginitis** | Postmenopausal dryness, dyspareunia, pale thin mucosa |
| **Prolapse** | Dragging sensation, visible bulge on straining |

**Postmenopausal bleeding** and **intermenstrual or postcoital bleeding** warrant investigation (transvaginal ultrasound, endometrial sampling, colposcopy) to exclude cancer.

### 19.5 The urinary tract

#### History

Urinary frequency, urgency, nocturia, dysuria, hesitancy, poor stream, terminal dribbling, incomplete emptying, incontinence (stress, urge, overflow), **haematuria** (visible or non-visible; painless gross haematuria in an adult is **cancer until proven otherwise**), loin pain, fever, rigors, colour and odour of the urine, volume (polyuria), foamy urine, renal stones, previous UTIs, catheter or instrumentation, family history (polycystic kidney disease), drugs.

#### Examination

- **General:** hydration, blood pressure, fever, oedema, pallor (anaemia of CKD), uraemic frost and fetor (rare), asterixis, pericardial rub.
- **Abdomen:** kidney palpation by ballotting (Chapter 14), **renal angle tenderness,** bladder (suprapubic dullness), bruits, aorta, masses.
- **External genitalia:** meatus, discharge, phimosis (men); prolapse, atrophy (women).
- **Digital rectal examination** (prostate in men; faecal loading in anyone).
- **Pelvic examination** (women with incontinence, prolapse).
- **Neurological examination** of the lower limbs and sacral dermatomes if neurogenic bladder is suspected.

#### Urinalysis (urine dipstick)

The urine dipstick is a rapid, cheap test with significant diagnostic value if its limitations are understood. **Use a fresh midstream specimen,** read at the time specified.

| Parameter | Interpretation |
|---|---|
| **Leucocyte esterase** | Pyuria (UTI, inflammation, stones, contamination) |
| **Nitrite** | Gram-negative bacteria (Enterobacteriaceae); specific but insensitive (needs bladder residence time >4 hours; many organisms such as Enterococcus do not reduce nitrate) |
| **Blood (haematuria)** | Haematuria (stones, infection, glomerular disease, tumour, trauma), haemoglobinuria or myoglobinuria (false positive for red cells), menstruation contamination, vigorous exercise |
| **Protein** | Glomerular disease, infection, fever, exercise, pregnancy; quantify with the urine albumin:creatinine ratio (ACR) or protein:creatinine ratio (PCR) |
| **Glucose** | Diabetes, renal glycosuria, SGLT2 inhibitors, pregnancy |
| **Ketones** | Diabetic ketoacidosis, starvation, vomiting, alcohol, low-carbohydrate diet |
| **Bilirubin / urobilinogen** | Bilirubin in conjugated hyperbilirubinaemia (obstructive jaundice, hepatitis); urobilinogen increased in haemolysis, liver disease |
| **pH** | Urea-splitting organisms (Proteus: alkaline, struvite stones); renal tubular acidosis |
| **Specific gravity** | Concentration: dehydration; dilute: diabetes insipidus, polydipsia, CKD |

**UTI diagnosis:** In a symptomatic, non-pregnant adult woman with dysuria and frequency and no vaginal discharge, the probability of UTI is high (>90% in some series); a dipstick positive for nitrite and/or leucocytes strengthens the likelihood; negative nitrite and leucocytes make UTI unlikely. In **older adults,** **asymptomatic bacteriuria** is very common and should not be treated, and positive dipsticks should not be used to attribute non-specific symptoms such as confusion or falls to UTI without other signs (Chapter 2). **Men with UTI symptoms,** **children,** **pregnant women** and **those with catheters** need cultures.

#### Urinary tract conditions: bedside patterns

| Presentation | Findings |
|---|---|
| **Cystitis** | Dysuria, frequency, urgency, suprapubic discomfort; no fever or loin pain |
| **Pyelonephritis** | Fever, rigors, loin pain, renal angle tenderness, vomiting, positive dipstick; **obstructed infected kidney (pyonephrosis) is an emergency** requiring urgent decompression (nephrostomy or stent) |
| **Renal colic** | Sudden severe colicky loin-to-groin pain, patient restless, nausea, haematuria on dipstick (in most), non-tender abdomen; **exclude ruptured aortic aneurysm in older patients, ectopic pregnancy and testicular torsion in the young;** CT-KUB is the investigation of choice for stones |
| **Acute urinary retention** | Severe suprapubic pain, inability to void, a tender, palpable, dull bladder; causes: BPH, constipation, drugs (anticholinergics, opioids), UTI, neurological (cauda equina syndrome: check perianal sensation), post-operative |
| **Chronic retention with overflow** | Painless, dribbling incontinence, palpable bladder, renal impairment |
| **Haematuria (painless, visible)** | Bladder or kidney cancer until proven otherwise (urgent cystoscopy and imaging); also stones, infection, glomerulonephritis (red cell casts, proteinuria), vigorous exercise, anticoagulants (but investigate anyway) |
| **Nephrotic syndrome** | Heavy proteinuria (>3.5 g/day), hypoalbuminaemia, oedema, hyperlipidaemia (Chapter 49) |
| **Nephritic syndrome** | Haematuria, hypertension, oedema, oliguria, red cell casts (post-streptococcal, IgA nephropathy) |
| **Polycystic kidney disease** | Bilateral palpable irregular kidneys, hypertension, haematuria, cysts in the liver; family history |
| **Renal artery stenosis** | Resistant hypertension, abdominal/flank bruit, flash pulmonary oedema, renal impairment with ACE inhibitors |

### 19.6 Worked cases

#### Case 1: Acute scrotum in a teenager

*A 15-year-old boy wakes at 2 a.m. with severe left testicular pain and vomiting. He was playing football the previous day but did not recall an injury. Examination: the left hemiscrotum is swollen and red, the testis is high-riding with a horizontal lie, extremely tender; the cremasteric reflex is absent on the left and present on the right. He is afebrile; the urine dipstick is negative.*

**Reasoning.** Sudden onset, nausea and vomiting, high-riding horizontal testis and absent cremasteric reflex: **testicular torsion** until proven otherwise. The dipstick negative for leucocytes/nitrite makes epididymitis less likely (which is rare before puberty/in adolescents).

**Action.** Immediate urological/surgical assessment and **scrotal exploration** (do not delay for imaging; imaging only if the diagnosis is genuinely uncertain and it won't cause delay). The testis is explored, untwisted, and fixed (orchidopexy), together with fixation of the contralateral testis. Time to surgery determines salvage: <6 hours: >90%; 12 hours: ~50%; >24 hours: <10–20%.

#### Case 2: Painless testicular lump

*A 27-year-old man noticed a "hard pea" in his right testis during a shower, 4 weeks ago, and has mild heaviness. The right testis has a firm, non-tender, irregular 2-cm nodule within the body of the testis, which does not transilluminate; the epididymis is normal. Left testis normal. No gynaecomastia. No inguinal or supraclavicular nodes.*

**Reasoning.** A **painless hard intratesticular lump** in a young man: **testicular cancer** until proven otherwise (germ cell tumour: seminoma peak 30–40, non-seminomatous germ cell tumour peak 20–30). Risk factors: cryptorchidism, previous testicular cancer, family history, infertility.

**Action.** Urgent scrotal ultrasound; serum tumour markers (AFP, β-hCG, LDH) **before** orchidectomy; urgent urology referral for inguinal radical orchidectomy (**not scrotal biopsy**); CT staging of the chest, abdomen and pelvis; sperm banking discussion. Cure rates are over 95% for most stages.

#### Case 3: A breast lump

*A 45-year-old woman felt a lump in her left breast 2 weeks ago. Her mother had breast cancer at 52. Examination: a 2.5-cm hard, irregular, non-tender mass in the upper outer quadrant, mobile but with slight skin tethering when the arms are raised. A firm, 1-cm mobile node in the left axilla. No nipple discharge.*

**Reasoning.** An irregular hard mass with skin tethering, axillary node and a family history: **suspicious for breast cancer.** Clinical features alone cannot exclude cancer in less suspicious lumps and cannot confirm it in this one.

**Action.** Urgent referral to a one-stop breast clinic for **triple assessment:** clinical examination, imaging (mammography and ultrasound), and core biopsy (with axillary node ultrasound ± biopsy). Discuss the multidisciplinary plan, receptor status (ER, PR, HER2), staging as indicated, and genetic counselling given the family history.

#### Case 4: Lower abdominal pain in a young woman

*A 23-year-old woman has had 3 days of lower abdominal pain, fever 38.4 °C, deep dyspareunia and mucopurulent vaginal discharge. She has had a new sexual partner in the last 2 months. Pregnancy test negative. Abdominal examination: bilateral lower quadrant tenderness without peritonism. Speculum: mucopurulent endocervical discharge, friable cervix. Bimanual: cervical motion tenderness and bilateral adnexal tenderness, no mass.*

**Reasoning.** A sexually active young woman with lower abdominal pain, fever, discharge, cervical motion and adnexal tenderness: **pelvic inflammatory disease** (chlamydia, gonorrhoea, *Mycoplasma genitalium*, anaerobes). Differential: appendicitis, ectopic pregnancy (excluded by negative test), ovarian torsion, tubo-ovarian abscess, UTI.

**Management.** NAATs for chlamydia and gonorrhoea, HIV and syphilis testing; start empirical antibiotics promptly (low threshold to treat because delay increases the risk of infertility and ectopic pregnancy); partner notification and treatment; counselling; follow-up; admission if severely unwell, pregnant, or no response; ultrasound if a tubo-ovarian abscess is suspected.

#### Case 5: Positive pregnancy test and shock

*A 29-year-old woman, 7 weeks since her last period, collapses with sudden lower abdominal pain and shoulder-tip pain. Pulse 130, BP 80/50, pale, with a rigid, tender abdomen. Urine hCG strongly positive.*

**Reasoning.** **Ruptured ectopic pregnancy** with haemoperitoneum until proven otherwise.

**Action.** Resuscitate (two large-bore IV lines, cross-match, fluids/blood), **urgent gynaecological and anaesthetic review, theatre**; bedside ultrasound for free fluid if available. Do not delay for formal imaging in the unstable patient. Anti-D if Rh-negative.

#### Case 6: Retention in an older man

*A 76-year-old man with prostatism symptoms has been unable to pass urine for 12 hours and has severe suprapubic pain. He took a cold remedy containing a decongestant and an antihistamine yesterday. Examination: a tender, dull, palpable suprapubic mass reaching the umbilicus; DRE: a smooth, enlarged prostate.*

**Reasoning.** **Acute urinary retention** on a background of **benign prostatic hyperplasia,** precipitated by drugs with anticholinergic or sympathomimetic effects.

**Management.** Urethral catheterisation (monitor residual volume and post-obstructive diuresis), urea and creatinine, urine dipstick and culture, ultrasound if renal impairment, then alpha-blocker, trial without catheter, and urology follow-up. Consider constipation, UTI and neurological causes; if there are bilateral leg symptoms, perianal numbness or back pain, consider cauda equina syndrome.

---

### 19.7 Common pitfalls

- **Not offering a chaperone,** or not documenting it.
- **Omitting the testes in a boy with abdominal pain.**
- **Delaying surgery for imaging** in suspected torsion.
- **Treating a "swollen testis" as epididymitis** without considering torsion or tumour.
- **Assuming a breast lump in a younger woman is benign** without triple assessment.
- **Missing Paget disease of the nipple** by treating "eczema" with steroids.
- **Failing to do a pregnancy test** in any woman of reproductive age with abdominal pain or abnormal bleeding.
- **Attributing non-specific symptoms in older adults to a positive dipstick.**
- **Attributing painless haematuria to infection or anticoagulation** without investigation.
- **Not examining the skin and genitalia in patients with systemic or sexual health presentations.**
- **Insufficient communication, lack of consent, rushing, or making comments** that can traumatise.
- **Performing routine pelvic examinations** without indication.

---

### 19.8 Key points

- For intimate examinations: justify, explain, obtain consent, offer a chaperone, protect privacy, and respect the right to stop.
- Examine breasts by inspection (arms down and raised) and palpation (supine, systematic) including the axillae; use triple assessment for any suspicious lump.
- Acute scrotal pain with a high-riding horizontal testis and absent cremasteric reflex is torsion: operate; examine the testes in any boy with abdominal pain.
- A hard painless testicular lump in a young man is cancer until proven otherwise.
- Pelvic examination: inspection, speculum, bimanual; cervical motion tenderness suggests PID or ectopic pregnancy.
- Always check pregnancy status in women of reproductive age; ruptured ectopic pregnancy is a surgical emergency.
- Use the urine dipstick with understanding of its limits; do not over-diagnose UTI in older adults; investigate painless visible haematuria for cancer.

### 19.9 Self-assessment

1. List four elements of good practice for an intimate examination.
   *Answer (any four): explain the indication and procedure; obtain consent; offer a chaperone; ensure privacy and dignity; stop if asked; document.*

2. How does torsion of the testis typically present, and what should be done?
   *Answer: Sudden severe unilateral pain, nausea/vomiting, high-riding horizontal testis, absent cremasteric reflex; emergency surgical exploration, without delay for imaging.*

3. Describe the triple assessment of a breast lump.
   *Answer: Clinical examination, imaging (ultrasound/mammography), and pathology (core biopsy).*

4. Which scrotal swellings transilluminate?
   *Answer: Hydrocele and epididymal cyst (spermatocele).*

5. What are the clinical features of pelvic inflammatory disease on examination?
   *Answer: Lower abdominal tenderness, fever, mucopurulent cervical discharge, cervical motion tenderness, adnexal tenderness.*

6. Why is a positive urine dipstick not sufficient to explain confusion in an older adult?
   *Answer: Asymptomatic bacteriuria is common in older adults, so a positive dipstick is often not the cause of delirium; other causes must be sought.*
