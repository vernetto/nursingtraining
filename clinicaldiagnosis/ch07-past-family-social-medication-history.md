## Chapter 7. Past, Family, Social and Medication History

### Learning objectives

After studying this chapter you should be able to:

1. Take a past medical and surgical history that identifies conditions relevant to the current problem.
2. Obtain an accurate medication history, including over-the-counter, herbal and recreational substances, and assess adherence.
3. Distinguish a true drug allergy from an adverse effect or intolerance.
4. Construct a useful family history and recognise patterns suggesting inherited disease.
5. Take a social history covering occupation, housing, habits, travel, exposures and support, and quantify tobacco and alcohol use.
6. Ask about sensitive topics (sexual health, drug use, violence) respectfully and effectively.
7. Recognise how this background information changes pre-test probabilities and management.

---

### 7.1 Why the "background" history is not background

Students often treat past, family, social and drug history as a form-filling exercise to be completed after the "real" history. This is a mistake. These sections routinely supply the **decisive information**:

- A man's cough is explained by the **ACE inhibitor** started three weeks ago.
- A woman's breathlessness is explained by **a long-haul flight, oral contraceptive and a history of clots in her sister**.
- A "chest infection" that does not clear has an **occupational exposure** behind it.
- A fever has a diagnosis because of a **mosquito-endemic country visited five weeks ago**.
- A patient's "unexplained" collapses are explained by a **family history of sudden death** at a young age.
- A patient's "resistant" hypertension is explained by **not taking tablets** because of cost or side effects.

This history also determines **what you can safely prescribe**, what the patient can realistically do, and what support they will have at home.

Treat each section as a **hypothesis-testing tool**, not a ritual. Always ask: "Which diagnoses does this make more or less likely? Which treatments does it forbid or require?"

### 7.2 Past medical and surgical history

#### What to ask

Open: "Have you had any serious illnesses or operations in the past? Do you see a doctor or specialist regularly for anything?"

Then prompt for common and high-yield conditions, particularly those that alter risk or management:

| Domain | Prompts |
|---|---|
| Cardiovascular | Hypertension, heart attack, angina, stents or bypass, heart failure, arrhythmia, valve disease, stroke or TIA, peripheral vascular disease, blood clots |
| Respiratory | Asthma, COPD, tuberculosis, pneumonia, pneumothorax |
| Endocrine / metabolic | Diabetes (type, duration, treatment, complications), thyroid disease, high cholesterol, obesity |
| Renal / urological | Kidney disease, stones, recurrent urinary infection |
| Gastrointestinal / hepatic | Ulcers, reflux, inflammatory bowel disease, hepatitis, gallstones, pancreatitis, bowel surgery |
| Neurological | Epilepsy, migraine, head injury, neurological disease |
| Musculoskeletal / rheumatological | Arthritis, gout, osteoporosis, fractures |
| Haematological / oncological | Anaemia, bleeding disorders, cancer (type, date, treatment: chemotherapy, radiotherapy, surgery) |
| Infection | HIV, TB, hepatitis B/C, sexually transmitted infections, previous severe infections |
| Psychiatric | Depression, anxiety, psychosis, self-harm, hospital admissions |
| Obstetric / gynaecological | Pregnancies, deliveries, complications, menstrual pattern, contraception, menopause |
| Other | Allergies, blood transfusions, anaesthetic problems, hospital admissions, immunisation status |

#### How to explore each positive answer

For each significant condition, ask:

1. **When** was it diagnosed, and how (what tests)?
2. **Who** manages it? Last review?
3. **What treatment** is used, and does the patient take it?
4. **Complications or severity** (admissions, ICU, emergency visits)?
5. **How controlled** is it now?

*Examples.* "Diabetes" means different things: a 25-year-old on insulin for 10 years (type 1, risk of DKA) versus a 70-year-old on diet control for 2 years. "Asthma" can be mild and seasonal, or a history of ICU admission with intubation (a strong risk marker for fatal asthma). "Heart failure" demands the ejection fraction, the cause, and current diuretic dose.

#### Previous similar episodes

Always ask: **"Have you had anything like this before?"** If yes: What was it? How was it diagnosed? What happened? A prior diagnosed episode is useful, but beware of posterior probability error (Chapter 2): the current episode may be something different.

#### Previous investigations and operations

Previous operations alter anatomy and risk: adhesions after abdominal surgery (bowel obstruction), splenectomy (overwhelming infection), mastectomy (lymphoedema, no blood pressure in that arm), cardiac surgery, joint replacement (prosthetic infection), thyroidectomy (hypocalcaemia). Ask about **anaesthetic complications** (malignant hyperthermia, difficult airway) and **transfusions** (risk of reactions or transfusion-transmitted infection in some settings).

#### Immunisation

Record childhood vaccination status, travel vaccines, influenza, pneumococcal, COVID-19, hepatitis B, tetanus (especially for wounds), HPV. An unvaccinated child with fever raises different possibilities than a fully vaccinated one.

#### Using documented records

Where available, verify the history in medical records or discharge summaries. Patients can misremember or mislabel conditions ("a heart murmur" may be an innocent flow murmur). Records also reveal results and allergies that the patient forgets.

### 7.3 Medication history

The medication history is among the highest-yield and most error-prone parts of clinical assessment. Medication errors at transitions of care (admission, transfer, discharge) are common and often caused by an incomplete history.

#### Aims

- Establish a **complete and accurate list** of what the patient is actually taking (the "best possible medication history").
- Identify **adverse effects** and **interactions**, and drugs that may be causing the presenting problem.
- Assess **adherence** and the reasons for non-adherence.
- Identify **allergies and intolerances.**

#### Sources (use more than one)

1. The **patient or carer**, ideally with the actual boxes, blister packs or bottles.
2. **Primary care records**, pharmacy dispensing records.
3. **Previous discharge summaries and referral letters.**
4. **Medication charts and administration records** (for inpatients and care homes).
5. **Repeat prescription lists** (which may include discontinued medicines).

#### What to ask

- "What tablets, injections, inhalers, patches, drops, creams or other treatments do you use? Anything you take occasionally?"
- "Do you take anything you buy without a prescription: painkillers, anti-inflammatories, antacids, sleeping tablets, laxatives, cough and cold remedies?"
- "Any vitamins, supplements, herbal or traditional remedies?"
- "Any recreational drugs, including cannabis? Any energy drinks or high-dose caffeine?"
- "Any recent changes: new medications, changed doses, stopped medications, dose reductions?"
- "Are you using any medication belonging to someone else?"

For each medicine record: **name, dose, route, frequency, duration, indication, who prescribed it.**

#### Adherence

Non-adherence is very common (up to half of patients with chronic disease take medicines less than as prescribed). It is rarely detected by direct questions like "Do you take your tablets?" (the answer is nearly always "yes"). Better:

- "Many people find it hard to take every dose. How often do you miss a tablet?"
- "How do you manage your tablets during the week?"
- "Have you ever stopped a medicine because of how it made you feel, or because of cost?"
- "What do you think the medicine does?"

Explore reasons: side effects, cost, forgetfulness, complicated regimens, beliefs ("I don't like chemicals"), misunderstanding, fear of dependence, or health beliefs. Unexplained treatment failure (uncontrolled blood pressure, recurrent seizures, high HbA1c, subtherapeutic INR) is non-adherence until shown otherwise.

#### Drugs that cause symptoms: a diagnostic category of its own

Drug-induced illness is a large and frequently overlooked cause of presenting complaints, especially in older adults on multiple medications. Examples:

| Symptom or problem | Examples of drugs implicated |
|---|---|
| Cough (dry) | ACE inhibitors |
| Ankle oedema | Calcium-channel blockers (amlodipine), NSAIDs, corticosteroids, pioglitazone |
| Fatigue, bradycardia | Beta-blockers |
| Constipation | Opioids, anticholinergics, iron, calcium-channel blockers (verapamil) |
| Confusion, delirium | Anticholinergics, benzodiazepines, opioids, steroids, many others |
| Falls / postural hypotension | Antihypertensives, diuretics, alpha-blockers, antipsychotics, sedatives |
| Hyponatraemia | Thiazides, SSRIs, carbamazepine |
| Hyperkalaemia | ACE inhibitors, ARBs, spironolactone, trimethoprim, potassium supplements |
| Gastrointestinal bleeding | NSAIDs, aspirin, anticoagulants, corticosteroids, SSRIs |
| Renal impairment | NSAIDs, ACE inhibitors/ARBs (especially with dehydration), aminoglycosides, contrast |
| Muscle pain / weakness | Statins, corticosteroids, fibrates |
| Gynaecomastia | Spironolactone, some antipsychotics, cimetidine |
| Photosensitivity | Doxycycline, amiodarone, thiazides |
| Bleeding | Warfarin, DOACs, antiplatelets, SSRIs, herbal products (e.g. ginkgo, high-dose fish oil) |
| Hyperglycaemia | Corticosteroids, thiazides, some antipsychotics |
| QT prolongation / arrhythmia | Macrolides, quinolones, antipsychotics, some antiemetics, methadone |

The rule: **"Every new symptom in a patient on drugs is a drug side effect until proven otherwise."** Timing is key: ask what was started or changed in the weeks before onset. Remember also the **withdrawal** of drugs (beta-blocker, antiepileptic, steroids, benzodiazepines, antidepressants, alcohol).

#### Prescribing cascades

A **prescribing cascade** occurs when a side effect is misread as a new medical condition and treated with another drug. Example: amlodipine causes ankle swelling → diuretic prescribed → urinary frequency and postural hypotension → anticholinergic for "overactive bladder" → confusion. Reviewing the **medication list against the symptom timeline** often unwinds such cascades.

#### Allergies and intolerances

Record **the drug, the reaction, the timing and the treatment needed.** Distinguish:

- **True allergy** (immune-mediated): urticaria, angioedema, bronchospasm, anaphylaxis, hypotension, often within minutes to an hour. Also delayed reactions (rash, severe cutaneous adverse reactions such as Stevens–Johnson syndrome).
- **Intolerance or side effect:** nausea from erythromycin, diarrhoea from antibiotics, cough from ACE inhibitor.
- **Non-reaction:** a family member's allergy or a label with no clear history.

Many patients labelled "penicillin allergic" are not allergic on formal testing (the large majority in most series). An incorrect label leads to worse antibiotic choices; clarify the history, and refer for formal testing when appropriate. Conversely, never dismiss a genuine history of anaphylaxis.

Also ask about **latex, food, contrast media, adhesive and other allergies.**

### 7.4 Family history

#### Purpose

- To identify **inherited diseases** and **familial risk** (heart disease, cancer, diabetes, thrombophilia, cardiomyopathy, hereditary haemorrhagic and metabolic disorders).
- To suggest **shared environmental exposure** (infectious contacts, household toxins such as carbon monoxide).
- To understand the **patient's fears**, which are often based on a relative's illness.

#### How to take it

Ask about **first-degree relatives** (parents, siblings, children) first, then grandparents, aunts and uncles. For each affected relative note: relationship, condition, **age at onset or death**, and cause of death. A **three-generation family tree (pedigree)** is the standard tool; draw it when a hereditary condition is suspected.

Useful questions:

- "Do any conditions run in your family?"
- "Have any close relatives died young, suddenly, or unexpectedly? How old were they and what happened?"
- "Anyone with heart disease, strokes, cancer, diabetes, high blood pressure, blood clots, bleeding disorders, kidney disease, mental illness, or neurological disease? At what age?"
- "Are your parents related to each other?" (consanguinity raises the likelihood of recessive conditions)
- "Any miscarriages, stillbirths, or children who died in infancy?" (for suspected genetic or metabolic disease)
- "Are there any similar problems in anyone else at home or at work?" (common exposures)

#### Patterns that matter

| Pattern | Possible implication |
|---|---|
| Premature coronary disease: a first-degree **male relative before 55 or female before 65** | Raised cardiovascular risk; familial hypercholesterolaemia |
| Sudden unexplained death under 40, drowning, unexplained single-vehicle crash, recurrent syncope in a family | Inherited arrhythmia (long QT, Brugada), hypertrophic cardiomyopathy |
| Multiple relatives with breast, ovarian, colorectal, endometrial cancers, or cancers at a young age | Hereditary cancer syndromes (BRCA1/2, Lynch syndrome) |
| Venous thromboembolism in relatives at a young age | Inherited thrombophilia |
| Autosomal dominant pattern (affects each generation, male-to-male transmission possible) | Examples: Huntington disease, familial hypercholesterolaemia, Marfan syndrome, polycystic kidney disease |
| X-linked pattern (affected males related through females) | Haemophilia, Duchenne muscular dystrophy |
| Multiple affected siblings, unaffected parents, consanguinity | Autosomal recessive conditions (cystic fibrosis, sickle cell disease, thalassaemia) |
| Bleeding after minor procedures in relatives | von Willebrand disease, haemophilia |
| Early dementia, early stroke | Genetic dementias, CADASIL |

The family history is a **risk modifier, not a diagnosis.** A negative family history does not exclude hereditary disease (small families, adoption, new mutations, incomplete information, non-paternity).

### 7.5 Social history

The social history describes the **context** in which the patient lives. It affects aetiology, management, and prognosis. Do not delay it: asking early, as part of the story, builds rapport ("Tell me a bit about your life: who do you live with, what do you do?").

#### Core elements

1. **Home and relationships.** Who do you live with? Type of housing (stairs, heating, damp, mould, overcrowding)? Are there smokers, pets, birds? Dependents? Carers? Social support? Any worries about money, housing, safety?
2. **Occupation.** Current and previous jobs, with duration and tasks. Occupational history is critical for respiratory, skin, musculoskeletal, neurological and psychiatric problems, and for fitness for work.
3. **Daily activity and function.** What does a typical day look like? Can they wash, dress, cook, shop, manage money (activities of daily living, ADLs and IADLs)? Do they use aids or help?
4. **Tobacco.** Current, former, never. **Pack-years = packs per day × years smoked** (1 pack = 20 cigarettes). Also e-cigarettes, cigars, pipes, hookah, smokeless tobacco, and cannabis smoking. Exposure to second-hand smoke.
5. **Alcohol.** How often do you drink? What? How much on a typical day? Any binges? Calculate units (UK: 1 unit = 10 mL / 8 g of pure ethanol; in the US, a "standard drink" = 14 g). Use a screening tool such as **AUDIT-C** or **CAGE** (Cut down? Annoyed? Guilty? Eye-opener?). Ask about withdrawal symptoms (tremor, sweats, seizures, delirium).
6. **Recreational drugs.** Which ones, how, how often, last use, injecting, sharing equipment? Ask without judgment.
7. **Diet and exercise.** Typical meals, special diets, caffeine, sugar-sweetened drinks; exercise type and frequency.
8. **Sleep.** Hours, quality, daytime sleepiness, snoring.
9. **Travel.** Where, when, for how long, accommodation, food and water, insect bites, animal contacts, freshwater exposure, sexual contacts, vaccinations, malaria prophylaxis adherence.
10. **Pets, animals and hobbies with exposures.** Birds (psittacosis, hypersensitivity pneumonitis), cats (toxoplasmosis, cat-scratch disease), farm animals (Q fever, brucellosis), reptiles (salmonella), gardening (Legionella, tetanus, sporotrichosis), swimming in fresh water (leptospirosis, schistosomiasis), hunting.
11. **Sexual history** (see 7.7).
12. **Safety and violence.** "Do you feel safe at home?" Screen for intimate partner violence, abuse and neglect (older adults, children).
13. **Financial and educational factors.** Literacy, language, access to transport, health insurance, ability to afford medicines or follow-up.
14. **Legal and military background, incarceration, homelessness, refugee status:** these carry specific health risks and can influence how you communicate.

#### Social determinants

Housing, income, employment, education, food security, social isolation and discrimination affect health profoundly. Asking about them is part of clinical care, and sometimes the most important element in making a plan that works.

### 7.6 Occupational and environmental history

A short occupational history is useful in many presentations. A systematic method:

- **List all jobs held, starting from the first,** with dates. People tend to mention only the current one.
- For each: **What did you do? What materials were used? Any dust, fumes, chemicals, noise, vibration, heavy lifting, radiation, infectious sources? Did you use protective equipment? Do symptoms improve on weekends or holidays?**

| Exposure | Possible disease |
|---|---|
| Asbestos (construction, shipyards, insulation, brake linings) | Pleural plaques, asbestosis, mesothelioma, lung cancer |
| Silica (mining, quarrying, sandblasting, stone work) | Silicosis, tuberculosis, lung cancer |
| Coal dust | Coal workers' pneumoconiosis |
| Mouldy hay, bird droppings | Hypersensitivity pneumonitis (farmer's lung, bird fancier's lung) |
| Isocyanates (spray painting), flour, wood dust, laboratory animals | Occupational asthma |
| Solvents, heavy metals (lead, mercury), pesticides | Neuropathy, encephalopathy, anaemia, renal damage |
| Repetitive tasks, vibration | Carpal tunnel syndrome, hand-arm vibration syndrome |
| Healthcare work, needle exposure | Hepatitis B/C, HIV |
| Dyes, rubber, certain industrial chemicals | Bladder cancer |
| Noise | Hearing loss |

**Pattern clue:** symptoms that improve on days off or holidays and worsen at work suggest an occupational or workplace-environmental cause. Ask about co-workers with similar symptoms.

At home: carbon monoxide (faulty heaters, flues; headache, nausea, symptoms affecting the whole household and worse in winter), damp and mould, lead paint or pipes, pesticides, well water.

### 7.7 Sensitive topics: sexual, substance use, violence, mental health

Patients will answer sensitive questions if you ask in a manner that is **respectful, matter-of-fact, non-judgmental,** and explains why you ask.

#### General techniques

- **Explain the reason:** "I ask all my patients about their sexual health because it can affect many aspects of health."
- **Reassure confidentiality** and explain its limits (e.g. safeguarding).
- **Use normalising language:** "Some people have trouble with... Has this happened to you?"
- **Use direct, clear, non-judgmental words;** avoid euphemisms and avoid assumptions about orientation or partners ("Do you have a partner? Is your partner male, female, or both?").
- **Ask at a calm moment,** preferably with the patient clothed and seated, not at the door.
- **Listen without reaction.** Visible surprise or disapproval shuts the conversation down.
- Offer a **chaperone** and an **interpreter** (not a family member) when appropriate.

#### Sexual history

Useful framework (the "5 Ps"): **Partners, Practices, Protection from STIs, Past history of STIs, Prevention of pregnancy.** Ask: number and gender of partners in the last 3 months and year; types of sex (vaginal, anal, oral); condom use; recent new partners; previous STIs and testing; contraception; pregnancy intentions; any exchange of sex for money or goods; symptoms (discharge, ulcers, pain, bleeding); vaccination status (HPV, hepatitis B). Include sexual function where relevant.

#### Substance use

Ask: what, how much, how often, route, last use, consequences, and whether the patient wants help. Use validated screening tools where available (AUDIT-C for alcohol, DAST-10 for drugs). Remember to ask about **non-medical use of prescription drugs** (opioids, benzodiazepines, stimulants) and about **anabolic steroids and supplements** among gym users.

#### Violence, abuse and safety

Ask universally and privately: "Because it's so common, I ask all my patients: have you ever been hurt or threatened by someone close to you? Do you feel safe at home?" Patients are unlikely to disclose in front of the partner, so create an opportunity to see the patient alone. Recognise indicators: injuries inconsistent with the story, delayed presentation, repeated "accidents", controlling partner who answers for the patient. Know your local procedures and the support available. Never confront the alleged perpetrator.

#### Mental health

Ask routinely about mood, anxiety, sleep and stressors. Use short validated screens (PHQ-2/PHQ-9, GAD-7). Ask directly about suicidal thoughts when mood is low or risk factors exist: "Have you had thoughts of ending your life?" Asking does not plant the idea; it opens the door to help (Chapter 56).

#### HEADSS for adolescents

For young people, a structured, confidential interview is valuable: **H**ome, **E**ducation/Employment, **A**ctivities, **D**rugs, **S**exuality, **S**uicide/depression (some versions add **S**afety). Talk to the adolescent alone for part of the consultation, with clear explanation of confidentiality and its limits (Chapter 60).

### 7.8 Putting the background to work

After collecting these data, **integrate them.** Ask three questions:

1. **What does this change in my differential?** (A traveller returning from sub-Saharan Africa with fever: malaria until proven otherwise.)
2. **What does this forbid or require in management?** (Allergy, pregnancy, renal impairment, interactions, anticoagulation, immunosuppression.)
3. **What does this say about feasibility?** (Will the plan work given housing, finances, caregivers, health literacy, transport?)

Record the information clearly and keep it current; update the problem list.

### 7.9 Worked cases

#### Case 1: The cough that was a drug

*A 61-year-old man with hypertension presents with a persistent dry, tickly cough for 5 weeks. No fever, sputum, wheeze, breathlessness, weight loss or haemoptysis; he has never smoked. Chest examination and chest X-ray are normal.*

**Medication history reveals** that ramipril (an ACE inhibitor) was started 2 months ago, and the cough began a few weeks after.

**Assessment.** ACE-inhibitor cough, a recognised, dose-independent class effect caused by accumulation of bradykinin, affecting a minority of patients, usually dry and persistent, and typically resolving within a few weeks (sometimes up to a month or so) of stopping the drug.

**Plan.** Replace with an angiotensin receptor blocker (ARB); review the cough in 4 weeks; if the cough persists, re-investigate. Document the intolerance. (Angioedema is a rarer but more serious ACE-inhibitor reaction; counsel on swelling of lips, tongue or throat.)

*Lesson.* An expensive work-up (CT chest, bronchoscopy) was avoided by a single question.

#### Case 2: Fever after travel

*A 34-year-old woman presents with 3 days of fever, rigors, headache and muscle pains. She returned 5 weeks ago from a 3-week trip to rural Nigeria, where she visited family; she took no malaria prophylaxis. Temperature 39.2 °C, pulse 110, mildly jaundiced, spleen tip palpable.*

**Travel history** supplies the diagnosis: exposure to a malaria-endemic region without prophylaxis, an incubation consistent with falciparum malaria (most *Plasmodium falciparum* infections present within 3 months of leaving an endemic area, with the majority in the first weeks; *P. vivax* and *P. ovale* can relapse months or years later).

**Action.** Urgent malaria blood films and rapid antigen tests (repeat if the first set is negative and suspicion remains), full blood count (thrombocytopenia, anaemia), glucose, renal and liver function, lactate, blood cultures; consider other tropical and non-tropical causes (typhoid, dengue, viral hepatitis, bacterial sepsis). Treat according to local protocols as a medical emergency if positive; assess for features of severe malaria (impaired consciousness, respiratory distress, hypoglycaemia, jaundice, renal impairment, hyperparasitaemia).

*Lesson.* *"Fever + travel = malaria until excluded."* The history (country, date, prophylaxis) mattered more than any examination finding.

#### Case 3: Occupational asthma

*A 40-year-old man has had "adult-onset asthma" for 8 months: wheeze, chest tightness and cough that wake him at night. Inhalers help a little. Examination is unremarkable between attacks.*

**Occupational history** shows he has worked in a car body shop for 6 years, spraying two-pack paints (isocyanates). His symptoms are worse by the end of the working week and improve on holidays, and a colleague has similar symptoms.

**Assessment.** Probable occupational asthma. Confirmation requires serial peak flow measurements at work and away from work, and specialist assessment. Early removal from exposure improves the prognosis; delay increases the risk of permanent disease. The patient may also be entitled to compensation and workplace adjustments.

*Lesson.* An unexamined occupational history leads to years of treating a disease that is potentially preventable.

#### Case 4: A family history that explains the collapses

*A 19-year-old woman has had two episodes of sudden collapse, both during emotional stress or loud noise (once an alarm clock), with brief loss of consciousness and rapid recovery. She was told she has "epilepsy" (no EEG abnormalities were found). Examination is normal.*

**Family history:** her maternal uncle died suddenly at 22 while swimming, and a cousin "drowned" at 17.

**Assessment.** Unexplained sudden death in young relatives with emotionally triggered collapses suggests **inherited arrhythmia syndrome (long QT syndrome)**, with episodes triggered by adrenergic surges. An ECG with careful QT measurement (corrected for heart rate) is essential and may be diagnostic. Genetic testing and cascade screening of relatives follow.

**Management.** Specialist cardiology; avoid QT-prolonging drugs; beta-blockers; counselling about triggers (including swimming); family screening.

*Lesson.* A family history of *sudden unexplained death* is a high-yield red flag. Ask the specific question ("Has anyone died suddenly or very young?").

#### Case 5: "Resistant hypertension"

*A 55-year-old man is on four antihypertensive drugs, with blood pressure persistently 170/100 mmHg, and referral to a specialist clinic is being considered.*

**Medication history with non-judgemental questions:** he admits he takes "about half" his tablets because the diuretic makes him urinate during long-distance driving shifts, and he experienced erectile difficulty on a beta-blocker. He buys ibuprofen over the counter daily for knee pain, and drinks about 40 units of alcohol per week.

**Assessment.** Pseudo-resistance: non-adherence due to side effects and lifestyle, plus drugs and substances that raise blood pressure (NSAIDs, alcohol). Further investigations would be premature.

**Plan.** Discuss his concerns and tailor the regimen (timing of the diuretic, switching the beta-blocker), advise against regular NSAIDs, address alcohol with a brief intervention, and re-measure blood pressure (consider home or ambulatory monitoring) before considering secondary causes.

*Lesson.* Before labelling hypertension "resistant", check adherence, interfering substances and measurement technique.

#### Case 6: Alcohol history prevents a catastrophe

*A 52-year-old man is admitted after a fall with a hip fracture. On day 2 he becomes agitated, tremulous, sweaty and hallucinating, with pulse 118 and temperature 37.9 °C.*

**Alcohol history from his wife** reveals about a bottle of spirits per day for years, with no alcohol since admission. A brief interview with the patient in the emergency department had only recorded "social drinker".

**Assessment.** Alcohol withdrawal (possibly delirium tremens), 48 hours after the last drink, the typical time window. Treat with benzodiazepines according to a structured protocol, give thiamine before glucose, correct electrolytes, and exclude other causes (infection, head injury, hypoglycaemia).

*Lesson.* Ask about alcohol quantitatively and seek collateral information. A missed alcohol history can turn a routine admission into an emergency.

### 7.10 Common pitfalls

- **Skipping the medication history** or taking it from memory only, without checking sources.
- **Omitting over-the-counter, herbal and recreational substances.**
- **Accepting "allergy" labels without detail.**
- **Not asking for the age at which relatives became ill or died.**
- **Failing to ask about sudden or early deaths in the family.**
- **Not taking an occupational history,** or only asking about the current job.
- **Assuming social class, sexuality or lifestyle.**
- **Asking sensitive questions in front of relatives** (or the opposite: failing to ask because of discomfort).
- **Forgetting the travel history in febrile patients.**
- **Treating the social history as irrelevant** to the medical plan.

---

### 7.11 Key points

- The past, medication, family and social history frequently supply the diagnosis, the cause of treatment failure, or the constraint on management.
- Obtain medication history from more than one source; include over-the-counter, herbal and recreational substances, and explore adherence without blame.
- Treat every new symptom in a patient on drugs as possibly drug-related; watch for prescribing cascades.
- Clarify allergy labels: true allergy, intolerance or no reaction.
- Take the family history by degree and age; ask specifically about sudden early deaths.
- Quantify tobacco (pack-years) and alcohol (units); ask about drugs, travel, occupation and exposures.
- Ask sensitive questions in a respectful, normalising, matter-of-fact way.
- Use the background history to adjust probabilities and to make plans that fit the patient's life.

### 7.12 Self-assessment

1. A man smokes 15 cigarettes a day and has done so for 30 years. Calculate his pack-years.
   *Answer: 15/20 = 0.75 packs/day × 30 years = 22.5 pack-years.*

2. List five drugs or drug classes that commonly cause confusion in older people.
   *Answer (any five): anticholinergics, benzodiazepines, opioids, corticosteroids, sedating antihistamines, tricyclic antidepressants, antipsychotics, antiepileptics, H2-blockers, some antibiotics; also withdrawal of sedatives/alcohol.*

3. Distinguish a drug allergy from an intolerance, with an example of each.
   *Answer: Allergy is immune-mediated (urticaria, angioedema, anaphylaxis after penicillin); intolerance is a predictable side effect (nausea after erythromycin, cough with ACE inhibitor).*

4. Which family history findings raise suspicion of an inherited arrhythmia syndrome?
   *Answer: Sudden unexplained death (especially under 40), drowning or single-car accidents with no explanation, recurrent syncope, or known long QT/Brugada/cardiomyopathy in relatives.*

5. A patient's asthma symptoms improve during holidays. What should you ask next?
   *Answer: A full occupational history: tasks, materials (e.g. isocyanates, flour, wood dust), protective equipment, similar symptoms among co-workers, and the temporal relation of symptoms to work.*

6. Why is "Do you take your tablets?" a poor adherence question, and what is a better alternative?
   *Answer: It invites a socially desirable "yes". Better: "Many people find it hard to take every dose; how often do you miss one?" and explore barriers non-judgementally.*
