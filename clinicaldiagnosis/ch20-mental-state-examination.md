## Chapter 20. Mental State Examination

### Learning objectives

After studying this chapter you should be able to:

1. Conduct and document a structured mental state examination (MSE): appearance and behaviour, speech, mood and affect, thought form and content, perception, cognition, insight and judgement.
2. Use correct psychopathological terms and describe symptoms in the patient's own words as well as in clinical language.
3. Screen for depression, anxiety, alcohol use, cognitive impairment and delirium using brief validated tools.
4. Distinguish delirium, dementia and depression, and recognise organic (medical) causes of psychiatric presentations.
5. Ask directly and safely about suicidal thoughts, and carry out a basic risk assessment for harm to self and others.
6. Assess decision-making capacity in the context of the mental state examination.
7. Recognise psychiatric emergencies and know when to seek urgent specialist help.

---

### 20.1 Why every clinician needs a mental state examination

Mental disorders are common in every clinical setting: roughly one in four adults experiences a mental health problem in a given year, and the proportion is higher among people with chronic physical illness, in general hospitals and in primary care. Yet mental state is often neglected in non-psychiatric settings, with two predictable consequences:

- **Missed psychiatric illness** (depression, anxiety, psychosis, substance use, suicidal intent).
- **Missed physical illness presenting as psychiatric** (delirium from infection, hypoglycaemia, hypoxia, thyroid disease, drug toxicity, encephalitis, brain tumour, subdural haematoma).

The **mental state examination** is to psychiatry what the physical examination is to the rest of medicine: a structured description of the patient's mental functioning **at the time of the interview.** It is a *snapshot* (the history provides the "movie"), based on **observation as much as questioning.** Like any examination, it needs to be documented in a standard format, so that changes over time can be detected.

---

### 20.2 Setting and approach

- **Safety first.** Before beginning, consider the safety of the patient, staff and others: ensure that you can leave the room, that there are no hazardous objects within reach, that a colleague knows where you are, and that help is available. Do not block the exit. Sit at an angle, not directly facing, at a similar eye level.
- **Privacy and time.** A quiet room, unhurried. Switch off phones.
- **Rapport.** Introduce yourself, explain what you will do, and begin with open questions. Acknowledge distress. Do not argue with delusions; do not collude with them.
- **Observe continuously,** from the moment of first contact: how the person walks in, interacts, and looks.
- **Language:** use the patient's own words for key phrases, and avoid jargon. Use an interpreter if required (Chapter 8).
- **Collateral history** from family, friends, GP, carers, police or ambulance crews is critical, especially if the patient lacks insight or is acutely unwell.
- **Physical causes:** always consider whether the mental state could be due to a medical condition, intoxication or drug withdrawal. Measure vital signs and blood glucose in every acutely disturbed patient.

#### The psychiatric history (brief)

Presenting complaint (in the patient's words), history of presenting illness (onset, course, stressors, symptoms), previous psychiatric history (diagnoses, treatments, admissions, self-harm, suicide attempts, violence), past medical and drug history (including recent medication changes), **substance use** (alcohol, drugs, caffeine, prescription medications), family history (mental illness, suicide), personal history (development, education, work, relationships, forensic history), social circumstances, premorbid personality.

### 20.3 The components of the MSE

The following sequence is standard. Document each domain even if it is normal ("no evidence of hallucinations").

#### 1. Appearance and behaviour

**Observe:** age (apparent vs. actual), sex, build, dress (appropriate, dishevelled, bizarre, provocative), grooming and hygiene, nutrition, evidence of self-neglect, scars (self-harm), tattoos, injection marks, odour (alcohol, unwashed), posture, **eye contact** (reduced in depression, anxiety, autism; intense in paranoia), **psychomotor activity** (agitation, restlessness, retardation, stupor, catatonia), **abnormal movements** (tremor, tics, dyskinesias, akathisia, mannerisms, stereotypies), **rapport** (engaged, guarded, hostile, suspicious, disinhibited), cooperation, and signs of response to hallucinations (talking to oneself, looking around, cocking the head).

| Finding | Possible associations |
|---|---|
| **Psychomotor retardation** | Depression, Parkinsonism, hypothyroidism, catatonia |
| **Psychomotor agitation** | Anxiety, mania, agitated depression, akathisia, stimulant intoxication, delirium |
| **Stupor, mutism, posturing, waxy flexibility, negativism** | **Catatonia** (schizophrenia, mood disorders, organic causes; responds to benzodiazepines) |
| **Disinhibition, inappropriate behaviour** | Mania, frontal lobe disorders, intoxication, dementia |
| **Neglected appearance** | Depression, schizophrenia, dementia, substance misuse |
| **Overly bright, flamboyant dress** | Mania |

#### 2. Speech

**Describe (not interpret):** rate (rapid, slow), volume, tone and modulation (monotone, pressured), amount (poverty of speech, volubility), **fluency and latency** (delay before answers), spontaneity, coherence, articulation (slurred, dysarthria), and **abnormalities of language** (aphasia, neologisms, word-salad).

- **Pressure of speech:** rapid, difficult to interrupt (mania).
- **Poverty of speech/speech latency:** depression, schizophrenia (negative symptoms), dementia.
- **Dysarthria / aphasia:** neurological disease (stroke, tumour, intoxication).

#### 3. Mood and affect

- **Mood** is the **sustained, subjective emotional state** reported by the patient ("How have your spirits been?" "How would you describe your mood?"). Record in the patient's words, and rate (e.g. 0–10).
- **Affect** is the **observed, moment-to-moment emotional expression:** range (restricted, blunted, flat, labile), appropriateness (congruent with mood and thought content?), and reactivity (does it brighten when appropriate?).

| Term | Meaning | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Depressed mood** | Sadness, hopelessness | Major depression, bereavement, adjustment disorder |
| **Elevated/euphoric mood** | Expansive, excessively cheerful, sometimes irritable | Mania, hypomania, substance use, frontal lobe disease |
| **Anxious mood** | Fear, worry, tension | Anxiety disorders, thyrotoxicosis, withdrawal |
| **Irritable mood** | Easily angered | Mania, depression, PTSD, personality disorder |
| **Euthymic** | Normal | – |
| **Restricted affect** | Reduced range | Depression, PTSD |
| **Blunted/flat affect** | Markedly diminished or absent emotional expression | Schizophrenia (negative symptoms), severe depression, brain injury |
| **Labile affect** | Rapid shifts | Mania, delirium, frontal lobe disease, pseudobulbar affect |
| **Incongruent affect** | Mismatch with mood or content (laughing while describing sad events) | Schizophrenia |
| **Anhedonia** | Inability to experience pleasure | Depression |

Also ask about **biological (somatic) features of depression:** sleep change (early-morning waking), appetite and weight, libido, energy, diurnal variation (worse in the morning), concentration, psychomotor change.

#### 4. Thought: form (process) and content

**Thought form** refers to *how* thoughts are connected and expressed. **Thought content** refers to *what* the person thinks about.

##### Form

| Term | Description | Associations |
|---|---|---|
| **Normal** | Logical, goal-directed, relevant | – |
| **Circumstantial** | Excess detail, but eventually reaches the point | Anxiety, anankastic personality, mania, dementia |
| **Tangential** | Moves away from the topic and never returns | Schizophrenia, mania |
| **Flight of ideas** | Rapid succession of thoughts linked by associations (puns, rhymes, clangs), difficult to follow, with pressured speech | **Mania** |
| **Loosening of associations / derailment** | Thoughts shift between unrelated topics | Schizophrenia |
| **Thought blocking** | Abrupt interruption of thought | Schizophrenia |
| **Perseveration** | Persistent repetition of words or ideas | Dementia, frontal lobe disease, delirium |
| **Neologisms, word salad** | Invented words; incoherent mixture of words | Severe thought disorder in psychosis |
| **Poverty of thought** | Little spontaneous thinking | Depression, schizophrenia |
| **Slowed thinking (bradyphrenia)** | Slow, effortful | Depression, Parkinson disease, hypothyroidism |

##### Content

- **Delusions:** **fixed, false beliefs that are out of keeping with the person's cultural and educational background, are held with conviction, and are unshakeable in the face of contrary evidence.** Describe the content and its theme:
  - **Persecutory (paranoid):** being followed, poisoned, spied on.
  - **Grandiose:** special powers, wealth, identity.
  - **Delusions of reference:** neutral events carry special personal meaning (the television is talking about me).
  - **Nihilistic/Cotard:** that one is dead or that parts of the body do not exist.
  - **Guilt/worthlessness, poverty, hypochondriacal:** psychotic depression.
  - **Jealousy (Othello syndrome), erotomania** (belief that someone is in love with the patient).
  - **Delusions of control or passivity:** thoughts, feelings, actions are imposed from outside (first-rank symptoms: thought insertion, withdrawal and broadcasting, made feelings, made actions).
  - **Bizarre vs non-bizarre.** Is the delusion **mood-congruent** (grandiose in mania; guilt in depression) or **mood-incongruent?**
  - **Distinguish delusions from overvalued ideas** (strongly held but not fixed or false, associated with emotion: religious or political beliefs, hypochondriasis) and from **culturally normative beliefs.**
- **Obsessions:** recurrent, intrusive, unwanted thoughts, images or urges that the person recognises as their own and resists (contamination, doubting, aggressive or sexual thoughts); **compulsions** are repetitive behaviours performed to reduce the anxiety.
- **Phobias:** persistent irrational fear with avoidance.
- **Preoccupations and ruminations:** health, finances, guilt, a trauma.
- **Suicidal and homicidal ideation:** see 20.5.
- **Worries and anxieties.**

#### 5. Perception

**Hallucinations** are **perceptions without an external stimulus,** experienced as real and in external space. **Illusions** are misperceptions of real stimuli. **Pseudohallucinations** are perceived as being "in the head" with insight.

| Type | Features and associations |
|---|---|
| **Auditory** | Most common in psychosis. Voices (second person: talking to the patient; **third person:** commenting on the patient's actions or discussing them: first-rank symptom of schizophrenia), commands, non-verbal sounds. Also in severe depression (mood-congruent), alcohol withdrawal, substance use, delirium, grief (hearing the deceased: usually normal) |
| **Visual** | **Suggest an organic cause** (delirium, dementia with Lewy bodies, Parkinson disease, alcohol withdrawal, drug intoxication, epilepsy, Charles Bonnet syndrome in visual impairment). Small moving animals ("Lilliputian") in alcohol withdrawal |
| **Tactile** | Formication (insects crawling: cocaine, alcohol withdrawal), delusional parasitosis |
| **Olfactory, gustatory** | Temporal lobe epilepsy, migraine, brain tumour, depression |
| **Hypnagogic/hypnopompic** | When falling asleep or waking; normal, narcolepsy |
| **Somatic passivity** | Bodily sensations felt as imposed from outside (schizophrenia) |

Ask: "Do you ever hear things that other people can't, like voices or noises? Do you ever see things others can't? What do the voices say? Do they tell you to do things? Can you control them?" Also ask about **depersonalisation** (feeling detached from oneself) and **derealisation** (the world feels unreal), common in anxiety, depression, trauma, and substance use.

#### 6. Cognition

Assess:

- **Level of consciousness and alertness.**
- **Orientation:** time (date, day, month, year), place (where are we?), person (own name, who am I?). Disorientation in time first, then in place, is typical of delirium and dementia.
- **Attention and concentration:** **serial sevens** (subtract 7 from 100: five times) or spell "WORLD" backwards; **months of the year backwards**; digit span (forward 5–7; backwards 4–5).
- **Memory:** **immediate** (repeat three words), **short-term/recent** (recall of the three words after 5 minutes; recent events), **long-term** (personal history).
- **Language:** naming, fluency, repetition, comprehension, reading, writing.
- **Executive function:** abstract thinking (proverbs: concrete responses suggest dementia, frontal lobe disease or schizophrenia), planning (clock drawing), verbal fluency (animals named in one minute: <15 abnormal), set-shifting.
- **Visuospatial function:** copying a figure, drawing a clock face.
- **Praxis and gnosis** if indicated.

**Formal screening tools:** Abbreviated Mental Test Score (AMTS), 6-item Cognitive Impairment Test (6CIT), **Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE),** **Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA;** more sensitive for mild impairment and executive dysfunction; a score <26/30 suggests impairment, adjusted for education), clock-drawing test, **Addenbrooke's Cognitive Examination (ACE-III)**, **4AT** and **CAM (Confusion Assessment Method)** for delirium. These tools are screens; they do not diagnose dementia and are affected by education, language, culture, sensory impairment, depression and delirium.

**Delirium screening (CAM):** requires (1) **acute onset and fluctuating course** AND (2) **inattention,** plus either (3) **disorganised thinking** OR (4) **altered level of consciousness.** The **4AT** (alertness, the AMT4 orientation questions, attention by months backwards, acute change or fluctuating course) is a quick bedside alternative; a score ≥4 suggests delirium.

#### 7. Insight and judgement

- **Insight:** the person's understanding that they have a problem, that it is a mental illness (or at least abnormal), that they need treatment, and the consequences. Describe as full, partial, or absent. Ask: "What do you think is happening to you?" "Do you think you need help?" "Do you think you are ill?"
- **Judgement:** the ability to make sensible decisions. Observed from behaviour and from the patient's responses to hypothetical situations ("What would you do if you found a stamped, addressed envelope on the street?" is of limited use). Better to assess actual decisions, such as refusing food, driving while unwell, giving away money.
- **Capacity** to make a specific decision is distinct from insight and diagnosis (Chapter 8). A patient with a mental disorder may have capacity for some decisions and not for others.

| **Anxiety** | **GAD-7** | 5, 10, 15 = mild, moderate, severe |

### 20.5 Risk assessment

Every mental state examination includes consideration of **risk:** to self (suicide, self-harm, self-neglect), to others (violence, harm to children or dependants), and from others (vulnerability, exploitation, abuse).

#### Asking about suicide

**Asking directly does not increase the risk of suicide;** it opens the door to help. Use clear, non-judgemental language, and introduce the topic gradually.

> "You've told me you've been feeling very low and hopeless. Sometimes when people feel like this, they have thoughts of ending their life. Have you had thoughts like that?"

If yes: explore **intent, plan, preparation, access to means, timing, protective factors, and previous attempts.** Examples of questions:

- "How often do you have these thoughts? How strong are they? Can you put them aside?"
- "Have you thought about how you would do it? Have you made any plans or preparations?"
- "Is there anything stopping you? What keeps you going? Who would you tell?"
- "Have you ever acted on these thoughts before?"
- "Have you been drinking or using drugs?"

Do not share or ask for details beyond what is needed for risk assessment, and avoid pressuring the patient.

#### Risk factors and protective factors

| Increased risk | Reduced risk |
|---|---|
| Previous suicide attempt or self-harm (strongest predictor) | Strong social support, family connection |
| Current plan, intent, preparation, means available | Responsibility for children or pets |
| Depression, psychosis, bipolar disorder, personality disorder, substance misuse, eating disorders | Engagement with treatment and services |
| **Hopelessness**, severe anxiety or agitation, insomnia | Future orientation, plans |
| Recent discharge from psychiatric hospital | Religious or cultural objections |
| Male sex, older age (men), adolescence and young adults (including LGBTQ+ young people) | Problem-solving skills |
| Recent loss, bereavement, relationship breakdown, job or financial loss | |
| Chronic physical illness, pain, new serious diagnosis | |
| Social isolation, being unemployed, homelessness, being a prisoner | |
| Family history of suicide, history of abuse or trauma | |
| Access to lethal means (including medications, firearms) | |
| Impulsivity, intoxication | |
| Healthcare workers and other occupations with access to means | |

Risk is **dynamic:** it varies with circumstances, and **risk factors cannot predict who will die by suicide.** Risk assessment guides **safety planning and care,** not prediction.

#### Responding to risk

- **Take it seriously;** do not leave a high-risk patient alone. Remain with the patient and maintain a calm, non-judgemental approach.
- **Remove or restrict access to means** where feasible.
- **Safety planning:** identify warning signs, coping strategies, social contacts, professional contacts and crisis lines, and make the environment safer.
- **Involve the patient's family or friends** with consent (or without it where the risk is imminent and local law permits).
- **Treat contributing conditions:** depression, psychosis, alcohol withdrawal, pain, insomnia.
- **Arrange urgent psychiatric assessment** for patients with active suicidal ideation, plans, or intent, or those who cannot guarantee safety. In an emergency, **follow local mental health legislation** (e.g. detention for assessment) when the patient lacks capacity or refuses help and there is imminent risk.
- **Follow-up** after discharge or crisis: the period after discharge is of particularly high risk.

#### Risk to others

Ask about **thoughts of harming others,** specific targets, previous violence, command hallucinations, paranoid delusions about specific people, substance misuse, access to weapons, and recent threats. Consider the safety of children and vulnerable people. Duty to protect/warn and safeguarding duties vary with jurisdiction; know your local procedures.

#### Self-neglect and vulnerability

Dementia, psychosis, severe depression and substance use may leave patients unable to look after themselves. Assess food, hygiene, finances, home safety, vulnerability to exploitation.

### 20.6 Delirium, dementia and depression

These three conditions are commonly confused in older adults and frequently coexist.

| Feature | **Delirium** | **Dementia** | **Depression** (pseudodementia) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Onset** | Acute (hours to days) | Insidious (months to years) | Variable, weeks to months |
| **Course** | **Fluctuating,** worse at night | Progressive, stable day to day | Often worse in the morning |
| **Attention** | **Impaired** | Preserved until late | May be reduced by poor concentration |
| **Level of consciousness** | **Altered** (drowsy or hypervigilant) | Alert until late | Alert |
| **Orientation** | Impaired, fluctuating | Impaired in later stages | Usually intact |
| **Memory** | Impaired | Impaired (recent memory first) | Variable, patients say "I don't know" |
| **Thought** | Disorganised | Impoverished | Slowed, negative |
| **Perception** | Illusions, **visual hallucinations** common | Hallucinations in Lewy body disease | Mood-congruent voices in psychotic depression |
| **Mood** | Labile | Labile or apathetic | Persistently low |
| **Insight** | Variable | Poor | Preserved or exaggerated |
| **Reversibility** | Often reversible | Progressive (some causes reversible) | Treatable |

**Delirium is a medical emergency**: it signals acute brain dysfunction and carries high morbidity and mortality. **Causes (mnemonic I WATCH DEATH, or "DELIRIUMS"):** infection (UTI, pneumonia, sepsis), withdrawal (alcohol, benzodiazepines), acute metabolic disturbance (hypoglycaemia, hyponatraemia, hypercalcaemia, uraemia, hepatic encephalopathy, hypoxia, hypercapnia), trauma and surgery, CNS pathology (stroke, subdural haematoma, encephalitis, meningitis, seizures, tumour), hypoxia, deficiencies (B12, thiamine), endocrine (thyroid, adrenal), acute vascular (MI, heart failure, shock), toxins and **drugs** (anticholinergics, opioids, benzodiazepines, steroids, many others), **urinary retention and constipation,** pain, sleep deprivation, sensory deprivation. Management: find and treat the cause, review medication, hydrate, correct sensory impairment, orient, mobilise, maintain sleep, involve family; avoid physical restraint; use low-dose antipsychotics only for distressing psychosis or danger to self or others.

**Dementia:** progressive decline in memory and at least one other cognitive domain, affecting daily function. **Types:** Alzheimer disease (episodic memory loss, gradual), vascular (stepwise, focal signs), Lewy body (fluctuating cognition, visual hallucinations, parkinsonism, REM sleep behaviour disorder, neuroleptic sensitivity), frontotemporal (behaviour and personality change, language impairment, early in life), and mixed. **Reversible contributors:** depression, hypothyroidism, B12 deficiency, drugs, alcohol, normal pressure hydrocephalus (gait disturbance, incontinence, cognitive decline), subdural haematoma, neurosyphilis, HIV (Chapter 51).

**Depression in the elderly** may present with cognitive complaints, somatic symptoms, irritability, agitation or withdrawal rather than low mood, and is a major risk factor for suicide.

### 20.7 Medical conditions that present as psychiatric illness (and vice versa)

**Red flags for an organic cause:** acute onset, first episode in an older adult (>40), visual or olfactory hallucinations, disorientation or fluctuating consciousness, focal neurological signs, abnormal vital signs, history of head injury, seizures or headache, no personal or family psychiatric history, recent drug change or substance exposure, systemic illness.

| Psychiatric presentation | Medical causes to consider |
|---|---|
| **Anxiety, panic** | Thyrotoxicosis, phaeochromocytoma, hypoglycaemia, arrhythmia, hypoxia, asthma, PE, caffeine, stimulants, withdrawal (alcohol, benzodiazepines), carcinoid |
| **Depression** | Hypothyroidism, anaemia, Cushing syndrome, Addison disease, B12 deficiency, Parkinson disease, stroke, cancer (pancreatic), multiple sclerosis, drugs (steroids, interferon, beta-blockers [weak evidence], isotretinoin, levetiracetam), sleep apnoea |
| **Mania** | Steroids, thyrotoxicosis, stimulants, antidepressants, stroke (right frontal), neurosyphilis, multiple sclerosis |
| **Psychosis (first episode)** | Substance (cannabis, amphetamines, hallucinogens), drugs (steroids, L-dopa), encephalitis (including **anti-NMDA receptor encephalitis:** young woman with psychosis, seizures, dyskinesias, autonomic instability), temporal lobe epilepsy, SLE, HIV, neurosyphilis, Wilson disease, B12 deficiency, tumour, delirium |
| **Catatonia** | Encephalitis, metabolic disturbance, drugs, as well as psychiatric causes |
| **Personality change** | Frontal lobe tumour or dementia, Huntington disease, head injury |
| **Memory loss** | See dementia |
| **Unexplained physical symptoms** | Somatic symptom disorder, depression, anxiety; but exclude organic disease first |

**And vice versa:** people with mental illness have **higher rates of physical illness** and **die earlier** (by 10–20 years in serious mental illness) due to cardiovascular disease, diabetes, respiratory disease and cancer, partly because of under-diagnosis and under-treatment. **Always examine physically** and investigate symptoms seriously (Chapter 8: psych-out error).

#### Basic investigations in a first presentation or acute change

Vital signs, **blood glucose,** oxygen saturation, temperature, full blood count, U&E, calcium, liver function, thyroid function, B12/folate, CRP, urine dipstick, drug and alcohol screen, ECG; consider CT/MRI brain, EEG, lumbar puncture (autoimmune/infectious encephalitis), HIV, syphilis serology, autoimmune antibodies, medication review.

### 20.8 Documenting the MSE

Write in a standard order, using descriptive language, quoting the patient where valuable.

> **Example (depressive episode):**
> **Appearance and behaviour:** 42-year-old man, dishevelled, unshaven, wearing the same clothes as at the previous visit. Poor eye contact; psychomotor retardation; tearful at times; cooperative but withdrawn.
> **Speech:** Quiet, slow, with long response latency; reduced spontaneous speech; coherent.
> **Mood:** "Empty, hopeless" (subjective 2/10); worse in the morning with early waking.
> **Affect:** Restricted, congruent, tearful; reactive to a small degree.
> **Thought form:** Slowed; logical, relevant.
> **Thought content:** Ruminations on failure and guilt about job loss; hopelessness about the future. No delusions. **Suicidal ideation:** passive thoughts of death ("I wish I would not wake up") on most days; no active plan or intent; denies preparation; no previous attempts; identifies his children as a protective factor. No homicidal ideation.
> **Perception:** No hallucinations; no illusions.
> **Cognition:** Alert; oriented in time, place and person; attention reduced (serial sevens: 2 errors in 5); recall 3/3 words at 5 minutes; no gross cognitive impairment.
> **Insight:** Partial: recognises he is "not himself" and wants help; unsure whether it is an illness.
> **Judgement:** Intact.
> **Risk:** Low–moderate current risk to self (passive ideation, protective factors, no plan); low risk to others; self-neglect mild. Safety plan agreed; follow-up in one week; crisis contacts provided.

### 20.9 Capacity and the Mental Health Act (brief)

Mental illness does not by itself remove the capacity to consent to or refuse treatment. For each decision, evaluate **understanding, retention, weighing, and communication** (Chapter 8). Where a patient lacks capacity because of mental disorder, treatment in their best interests is permitted under capacity legislation in many jurisdictions (for physical treatment), while **compulsory detention and treatment of mental disorder** require specific legal criteria (usually: mental disorder, risk to self or others, treatment necessary, and often impaired judgement), with safeguards, review and rights of appeal. Know the legal framework and the procedure in your own region, and involve psychiatry and legal services early.

### 20.10 Psychiatric emergencies

| Emergency | Features | First steps |
|---|---|---|
| **Suicide attempt or imminent risk** | Self-harm, overdose, expressed intent | Medical stabilisation first (ABCDE, glucose, antidotes, toxicology), do not leave alone, psychiatric assessment |
| **Acute severe agitation or violence** | Threats, aggression, intoxication, psychosis | De-escalation, safety, check glucose, medical causes, calm environment, rapid tranquillisation only if necessary and per protocol |
| **Acute psychosis** | Hallucinations, delusions, disorganisation, risk behaviour | Exclude organic causes, safety, urgent psychiatric review |
| **Delirium** | Acute confusion, fluctuating | Find the cause; treat |
| **Alcohol withdrawal (including delirium tremens)** | Tremor, sweating, tachycardia, hallucinations, seizures, confusion within 6–72 hours | **Benzodiazepines,** thiamine **before** glucose, fluids, electrolytes |
| **Serotonin syndrome** | Agitation, tremor, clonus, hyperreflexia, sweating, hyperthermia, diarrhoea after serotonergic drugs | Stop the drug, supportive care, benzodiazepines, cooling; cyproheptadine |
| **Neuroleptic malignant syndrome** | Fever, muscle rigidity, altered consciousness, autonomic instability, raised CK after antipsychotics | Stop the drug, supportive care, ICU |
| **Lithium toxicity** | Coarse tremor, vomiting, diarrhoea, ataxia, confusion, seizures | Stop the drug, urgent level, fluids, nephrology |
| **Catatonia** | Mutism, stupor, posturing, rigidity | Benzodiazepines, rule out NMS, urgent psychiatric review |
| **Severe anorexia nervosa** | Very low BMI, bradycardia, hypotension, hypokalaemia, hypoglycaemia | Medical admission; cautious refeeding (refeeding syndrome risk) |
| **Postpartum psychosis** | Within days to weeks after delivery, rapid onset, confusion, mood swings, hallucinations, risk of infanticide and suicide | **Psychiatric emergency:** urgent admission, ideally mother-and-baby unit |
| **Acute dystonic reaction** | Oculogyric crisis, torticollis, laryngospasm after antipsychotics or antiemetics | Anticholinergic (procyclidine, benztropine) |

### 20.11 Worked cases

#### Case 1: Confusion, not "psychosis"

*A 78-year-old woman is brought in by her son because she has become suspicious and agitated over two days: she accuses carers of stealing, sees "children in the room," and is awake all night. She has urinary frequency. Temperature 38.1 °C, pulse 104. She is drowsy at times, disoriented to place, unable to say the months backwards; her mood is anxious and her speech is rambling. She has no psychiatric history.*

**Reasoning.** **Acute onset, fluctuating attention, disorganised thinking, visual hallucinations and altered level of consciousness: delirium** (CAM positive). Causes: infection (urinary or chest), drugs, metabolic. The label "late-onset psychosis" would be wrong and potentially dangerous.

**Action.** Vital signs and glucose, full examination, bloods (FBC, U&E, calcium, CRP, LFT), urine culture, chest X-ray, medication review (anticholinergics, opioids, benzodiazepines), CT head if indicated; treat the cause; non-pharmacological measures; low-dose antipsychotic only for severe distress or danger; communicate with the family about the cause and the prognosis.

#### Case 2: Depression and suicidal thoughts

*A 35-year-old woman, a nurse, has had 6 weeks of low mood, loss of interest, poor sleep with early waking, and loss of appetite after a relationship breakdown. On direct questioning she admits to thoughts that "everyone would be better off without me." She has a supportive sister and a 5-year-old daughter.*

**Assessment.** Moderate-to-severe depressive episode with biological features and passive-to-active suicidal ideation. **Explore:** intent, plans, means (as a nurse she has access to medicines), previous attempts, alcohol, protective factors. If she has no active plan or intent, and engages in a safety plan, outpatient management with close follow-up may be appropriate; if she has plans, intent or cannot agree to safety, urgent psychiatric assessment.

**Management.** Safety planning (involve sister, restrict access to means), psychological therapy and/or antidepressant, review within days, address sleep, alcohol, and social support.

#### Case 3: First-episode psychosis

*A 21-year-old university student has become withdrawn over six months, stopped attending lectures, and told his flatmates that "the neighbours are monitoring my thoughts" and that he hears them commenting on his actions. He appears unkempt, speaks little, with blunted affect. He is oriented and attentive, has smoked cannabis daily for two years, and has no insight.*

**Reasoning.** Third-person auditory hallucinations (commentary), delusions of thought broadcasting and persecution, negative symptoms, deterioration in function over 6 months: features consistent with **schizophrenia (first episode).** Consider cannabis-induced psychosis, organic causes (autoimmune encephalitis, drugs, epilepsy), and mania.

**Action.** Physical examination and basic investigations (including urine drug screen), risk assessment, **early intervention in psychosis** team referral, antipsychotic (after assessment), psychosocial support and family involvement, advice on cannabis cessation.

#### Case 4: A "panic attack"

*A 29-year-old woman attends the emergency department with palpitations, tremor, sweating and a feeling of impending doom. She has had weight loss and heat intolerance for 3 months. Pulse 126 (irregularly irregular), BP 142/70. A fine tremor, warm moist skin, lid lag, and a diffuse goitre.*

**Reasoning.** **Thyrotoxicosis** with atrial fibrillation, not primary anxiety. The anxiety is a symptom of the endocrine disease. Many anxiety presentations have a medical cause or contributing factor.

**Action.** TSH and thyroid hormones, ECG, rate control, treatment of thyrotoxicosis (Chapter 48); address anxiety symptoms as part of treatment.

#### Case 5: Cognition screening

*A 74-year-old retired teacher is brought by his wife, who is worried about increasing forgetfulness over 18 months: repeated questions, getting lost in a familiar shop, difficulty managing bills. He is alert, oriented to place and person but not date, MoCA 19/30 (poor delayed recall, visuospatial and executive deficits). No depression on PHQ-9. No focal neurological signs. B12, thyroid function, calcium, FBC normal; CT head shows generalised atrophy.*

**Reasoning.** Progressive episodic memory loss, impaired executive and visuospatial function and functional decline over 18 months, with no delirium, depression or reversible cause: **probable Alzheimer dementia** (mild-to-moderate stage). Review for vascular risk factors, drugs, sensory impairment.

**Action.** Memory clinic referral; driving advice; capacity and advance care planning discussions with the patient while he can participate; carer support; consider cholinesterase inhibitor; address risk and safety.

### 20.12 Common pitfalls

- **Diagnosing a psychiatric disorder before excluding a medical cause,** especially in older adults and in acute onset.
- **Missing delirium,** which is often labelled "dementia," "depression" or "psychosis."
- **Not asking about suicide** for fear of "putting the idea in the patient's head."
- **Collusion or confrontation** with delusional beliefs. Explore without agreeing or arguing.
- **Basing diagnosis on a single snapshot,** without collateral history.
- **Using a screening tool as a diagnostic test.**
- **Not checking blood glucose** in any agitated or confused patient.
- **Assuming a psychiatric patient's physical complaint is "functional."**
- **Ignoring culture and language:** unusual beliefs may be normative in a particular culture or religious group; interpret with cultural humility.
- **Leaving a high-risk patient unobserved,** or discharging without safety planning.
- **Documenting with labels instead of description** ("manic," "psychotic").

---

### 20.13 Key points

- The MSE is a structured description of the mental state at the time of examination: appearance and behaviour, speech, mood and affect, thought (form and content), perception, cognition, insight and judgement.
- Observe as well as ask; describe rather than label.
- Always screen for delirium in acutely confused patients; it is common, serious and treatable.
- Screening tools (PHQ-9, GAD-7, AUDIT, MoCA, 4AT) support but do not replace clinical assessment.
- Ask directly about suicide and harm to others; assess intent, plan, means and protective factors; use safety planning.
- Consider organic causes of psychiatric presentations (red flags: acute onset, older age, visual hallucinations, disorientation, focal signs).
- Mental illness does not equal incapacity; assess capacity for each decision.
- Recognise psychiatric emergencies and know your local legal and referral pathways.

### 20.14 Self-assessment

1. List the components of the mental state examination.
   *Answer: Appearance and behaviour; speech; mood and affect; thought form and content; perception; cognition; insight and judgement (with risk assessment).*

2. Distinguish mood from affect.
   *Answer: Mood is the patient's sustained subjective emotional state; affect is the observed moment-to-moment emotional expression.*

3. What are the core features of delirium on the Confusion Assessment Method?
   *Answer: Acute onset with a fluctuating course, plus inattention, plus either disorganised thinking or altered level of consciousness.*

4. How would you ask a patient about suicidal thoughts, and does asking increase risk?
   *Answer: Ask directly and non-judgementally (e.g. "Have you had thoughts of ending your life?") and then explore intent, plan, means and protective factors. Asking does not increase risk.*

5. Why do visual hallucinations suggest an organic cause?
   *Answer: They are more typical of delirium, dementia with Lewy bodies, drug or alcohol intoxication or withdrawal, epilepsy and neurological disease than of primary psychiatric disorders (where auditory hallucinations predominate).*

6. Describe three features that distinguish delirium from dementia.
   *Answer (any three): acute vs insidious onset; fluctuating course; impaired attention and altered consciousness; often reversible; disorganised thinking; visual hallucinations.*
