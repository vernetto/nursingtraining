## Chapter 24. Headache and Dizziness

### Learning objectives

After studying this chapter you should be able to:

1. Distinguish primary from secondary headache using the history, red-flag screening and the neurological examination.
2. Recognise the dangerous causes of headache (subarachnoid haemorrhage, meningitis/encephalitis, raised intracranial pressure, giant cell arteritis, cerebral venous thrombosis, arterial dissection, acute angle-closure glaucoma, carbon monoxide poisoning) and act on them.
3. Diagnose migraine, tension-type headache, cluster headache and medication-overuse headache from their characteristic features.
4. Use the SNOOP-style red-flag approach and the Ottawa SAH rule to decide who needs urgent imaging or lumbar puncture.
5. Clarify what the patient means by "dizzy" and classify dizziness by timing and triggers into vertigo syndromes, presyncope, disequilibrium and non-specific dizziness.
6. Apply the HINTS examination and the Dix–Hallpike manoeuvre appropriately, and identify patients with vertigo from a central (stroke) cause.
7. Combine history, examination and a limited set of tests to reach a diagnosis and plan for safe follow-up.

# Part A: Headache

### 24.1 Why headache is hard

Headache is among the commonest symptoms in medicine. Nearly everyone has headaches; the overwhelming majority are benign **primary headaches** (tension-type headache and migraine account for most). A small percentage are **secondary headaches** caused by a dangerous underlying condition. The challenge is to find the few without subjecting the many to unnecessary tests.

The approach rests on three questions:

1. **Is this a new, severe, or different headache that suggests a dangerous cause?** (Red flags, 24.3.)
2. **If not, which primary headache disorder does this fit?** (Positive features, 24.5.)
3. **What is the plan for treatment, trigger management, and safety-netting?**

Neuroimaging in patients with normal examinations and typical primary headache features yields very few significant findings and many incidental ones; the history is the primary diagnostic tool.

---

### 24.2 The headache history

Ask about the following, in the patient's words first, then with targeted questions (Chapter 6):

| Dimension | Questions | Points |
|---|---|---|
| **Onset** | Sudden or gradual? How long to peak? First headache of this type? | **Thunderclap** (maximal within about a minute): SAH, RCVS, dissection, CVT, pituitary apoplexy. Migraine builds over minutes to hours |
| **Time course** | How long does an attack last? How often? Daily? Pattern over weeks or months? | Migraine 4–72 h; cluster 15–180 min; trigeminal neuralgia seconds; tension-type 30 min to days; chronic daily headache >15 days/month |
| **Location** | Unilateral or bilateral? Frontal, temporal, occipital, behind the eye? Neck? | Migraine often unilateral; tension-type bilateral band-like; cluster orbital/periorbital strictly unilateral; occipital with neck pain (cervicogenic, dissection, SAH, meningitis) |
| **Quality and severity** | Throbbing, pressing, stabbing, burning? 0–10? | Throbbing: migraine; pressing: tension-type; stabbing: cluster, neuralgia; severity alone does not discriminate |
| **Associated symptoms** | Nausea, vomiting, photophobia, phonophobia, aura (visual, sensory, speech)? Fever, neck stiffness, rash? Eye redness, tearing, rhinorrhoea? Visual loss, diplopia? Weakness, confusion, seizure? | Cluster and TACs have autonomic features; meningitis has fever/meningism; aura points to migraine |
| **Triggers and modifiers** | Stress, sleep, menstruation, foods, alcohol, light, exertion, coughing, straining, posture (worse lying down, worse standing up), pain on chewing | Positional and Valsalva-induced headaches suggest pressure disorders |
| **Behaviour during an attack** | Lying still in a dark room, or pacing and restless? | Migraine: still; cluster: restless and agitated |
| **Previous headache history** | Previous headaches? Is this different? | "Worst ever" or "different" is a red flag |
| **Treatments** | What drugs do you take for headaches, how often, and what is the response? | Frequent use of analgesics suggests medication overuse |
| **Impact** | Work, social life, mood, sleep | Guides treatment intensity |
| **Background** | Hypertension, cancer, HIV, immunosuppression, pregnancy, head injury, recent procedures (LP, epidural), anticoagulants, family history, drugs (nitrates, PDE-5 inhibitors, calcium antagonists), carbon monoxide exposure | |

**Ask about women's health:** relation to menstruation, contraceptives, pregnancy or postpartum status (pre-eclampsia, CVT, RCVS, pituitary apoplexy).

### 24.3 Red flags: when headache is dangerous

The **"SNOOP"** mnemonic (and extended versions) lists features that should raise suspicion of secondary headache:

| Red flag | Possible causes |
|---|---|
| **S**ystemic symptoms (fever, weight loss, rash) or **S**econdary risk factors (HIV, cancer) | Meningitis, encephalitis, abscess, vasculitis, malignancy, GCA |
| **N**eurological symptoms or signs (confusion, weakness, papilloedema, seizure, diplopia) | Mass lesion, stroke, haemorrhage, encephalitis, raised ICP |
| **O**nset sudden (thunderclap) | SAH, RCVS, dissection, CVT, pituitary apoplexy, hypertensive crisis |
| **O**lder age (new headache after 50) | GCA, mass, subdural haematoma, cervical disease, stroke |
| **P**attern change or progression (new, worsening, different) | Mass, hematoma, medication overuse, sinus disease |
| **P**ositional (worse lying down, on standing, or on Valsalva/coughing/exertion) | Raised ICP, Chiari malformation, intracranial hypotension, mass |
| **P**regnancy or puerperium | Pre-eclampsia, CVT, RCVS, pituitary apoplexy, post-dural puncture headache |
| **P**ainful red eye with halos and visual loss | Acute angle-closure glaucoma |
| **P**ost-traumatic | Subdural/epidural haematoma, dissection, contusion |
| **P**ain with chewing, scalp tenderness, jaw claudication, visual symptoms | Giant cell arteritis |
| **P**ersisting/progressive headache with immune compromise | Opportunistic infection, lymphoma |
| **P**olycythaemia, anticoagulants | Bleeding |

Also consider **carbon monoxide** if multiple household members have the same headache, particularly in winter and with fuel-burning appliances.

### 24.4 Examination

A focused but complete examination includes:

- **Vital signs:** temperature (fever), blood pressure (hypertensive emergency, pre-eclampsia), pulse, respiratory rate, SpO₂, glucose.
- **General:** appearance, rash (petechiae, purpura, shingles), neck stiffness (flexion), Kernig and Brudzinski (limited sensitivity), sinus and temporal artery tenderness, scalp tenderness, jaw claudication, dentition, temporomandibular joint, cervical spine mobility and tenderness.
- **Eyes:** visual acuity, visual fields (pituitary), pupils (Horner syndrome in carotid dissection; fixed dilated pupil in glaucoma and third nerve palsy), eye movements (diplopia), red eye, **fundoscopy** (papilloedema, retinal haemorrhages, spontaneous venous pulsation present in most normal people), corneal clouding.
- **Cranial nerves:** all, including facial sensation and weakness, hearing.
- **Motor and sensory examination, reflexes, plantar responses, cerebellar signs, gait.**
- **Cognition and mental state:** orientation, attention, speech.
- **Cardiovascular:** carotid bruits, pulses, blood pressure in both arms (dissection).
- **Signs of systemic illness.**

A **normal neurological examination** substantially lowers the probability of a structural cause for a chronic stable headache, but does **not** exclude SAH, meningitis in the early phase, venous thrombosis, GCA or pituitary apoplexy.

### 24.5 Primary headache disorders

#### Migraine

**Diagnostic features (International Classification of Headache Disorders, simplified):**

- At least five attacks, each lasting **4–72 hours** (untreated).
- At least **two** of: **unilateral** location; **pulsating** quality; **moderate or severe** intensity; **aggravated by routine physical activity** (or causing avoidance of it).
- At least **one** of: **nausea and/or vomiting**; **photophobia and phonophobia**.
- Not better accounted for by another disorder.

**Migraine with aura:** fully reversible visual (zigzags, scotoma, flashing lights), sensory (pins and needles spreading), or speech disturbance, developing gradually over ≥5 minutes and lasting 5–60 minutes, followed by headache within an hour (or with headache). Typical aura evolves gradually; a sudden-onset deficit suggests TIA. Migraine without aura is more common.

**Phases:** prodrome (mood change, yawning, food cravings), aura, headache, postdrome ("hangover").

**Triggers** (individual): irregular sleep or meals, stress and relaxation after stress, menstruation, alcohol (especially red wine), certain foods, bright lights, strong smells, weather changes, dehydration.

**Features that should lead to reconsideration:** first attack of aura after age 40, motor weakness (hemiplegic migraine is rare; exclude stroke), prolonged aura (>60 minutes), brainstem symptoms, thunderclap onset, headache that has changed in character.

**Management:**

- **Acute treatment:** simple analgesics (aspirin, ibuprofen, paracetamol), antiemetic (metoclopramide), **triptans** (sumatriptan, rizatriptan, others; contraindicated in coronary disease, uncontrolled hypertension, hemiplegic or basilar migraine), gepants and ditans in some settings. Treat early. Avoid opioids.
- **Preventive therapy** if ≥ 4 attacks/month or severe disability: beta-blockers (propranolol), topiramate, amitriptyline, candesartan, flunarizine, venlafaxine, CGRP monoclonal antibodies, onabotulinumtoxinA for chronic migraine. Combined oral contraception: use caution in migraine with aura (increased stroke risk).
- **Limit acute medication use** to avoid medication-overuse headache.
- **Lifestyle:** regular sleep, hydration, meals, exercise; headache diary.

#### Tension-type headache

- **Bilateral**, pressing or tightening ("band"), **mild to moderate**, not aggravated by routine physical activity, **no nausea** (mild photophobia or phonophobia possible, but not both), lasting 30 minutes to 7 days.
- Episodic (<15 days/month) or chronic (≥15 days/month for >3 months).
- Treatment: simple analgesics, stress management, exercise, relaxation; amitriptyline for chronic forms.

#### Cluster headache and other trigeminal autonomic cephalalgias (TACs)

- **Severe to very severe, strictly unilateral** orbital, supraorbital or temporal pain, lasting **15–180 minutes**, with frequency from every other day to **eight times a day**, often at the same time of day or night, occurring in **cluster periods** of weeks to months, followed by remission.
- Ipsilateral **autonomic features:** conjunctival injection, lacrimation, nasal congestion or rhinorrhoea, eyelid oedema, ptosis, miosis (partial Horner), forehead sweating; and **restlessness or agitation** (patients pace, rock, or bang their heads).
- More common in men, smokers; alcohol is a trigger during cluster periods.
- **Acute treatment:** 100% oxygen at 12–15 L/min for 15 minutes via a non-rebreathing mask; subcutaneous sumatriptan 6 mg; intranasal zolmitriptan. **Prophylaxis:** verapamil (with ECG monitoring), short courses of prednisolone or occipital nerve block as a bridge; lithium, topiramate.
- Other TACs: paroxysmal hemicrania (shorter, more frequent attacks; absolute response to indomethacin), SUNCT/SUNA (very brief, stabbing). **All TACs require MRI** at diagnosis to exclude pituitary or posterior fossa lesions.

#### Medication-overuse headache (MOH)

- Headache on **≥15 days/month** in a patient with a pre-existing headache disorder, who regularly overuses acute medication for >3 months: simple analgesics (paracetamol, aspirin, NSAIDs) ≥15 days/month, or opioids, triptans, ergots, or combination analgesics ≥10 days/month.
- Features: daily or near-daily headache, worst on waking, poor response to preventives, escalating analgesic use.
- Management: explain, withdraw the overused drug (abrupt withdrawal is usual, except opioids, barbiturates and benzodiazepines which need tapering), start prevention, and provide support. Expect initial worsening for 1–2 weeks.

#### Other primary headaches

- **Trigeminal neuralgia:** brief (seconds), severe, electric-shock-like pain in the distribution of the trigeminal nerve (V2/V3 commonly), triggered by light touch, chewing, shaving, cold air; carbamazepine is first-line; MRI to exclude secondary causes (MS, tumour, vascular compression).
- **Primary stabbing headache, primary cough headache, exercise headache, sex headache:** benign, but each requires exclusion of secondary causes (especially cough and exertional headache, which may indicate posterior fossa lesions).
- **Hypnic headache:** older adults, waking from sleep at a regular time.
- **New daily persistent headache.**

### 24.6 Dangerous secondary headaches

#### Subarachnoid haemorrhage (SAH)

- **Features:** sudden, severe ("thunderclap," "worst of my life," "like being hit with a bat") headache peaking within seconds to a minute, often with vomiting, neck stiffness, photophobia, brief loss of consciousness, seizure, or focal signs (third nerve palsy: posterior communicating artery aneurysm). A **sentinel headache** may precede rupture by days to weeks. About half of patients have onset during exertion or straining, but SAH can occur at rest. Some patients have a normal neurological exam and a "mild" presentation, which is easily missed.
- **Ottawa SAH rule:** investigate for SAH in alert patients ≥15 years with new severe non-traumatic headache reaching maximum intensity within 1 hour, if **any** of: age ≥40; neck pain or stiffness; witnessed loss of consciousness; onset during exertion; thunderclap (instantly peaking pain); limited neck flexion on examination. Sensitivity is very high; specificity low.
- **Investigation:** **non-contrast CT head as soon as possible.** CT performed within **6 hours** of onset on a modern scanner, read by an experienced radiologist, has a sensitivity approaching 100% in neurologically intact patients. Sensitivity falls after 6 hours. If CT is negative and later than 6 hours (and clinical suspicion remains), perform **lumbar puncture** (≥12 hours after onset) for xanthochromia (spectrophotometry), or **CT angiography** according to local protocol. Identify the aneurysm (CT angiography or digital subtraction angiography) and refer for endovascular coiling or surgical clipping.
- **Complications:** rebleeding, hydrocephalus, vasospasm and delayed cerebral ischaemia, seizures, hyponatraemia, cardiac complications. Nimodipine is given to reduce poor outcomes.

#### Meningitis and encephalitis

- Fever, headache, neck stiffness, photophobia, vomiting, altered consciousness, rash in meningococcal disease. See Chapter 41. **Give antibiotics (and aciclovir if encephalitis is possible) without delay.** Do not defer treatment for CT or lumbar puncture.

#### Raised intracranial pressure and mass lesions

- Headache that is **worse on waking, on coughing, straining or lying flat**, with nausea and vomiting, and progression over weeks; papilloedema; focal deficits, seizures, personality change; known cancer. **Neuroimaging (MRI with contrast preferred)** and neurosurgical/neuro-oncology referral.
- **Idiopathic intracranial hypertension (IIH):** young, overweight women; daily headache, transient visual obscurations, pulsatile tinnitus, diplopia (sixth nerve palsy), papilloedema; **normal imaging** (exclude venous sinus thrombosis with MRV); LP opening pressure >25 cm CSF; treatment: weight loss, acetazolamide, visual monitoring, surgery to protect vision.

#### Giant cell arteritis (GCA)

- **Age ≥50** with **new headache**, scalp tenderness (pain on brushing hair), jaw or tongue claudication, visual disturbance (amaurosis fugax, diplopia, visual loss), polymyalgia rheumatica symptoms, fever, malaise, weight loss. Temporal arteries may be tender, thickened, nodular, or pulseless.
- **ESR and CRP** are usually raised (ESR >50 mm/h common; normal inflammatory markers in a small minority).
- **Time-critical:** start **high-dose glucocorticoids immediately** when suspected (do not wait for biopsy) to prevent irreversible blindness; arrange temporal artery biopsy or ultrasound (and large-vessel imaging) promptly. Visual loss is typically permanent and may affect the second eye within days if untreated.

#### Cerebral venous thrombosis (CVT)

- Headache (often subacute and progressive, sometimes thunderclap), seizures, focal deficits, papilloedema, altered consciousness. Risk factors: pregnancy/puerperium, oral contraceptives, thrombophilia, dehydration, infection (ear, sinus), malignancy, inflammatory disease. Diagnose with **MR or CT venography**; treat with anticoagulation.

#### Arterial dissection (carotid or vertebral)

- **Carotid dissection:** unilateral head, face or neck pain, ipsilateral partial Horner syndrome (miosis and ptosis without anhidrosis of the face), pulsatile tinnitus, followed by TIA or stroke. **Vertebral dissection:** occipital or posterior neck pain, brainstem or cerebellar symptoms (vertigo, ataxia, dysarthria, diplopia). Preceding minor neck trauma, manipulation, coughing or sneezing may occur; young and middle-aged people are affected; connective tissue disease is a risk factor. **CT or MR angiography.**

#### Pituitary apoplexy

- Sudden severe headache, visual loss or ophthalmoplegia, nausea, and hypotension from adrenal insufficiency, in someone with a pituitary adenoma. MRI; hydrocortisone and urgent neurosurgical review.

#### Acute angle-closure glaucoma

- Severe unilateral eye pain and headache, blurred vision, haloes around lights, nausea, vomiting; red eye, cloudy cornea, mid-dilated fixed pupil, hard eye on palpation. Ophthalmic emergency.

#### Carbon monoxide poisoning

- Headache, dizziness, nausea, confusion, often several people in the same household or workplace, winter, faulty heater. SpO₂ may read falsely normal; check carboxyhaemoglobin (co-oximetry). Treat with 100% oxygen; consider hyperbaric oxygen.

#### Hypertensive emergency, pre-eclampsia, RCVS

- **Severe hypertension with headache, visual disturbance, confusion** (hypertensive encephalopathy); **pre-eclampsia** (in pregnancy or up to six weeks postpartum): headache, visual changes, epigastric pain, hyperreflexia, proteinuria; **reversible cerebral vasoconstriction syndrome (RCVS):** recurrent thunderclap headaches, often triggered by exertion, sex, or vasoactive drugs (SSRIs, triptans, cannabis, cocaine, postpartum) with segmental vasoconstriction on angiography.

#### Other causes

- **Subdural haematoma** (older people, anticoagulants, falls; headache and confusion), **post-lumbar puncture or spontaneous intracranial hypotension** (headache worse upright, relieved lying down), **sinusitis**, **dental disease**, **cervicogenic headache**, **temporomandibular joint dysfunction**, **otitis**, **herpes zoster**, **obstructive sleep apnoea**, **hypoxia and hypercapnia**, **hangover and caffeine withdrawal**, **drugs** (nitrates, calcium channel blockers, PDE-5 inhibitors, vasodilators, hormone therapy).

### 24.7 Investigation strategy for headache

- **No investigation** is needed for typical migraine, tension-type or cluster headache with a normal neurological examination (MRI for TACs).
- **CT head (non-contrast):** thunderclap headache; head trauma; acute neurological deficit; suspected haemorrhage; as a quick screen when MRI is unavailable.
- **MRI head with contrast (and MRV/MRA as needed):** subacute or progressive headache with abnormal examination; suspected mass, venous thrombosis, intracranial hypotension, pituitary apoplexy, posterior fossa lesions, TAC.
- **Lumbar puncture:** suspected meningitis or encephalitis (after imaging if raised ICP risk), SAH with negative CT, suspected IIH (opening pressure). Contraindications: mass effect, coagulopathy, local infection.
- **ESR, CRP, platelets:** age ≥50 with new headache (GCA).
- **Blood tests:** FBC, U&E, glucose, thyroid function, as clinically indicated; blood cultures if infection suspected; carboxyhaemoglobin; pregnancy test.
- **Fundoscopy** should be done (or optical coherence tomography) whenever raised ICP is a possibility.
- **Avoid reflex imaging** for chronic stable migraine; a normal scan rarely reassures a patient who has a clearly defined clinical syndrome and carries a risk of incidental findings (Chapter 4).

# Part B: Dizziness

### 24.8 Clarify what the patient means

"Dizzy" is a vague word. The traditional division, based on the **quality** of the sensation, was:

- **Vertigo:** illusion of movement (spinning, tilting, swaying) of oneself or surroundings.
- **Presyncope:** feeling that one is about to faint, with lightheadedness, visual dimming.
- **Disequilibrium:** unsteadiness or imbalance, mainly while walking, without a head sensation.
- **Non-specific dizziness:** light-headedness, floating, "swimming head."

However, patients are inconsistent in using these words, and the quality is a poor predictor of cause. Modern approaches therefore classify by **timing and triggers** (the "TiTrATE" approach):

| Syndrome | Description | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Acute vestibular syndrome (AVS)** | Acute, continuous vertigo or dizziness lasting days, with nausea/vomiting, nystagmus, gait unsteadiness and head-motion intolerance | Vestibular neuritis (peripheral), **posterior circulation stroke** (central), Wallenberg syndrome, multiple sclerosis, Wernicke encephalopathy |
| **Triggered episodic vestibular syndrome** | Brief (seconds to a minute) episodes triggered by a specific change in head position or by orthostasis | **BPPV** (head positions), orthostatic hypotension (standing), central positional vertigo |
| **Spontaneous episodic vestibular syndrome** | Recurrent attacks lasting minutes to hours, not triggered | **Vestibular migraine**, **Ménière disease**, TIA of the posterior circulation, arrhythmia, panic attacks, hypoglycaemia |
| **Chronic vestibular syndrome** | Persistent dizziness or unsteadiness for weeks to months | Bilateral vestibulopathy, **persistent postural-perceptual dizziness (PPPD)**, medication effects, cerebellar degeneration, vestibular schwannoma, neuropathy, anxiety, depression |

The first step is to define the **timing** (seconds, minutes, hours, days, continuous), **triggers** (positional, orthostatic, none), and **associated features** (hearing loss, tinnitus, aural fullness, headache, neurological symptoms, palpitations). The next step is a **targeted examination**.

### 24.9 Presyncope and cardiovascular causes of dizziness

Presyncope has most often a **cardiovascular** or **metabolic** cause (see Chapter 28 for syncope).

| Cause | Clues |
|---|---|
| **Orthostatic hypotension** | Lightheadedness on standing; BP drop ≥20/10 mmHg within 3 minutes of standing (supine vs standing); causes: volume depletion, drugs (antihypertensives, diuretics, nitrates, alpha-blockers, antidepressants, antipsychotics), autonomic neuropathy (diabetes, Parkinson disease), age |
| **Vasovagal (reflex)** | Prolonged standing, heat, pain, emotional stress, prodromal sweating, nausea, pallor |
| **Arrhythmia** | Palpitations; tachy- or bradyarrhythmias; sick sinus syndrome; AV block |
| **Structural heart disease** | Exertional symptoms; aortic stenosis, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy |
| **Anaemia** | Pallor, fatigue, bleeding |
| **Hypoglycaemia** | Sweating, tremor, hunger; diabetic on insulin or sulfonylureas |
| **Hypovolaemia / sepsis** | Fever, dehydration, tachycardia |
| **Medications** | New drug or dose |
| **Anxiety / hyperventilation** | Tingling around the mouth and hands, dyspnoea, fear |

Check **postural BP**, pulse, ECG, glucose, haemoglobin, electrolytes, and review the medicines.

### 24.10 Vertigo: peripheral versus central

#### Peripheral vestibular causes

| Diagnosis | Features |
|---|---|
| **BPPV** | Brief (<1 min) spinning on head movement (turning over in bed, looking up, bending), no hearing loss, normal neurological examination; Dix–Hallpike positive (see below). Most common cause of vertigo |
| **Vestibular neuritis / labyrinthitis** | Acute continuous severe vertigo with nausea and vomiting lasting days, after viral illness; unidirectional horizontal-torsional nystagmus; hearing intact (neuritis) or reduced (labyrinthitis) |
| **Ménière disease** | Recurrent attacks of vertigo lasting 20 minutes to 12 hours with fluctuating low-frequency hearing loss, tinnitus, aural fullness; later permanent hearing loss |
| **Vestibular migraine** | Recurrent vertigo of variable duration (minutes to days) with migraine features; history of migraine; often photophobia, motion sickness |
| **Perilymphatic fistula, superior canal dehiscence** | Vertigo with loud sounds (Tullio) or straining; trauma |
| **Ototoxicity** | Gentamicin, cisplatin, loop diuretics, aspirin |
| **Vestibular schwannoma** | Progressive unilateral hearing loss and tinnitus; imbalance rather than spinning |
| **Herpes zoster oticus (Ramsay Hunt syndrome)** | Ear pain, vesicles in the ear canal, facial palsy, vertigo, hearing loss |
| **Otitis media, cholesteatoma** | Ear symptoms |

#### Central causes

- **Posterior circulation stroke or TIA:** vertebral or basilar artery disease; cerebellar infarction or haemorrhage; lateral medullary (Wallenberg) syndrome. Isolated vertigo can be the only symptom of a cerebellar stroke.
- **Multiple sclerosis, tumour, vestibular migraine, Chiari malformation,** Wernicke encephalopathy.

#### Red flags for a central cause

- Sudden onset with **vascular risk factors** (age, hypertension, diabetes, AF, smoking).
- **Inability to stand or walk unaided** (marked truncal ataxia) despite relatively mild vertigo.
- **New severe headache or neck pain** (vertebral dissection, cerebellar haemorrhage).
- **Other brainstem or cerebellar signs:** diplopia, dysarthria, dysphagia, facial numbness, limb ataxia, hemiparesis, Horner syndrome, hiccups.
- **Direction-changing, vertical or purely torsional nystagmus** or nystagmus not suppressed by visual fixation.
- **Sudden hearing loss** with vertigo (anterior inferior cerebellar artery stroke).
- **Continuous vertigo with a normal head impulse test** (HINTS).

#### The HINTS examination (for acute vestibular syndrome)

In patients with **continuous vertigo and spontaneous nystagmus** (acute vestibular syndrome), a three-part bedside examination performed by a trained examiner is more sensitive than early MRI diffusion-weighted imaging for detecting posterior circulation stroke in the first 24–48 hours:

- **H**ead **I**mpulse test: with the patient fixating on the examiner's nose, rapidly turn the head about 15° to each side. **Normal (central pattern):** the eyes stay on target. **Abnormal (peripheral pattern):** a corrective (catch-up) saccade occurs toward the side of the lesion.
- **N**ystagmus: **peripheral:** unidirectional, horizontal (with a torsional component), increased when looking toward the fast phase, suppressed by fixation. **Central:** direction-changing nystagmus on lateral gaze, or purely vertical or torsional nystagmus.
- **T**est of **S**kew: alternate cover test; a **vertical re-fixation movement** (skew deviation) indicates a central lesion.

**Central pattern (any one):** normal head impulse test, direction-changing nystagmus, or skew deviation. **Peripheral pattern (all three):** abnormal head impulse, unidirectional nystagmus, no skew.

HINTS is valid **only in patients with acute continuous vertigo and nystagmus**, not in episodic or positional vertigo, and requires training; its sensitivity drops with inexperienced users. Patients with any red flags or high vascular risk need **MRI (including diffusion-weighted imaging)** and possibly vascular imaging; a normal early MRI can miss small posterior circulation strokes, so repeat if suspicion remains.

#### The Dix–Hallpike manoeuvre (suspected BPPV)

1. Explain; warn that the manoeuvre may provoke vertigo.
2. With the patient sitting on a couch, turn the head 45° toward the side being tested.
3. Quickly lie the patient back with the head hanging 20° below horizontal, maintaining the 45° turn.
4. Observe for **latent (2–20 seconds) vertigo with torsional (upbeating-rotatory) nystagmus**, which fatigues within a minute and reverses on sitting up.
5. Repeat on the other side.

**Treatment:** **Epley canalith repositioning manoeuvre** (posterior canal BPPV), success in about 80–90% after one to three treatments; provide a home exercise (Brandt–Daroff) if needed. Vestibular suppressants (betahistine, prochlorperazine) are not effective for BPPV; short courses can be used for the acute severe vertigo of vestibular neuritis, then discontinued to allow central compensation, and early vestibular rehabilitation encouraged.

#### Other bedside tests

- **Gait and Romberg test;** tandem gait; ability to sit unaided.
- **Hearing tests:** whispered voice, Weber and Rinne, audiometry.
- **Cerebellar examination:** finger-nose, heel-shin, dysdiadochokinesia.
- **Cranial nerves,** including facial sensation and palate.
- **Otoscopy.**
- **Orthostatic BP.**

### 24.11 Disequilibrium and chronic dizziness

Unsteadiness mainly on walking, worse in the dark or on uneven ground, suggests **multisensory impairment** (peripheral neuropathy, vestibular loss, visual impairment, proprioceptive loss), **cerebellar disease, Parkinson disease, frontal gait disorders, normal-pressure hydrocephalus, myelopathy**, medication, alcohol, and **age-related** balance loss. Evaluate gait, vision, vestibular function, neuropathy (B12, diabetes, alcohol), cervical spine, and cognitive function; review drugs (sedatives, anticonvulsants, antihypertensives).

**Persistent postural-perceptual dizziness (PPPD):** chronic (≥3 months) non-spinning dizziness or unsteadiness, worse when upright, with motion, or in visually complex environments (supermarkets, scrolling), often following an acute vestibular event, panic or other illness, with normal examination and tests. Treatment: vestibular rehabilitation, CBT, SSRI/SNRI.

**Anxiety, panic and depression** frequently cause or amplify dizziness; they coexist with organic vestibular disease and should be addressed.

### 24.12 Worked cases

#### Case 1: Thunderclap headache

*A 44-year-old woman, a smoker, has sudden severe occipital headache while lifting a heavy box, reaching a maximum immediately, with vomiting and neck stiffness. She is alert. Neurological examination is normal. BP 168/94.*

**Reasoning.** Thunderclap onset with exertion, neck stiffness, age ≥40: Ottawa SAH rule positive; **SAH** until excluded.

**Action.** Urgent non-contrast CT head (within 6 hours of onset for the highest sensitivity). If CT shows blood: CT angiography, neurosurgical/neurovascular referral, BP control, nimodipine, analgesia. If CT is negative and the time since onset is <6 hours on a good scanner and expert read, SAH is very unlikely but not impossible; if >6 hours, perform LP (≥12 hours) or CTA per protocol. Do not discharge with a diagnosis of "migraine" without excluding SAH.

#### Case 2: Migraine with aura

*A 26-year-old woman has had headaches since adolescence. A typical attack begins with 20 minutes of zigzag flickering lights in the left visual field expanding slowly, followed by a right-sided throbbing headache with nausea and photophobia lasting 12 hours. She lies in a dark room. Three attacks per month. Neurological examination is normal. She takes a combined oral contraceptive pill.*

**Reasoning.** Classic **migraine with visual aura.** No red flags. Imaging is not required.

**Action.** Acute treatment: an early triptan plus an NSAID and antiemetic; headache diary; consider preventive therapy given frequency and disability. Advise on **switching from combined oestrogen-containing contraception** to a progestogen-only or non-hormonal method because of the increased ischaemic stroke risk in migraine with aura (plus smoking, if present).

#### Case 3: Headache in an older woman

*A 71-year-old woman has had 3 weeks of a new, right-sided temporal headache, tenderness over the scalp, pain on chewing, and has lost 4 kg. She noticed brief blurring of vision in the right eye yesterday. ESR 86 mm/h, CRP 90 mg/L.*

**Reasoning.** New headache after 50, jaw claudication, scalp tenderness, constitutional symptoms, amaurosis fugax and high ESR/CRP: **giant cell arteritis.** The visual symptom signals imminent risk of permanent vision loss.

**Action.** **Start high-dose prednisolone the same day** (e.g. 40–60 mg daily, or IV methylprednisolone if visual loss has already occurred), arrange urgent temporal artery biopsy or ultrasound within days, ophthalmology review, bone and gastric protection, and consider tocilizumab or other steroid-sparing agents in some patients.

#### Case 4: Cluster headache

*A 38-year-old man, a smoker, is woken at 2 a.m. nightly for three weeks by excruciating left periorbital pain, with tearing from the left eye, a blocked left nostril and a drooping eyelid. He paces the room. Each attack lasts about 45 minutes. He has had similar episodes in the spring for the last three years.*

**Reasoning.** Strictly unilateral orbital pain, autonomic features, restlessness, periodicity: **episodic cluster headache.**

**Action.** Acute: 100% oxygen at 12–15 L/min by non-rebreather mask or subcutaneous sumatriptan 6 mg. Prevention: verapamil (ECG at baseline and with dose changes), short course of oral prednisolone as a bridge, advise avoiding alcohol during the cluster period, smoking cessation, MRI of the brain/pituitary.

#### Case 5: BPPV

*A 66-year-old woman has had 5 days of brief intense spinning when rolling over in bed to the right or looking up to a shelf, lasting about 20 seconds, with nausea. No hearing loss, headache, weakness or double vision. Examination: normal neurological and ear examination. Dix–Hallpike on the right causes delayed (5 seconds) upbeating-torsional nystagmus with vertigo, lasting 25 seconds.*

**Reasoning.** Classic **right posterior canal BPPV.**

**Action.** Epley manoeuvre in the clinic; reassure; advise on avoiding sleeping on the affected side briefly if desired; re-test; refer for vestibular physiotherapy if recurrent. No imaging or vestibular suppressants are needed.

#### Case 6: Acute vertigo and a central cause

*A 62-year-old man with hypertension, diabetes and atrial fibrillation (not anticoagulated) has abrupt, continuous vertigo with vomiting and inability to stand unaided. His head impulse test is normal, nystagmus changes direction on gaze to the left and right, and there is a mild skew deviation. Left arm ataxia is present. No hearing loss.*

**Reasoning.** Acute vestibular syndrome with **central HINTS** signs (normal head impulse, direction-changing nystagmus, skew), truncal ataxia and limb ataxia in a patient with vascular risk and AF: **cerebellar (posterior circulation) infarction.** The risk of cerebellar swelling and brainstem compression in the following days makes this dangerous.

**Action.** Urgent stroke pathway, CT/MRI (diffusion-weighted) and vascular imaging, neurological observation, neurosurgical review if swelling develops, thrombolysis or thrombectomy where appropriate, anticoagulation decisions after imaging.

#### Case 7: Dizziness on standing

*A 79-year-old woman has episodes of lightheadedness and near-fainting when she gets out of bed or stands from a chair for 3 weeks, since her GP added a second blood pressure drug. Lying BP 148/84, standing at 1 minute 112/66 with HR 74 (a minimal rise). She has diabetes.*

**Reasoning.** **Orthostatic hypotension** (drop >20 mmHg systolic) with a blunted heart rate response (suggesting autonomic neuropathy from diabetes, plus the drug effect). Falls risk is high.

**Action.** Review and reduce or stop antihypertensives, ensure adequate hydration, compression stockings, advice on slow positional changes, check for anaemia and B12, consider fludrocortisone or midodrine if persistent, assess falls risk.

#### Case 8: Headache in a household

*A family of four reports headaches, nausea and drowsiness for several days, particularly in the mornings, during cold weather. Their cat seems lethargic. SpO₂ is 99% in all.*

**Reasoning.** Multiple household members, winter, pets affected: **carbon monoxide poisoning.** Pulse oximetry is unreliable (reads falsely normal).

**Action.** Move everyone to fresh air, 100% oxygen, check carboxyhaemoglobin (blood gas co-oximetry), emergency services/gas safety services, avoid returning until the source (boiler, heater, flue) is checked, observe for neurological sequelae; assess pregnant women and children carefully.

### 24.13 Common pitfalls

- **Anchoring on "migraine"** in a patient with a first or different headache (Chapter 2).
- **Using response to analgesia or triptan** as a diagnostic test; thunderclap headaches may improve with analgesia.
- **Missing SAH** because the CT was too late or poorly read, or because the patient looks well.
- **Failing to consider GCA** in patients over 50 and delaying steroids until after biopsy.
- **Over-imaging chronic stable headache,** or failing to image TACs, thunderclap, or progressive headache.
- **Neglecting the fundi** and blood pressure.
- **Overlooking medication-overuse headache** and treating it with more analgesics.
- **Ignoring carbon monoxide** in household clusters.
- **Dismissing dizziness as "inner ear" or "anxiety"** without checking orthostatic BP, ECG, gait, and HINTS.
- **Using HINTS in the wrong patients** (episodic or positional vertigo, no nystagmus) or without training.
- **Believing a normal early MRI** excludes posterior circulation stroke.
- **Giving long courses of vestibular suppressants,** which slow central compensation.
- **Discharging a patient with vertigo who cannot walk** without a central cause being excluded.

### 24.14 Key points

- Most headaches are primary (migraine, tension-type), but always screen for red flags: thunderclap onset, fever or meningism, focal neurology, age >50, progressive or positional pattern, immunosuppression, pregnancy, eye signs.
- **Thunderclap headache is SAH until proved otherwise**: urgent CT, then LP or CT angiography according to timing.
- **New headache over 50:** check ESR/CRP and think GCA; start steroids early to save vision.
- Migraine: 4–72 hours, unilateral, throbbing, nausea, photophobia; treat early and limit acute medication; cluster: short, severe, unilateral, autonomic, restless; treat with oxygen and sumatriptan.
- In dizziness, define timing and triggers, not just quality: acute continuous (AVS), triggered (BPPV, orthostatic), spontaneous episodic (Ménière, vestibular migraine, TIA), chronic.
- Check orthostatic BP, ECG, and glucose in presyncope; use Dix–Hallpike for positional vertigo; use HINTS in AVS with nystagmus; recognise central red flags (inability to walk, severe headache, brainstem signs).
- Treat BPPV with repositioning, not drugs.

### 24.15 Self-assessment

1. State the Ottawa SAH rule criteria.
   *Answer: Age ≥40, neck pain or stiffness, witnessed loss of consciousness, onset during exertion, thunderclap headache (instantly peaking), limited neck flexion on examination; any one warrants investigation in an alert patient with new severe headache peaking within an hour.*

2. List the three components of HINTS and a central pattern.
   *Answer: Head impulse, Nystagmus, Test of skew; central if the head impulse is normal, nystagmus is direction-changing (or vertical/torsional), or there is a skew deviation.*

3. How is medication-overuse headache defined?
   *Answer: Headache on ≥15 days/month in a patient with a pre-existing headache disorder, with regular overuse (>3 months) of simple analgesics ≥15 days/month, or opioids, triptans, ergots or combination analgesics ≥10 days/month.*

4. Why must steroids be started without waiting for biopsy in suspected GCA?
   *Answer: To prevent irreversible visual loss, which can occur suddenly and affect the second eye; biopsy remains positive for days to weeks after starting treatment.*

5. Describe a positive Dix–Hallpike test for posterior canal BPPV.
   *Answer: After lying back with the head turned 45° and hanging, latent (seconds) upbeating-torsional nystagmus with vertigo that fatigues within a minute and reverses on sitting up.*

6. A patient with vertigo cannot sit or walk without support. Why is this concerning?
   *Answer: Severe truncal ataxia out of proportion to vertigo suggests a central cause such as cerebellar stroke, with risk of swelling and brainstem compression.*
