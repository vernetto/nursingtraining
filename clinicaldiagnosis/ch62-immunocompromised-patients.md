# Chapter 62. Immunocompromised Patients

## Learning objectives

After studying this chapter you should be able to:

1. Classify immune deficiency (primary and secondary) and identify which arm of the immune system is affected from the clinical pattern of infection.
2. Recognise the major groups of immunocompromised patients: neutropenic, HIV-infected, transplant recipients, those on biologics or steroids, splenectomised, and patients with primary immunodeficiency.
3. Assess the immunocompromised patient with fever, respiratory symptoms, neurological symptoms, diarrhoea or skin lesions, using a differential that fits the type and timing of immunosuppression.
4. Manage febrile neutropenia as an emergency, and apply risk stratification (MASCC, CISNE).
5. Recognise opportunistic infections (Pneumocystis, CMV, cryptococcus, toxoplasma, aspergillus, mucormycosis, TB, nontuberculous mycobacteria, strongyloides) and know when to start empirical therapy.
6. Apply prophylaxis, vaccination and screening principles before and during immunosuppression.
7. Recognise non-infectious causes of fever and organ dysfunction in these patients (drug reactions, graft-versus-host disease, immune reconstitution inflammatory syndrome, tumour lysis, cytokine release syndrome, immune checkpoint inhibitor toxicity).
8. Communicate risks, avoid exposures, and know when to escalate care.

> **Note on thresholds and doses.** Antimicrobial regimens, prophylaxis durations and neutrophil thresholds follow widely used guidelines (IDSA, ESCMID, NCCN, ECIL) at the time of writing. Local microbiology, resistance patterns and haematology/transplant protocols take precedence.

## 1. Principles

### 1.1 Why this matters

Immunocompromised patients are an expanding group: cancer patients on chemotherapy, solid organ and stem cell transplant recipients, people with HIV, patients with autoimmune diseases on steroids or biologics, the elderly, diabetics, and those with chronic organ failure. They:

- Develop **more severe infections with less inflammatory response**: fever may be blunted (steroids, elderly), physical signs subtle (no pus, no infiltrate on X-ray in neutropenia), inflammatory markers unreliable.
- Are susceptible to **opportunistic organisms** that rarely cause disease in healthy hosts, and to common organisms with atypical presentation.
- Deteriorate rapidly: delays of hours in antimicrobial therapy increase mortality (neutropenic sepsis, meningitis).
- Have **non-infectious mimics** (drug fever, graft-versus-host disease, malignancy, thrombosis) and **multiple simultaneous processes**.

**Golden rules**: (1) Take fever seriously. (2) Investigate aggressively and early (CT, bronchoscopy, biopsy; don't wait for chest X-ray changes). (3) Cover empirically for the likely pathogens guided by the immune defect, then narrow. (4) Involve infectious diseases and the primary specialty early. (5) Never assume a single cause.

### 1.2 Defects and the infections they predispose to

| Immune defect | Examples of cause | Typical pathogens and syndromes |
|---|---|---|
| **Neutropenia / neutrophil dysfunction** (ANC < 0.5 × 10⁹/L, or < 1.0 and falling) | Chemotherapy, haematological malignancy, aplastic anaemia, drugs (clozapine, carbimazole, methotrexate), chronic granulomatous disease | Gram-negative bacilli (*Pseudomonas*, *E. coli*, *Klebsiella*), Gram-positive (viridans streptococci, *S. aureus*, coagulase-negative staphylococci with lines), *Candida*, *Aspergillus* (prolonged neutropenia > 7–10 days), anaerobes (mucositis, typhlitis); few localising signs |
| **T-cell (cell-mediated) deficiency** | HIV (CD4 < 200), transplant immunosuppression (calcineurin inhibitors), steroids, purine analogues (fludarabine), anti-TNF and other biologics, lymphoma, DiGeorge, SCID | Intracellular pathogens: *Mycobacterium tuberculosis* and NTM, *Listeria*, *Salmonella*, *Nocardia*, *Legionella*, *Pneumocystis jirovecii*, *Cryptococcus*, *Histoplasma*, *Toxoplasma*, CMV, HSV, VZV, EBV (PTLD), JC virus (PML), BK virus, adenovirus, *Strongyloides*, *Cryptosporidium*, *Candida* (mucosal) |
| **B-cell / antibody deficiency** | CLL, myeloma, rituximab, nephrotic syndrome, protein-losing enteropathy, CVID, X-linked agammaglobulinaemia, IgA deficiency, post-transplant | Encapsulated bacteria (*S. pneumoniae*, *H. influenzae*, *N. meningitidis*) causing sinopulmonary infections, bronchiectasis; enteroviral meningoencephalitis; *Giardia*; reactivation of hepatitis B with rituximab |
| **Complement deficiency** | C3 deficiency, terminal complement (C5–C9) deficiency, eculizumab/ravulizumab | Recurrent *Neisseria* (meningococcal disease, gonococcal bacteraemia), pyogenic infections |
| **Asplenia / hyposplenism** | Splenectomy, sickle cell disease, coeliac disease, IBD, stem cell transplant | Overwhelming post-splenectomy infection (OPSI) with encapsulated organisms (pneumococcus, Hib, meningococcus), *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* (dog bite), babesiosis, malaria (severe) |
| **Barrier breakdown** | Central lines, catheters, burns, mucositis, mechanical ventilation, skin disease | Coagulase-negative staphylococci, *S. aureus*, Candida, Gram-negatives, enterococci |
| **Diabetes, uraemia, cirrhosis, malnutrition, alcohol** | Mixed immune defects | *S. aureus*, Gram-negatives, TB, mucormycosis (DKA), *Vibrio vulnificus*, *Klebsiella* liver abscess |

## 2. Assessing the immunocompromised patient

### 2.1 The "net state of immunosuppression"

Estimate it from: (1) **underlying disease** (leukaemia, HIV, transplant), (2) **drugs** (type, dose, duration; steroids ≥ 20 mg prednisolone for ≥ 2–4 weeks is significant; biologics; chemotherapy cycle timing), (3) **neutrophil count and duration of neutropenia**, (4) **lymphocyte count, CD4**, immunoglobulin levels, (5) **time since transplant**, (6) **epidemiological exposures** (travel, pets, soil, construction, TB contacts, water, food), (7) **prior prophylaxis** and **previous infections or colonisation** (MRSA, ESBL, VRE, Aspergillus), (8) **comorbidities** (diabetes, renal failure, malnutrition), and (9) **vaccination history**.

### 2.2 Timeline after transplant (solid organ and HSCT)

| Period | Typical issues |
|---|---|
| **First month** | Surgical complications, nosocomial infections (MRSA, VRE, Gram-negatives, candida, C. difficile, line infections), donor-derived infections; HSV reactivation; in HSCT: neutropenia, mucositis, engraftment syndrome, early acute GVHD |
| **1–6 months** (peak immunosuppression) | Opportunistic infections: CMV, EBV (PTLD), adenovirus, *Pneumocystis*, *Aspergillus*, *Listeria*, *Nocardia*, *Toxoplasma*, *Strongyloides*, TB reactivation, HBV/HCV reactivation, BK virus nephropathy, acute rejection, acute GVHD |
| **> 6 months** | Community-acquired infections (influenza, pneumococcus, COVID-19), late CMV, EBV-PTLD, VZV/zoster, chronic viral hepatitis, fungal infections, chronic GVHD, skin cancers, drug toxicity (calcineurin inhibitor nephrotoxicity), malignancy |

### 2.3 History and examination

- **Symptoms**: fever, rigors, chills (may be absent), cough, dyspnoea, headache, confusion, visual change, abdominal pain, diarrhoea, dysuria, skin lesions, line-site pain, oral pain, perianal pain, arthralgia, weight loss, night sweats.
- **Ask**: medication adherence (immunosuppressant levels), recent chemotherapy cycle and timing of nadir, recent hospital stay, antibiotics, travel, sick contacts, pets, gardening, TB exposure, raw food, sexual exposures, vaccination status, steroid exposure (adrenal suppression), previous infections.
- **Examine with great care and thoroughness**: vital signs including SpO₂; mucous membranes (mucositis, thrush, HSV, necrotic palate), sinuses (tenderness, necrotic turbinate), skin (full body: line sites, pustules, nodules, necrotic lesions (ecthyma gangrenosum: *Pseudomonas*; fungi), petechiae, vesicles), perianal region (inspect; avoid digital rectal examination in neutropenia), lungs, abdomen (right lower quadrant tenderness in typhlitis), fundi (retinitis: CMV, candida endophthalmitis, toxoplasma), neurological examination, lymph nodes, joints, genitalia, catheter exit sites and tunnels.
- **Do not forget steroid effects**: blunted fever, masked peritonitis, adrenal insufficiency (hypotension, hyponatraemia, hyperkalaemia): give stress-dose hydrocortisone in sepsis.

### 2.4 Investigations

Common first line: FBC with differential (ANC, lymphocyte count), U&E, creatinine, LFT, CRP, lactate, glucose, blood cultures (≥ 2 sets; one from each lumen of central venous catheter and one peripheral), urine culture, chest X-ray (often normal in neutropenia: low threshold for CT), sputum/NPA viral PCR (influenza, RSV, SARS-CoV-2, other), swabs. Then guided by presentation: **high-resolution CT chest** (ground glass, nodules, halo sign, air-crescent, cavities), **CT sinuses**, CT abdomen, MRI brain, bronchoscopy with BAL (PCR for *Pneumocystis*, CMV, galactomannan, cultures, cytology), serum **galactomannan**, **beta-D-glucan**, cryptococcal antigen, CMV PCR, EBV PCR, adenovirus PCR, *Toxoplasma* PCR, TB testing (IGRA, sputum, urine), HIV test, stool studies (C. difficile, norovirus, adenovirus, cryptosporidium), LP for CNS symptoms, biopsy of skin or lung lesions. Drug levels (tacrolimus, ciclosporin). Remember: serology is often unreliable (impaired antibody response); rely on antigen/PCR.

## 3. Fever in the neutropenic patient

### 3.1 Definition

**Febrile neutropenia (FN)**: a single oral temperature ≥ 38.3 °C or ≥ 38.0 °C sustained over 1 hour (IDSA), with ANC < 0.5 × 10⁹/L or expected to fall below 0.5 within 48 hours. Mortality 5–20% overall, higher with bacteraemia and shock. Treat as a **medical emergency: antibiotics within 60 minutes of presentation** ("door to needle").

### 3.2 Initial management

1. **Assess and resuscitate** (ABCDE; sepsis bundle): oxygen, IV access, fluids 20–30 mL/kg for hypotension or lactate ≥ 4, vasopressors if needed. Check lactate.
2. **Cultures before antibiotics** but do not delay: blood cultures (central line lumens and peripheral), urine, sputum if cough, swab any suspicious skin lesion or catheter site, stool for C. difficile if diarrhoea.
3. **Empirical IV antipseudomonal beta-lactam**: piperacillin–tazobactam 4.5 g q6h (or 4.5 g q8h extended infusion per local), cefepime 2 g q8h, ceftazidime, or a carbapenem (meropenem 1 g q8h) if ESBL risk or unstable. Add aminoglycoside or fluoroquinolone for septic shock, resistant Gram-negative risk, or suspicion of *Pseudomonas*; add vancomycin (or linezolid/daptomycin) only for specific indications: haemodynamic instability, skin/soft tissue or catheter-site infection, pneumonia, MRSA colonisation, severe mucositis with quinolone prophylaxis, Gram-positive blood culture pending identification. Adjust to local resistance and prior colonisation.
4. **Risk stratification** (to decide inpatient vs outpatient):
   - **MASCC score** (Multinational Association for Supportive Care in Cancer): burden of illness (no or mild symptoms 5; moderate 3), no hypotension (SBP > 90) 5, no COPD 4, solid tumour or no prior fungal infection 4, no dehydration 3, outpatient status at onset 3, age < 60 2; maximum 26; **≥ 21 = low risk**.
   - **CISNE** for stable patients with solid tumours.
   - Low-risk patients (expected neutropenia < 7 days, no comorbidity, stable, reliable carer, access to hospital within 1 hour) may receive oral amoxicillin–clavulanate plus ciprofloxacin as outpatients in centres with protocols. High-risk: admit, IV therapy.
5. **Review at 48–72 hours**: if culture-positive: tailor; if culture-negative and afebrile for ≥ 48 hours with ANC recovering (> 0.5 and rising): consider stopping antibiotics (de-escalation strategies); persistent fever **> 4–7 days** on broad-spectrum antibiotics: search for fungal infection (CT chest/sinuses/abdomen, galactomannan, β-D-glucan) and start empirical or pre-emptive antifungal therapy (liposomal amphotericin B 3 mg/kg, caspofungin/micafungin, or voriconazole/isavuconazole depending on prophylaxis and CT findings), review line (remove infected central line in *S. aureus*, *Pseudomonas*, *Candida*, mycobacteria, persistent bacteraemia > 72 h), look for abscess, typhlitis, drug fever.
6. **Growth factors (G-CSF)**: consider in high-risk patients; not routine for established FN except in selected circumstances.
7. **Infection control**: protective isolation per local policy, hand hygiene, no rectal temperatures or enemas, no digital rectal examination, avoid fresh flowers/plants, neutropenic diet (evidence is weak).

### 3.3 Specific syndromes in neutropenia

- **Typhlitis (neutropenic enterocolitis)**: right lower quadrant pain, fever, diarrhoea, thickened caecum on CT/ultrasound; treat with bowel rest, broad-spectrum cover including anaerobes (piperacillin–tazobactam or meropenem), surgical review for perforation.
- **Perianal infection**: pain, tenderness; do not perform digital examination; cover for anaerobes and Gram-negatives.
- **Mucositis**: HSV and candida contribute; swab and treat.
- **Pneumonia**: CT within hours (sensitivity of chest X-ray low); cover bacteria and consider fungal; BAL when stable.
- **Skin lesions**: biopsy any new skin lesion for culture and histology (ecthyma gangrenosum, fungal infections, Sweet syndrome, leukaemia cutis).
- **Sinus pain/necrosis**: CT sinus and ENT urgently for invasive fungal sinusitis (mucormycosis, aspergillosis).
- **Central line infection**: culture from line and peripheral vein; treat as above; remove line if complicated infection.

## 4. HIV and AIDS (diagnostic aspects)

### 4.1 Recognition

HIV infection is diagnosed by 4th-generation antigen/antibody combination assay (detects p24 antigen and antibodies; window ≈ 2–4 weeks) confirmed by differentiation immunoassay and/or HIV RNA. Offer testing to all patients with indicator conditions: tuberculosis, hepatitis B/C, STIs, shingles, unexplained weight loss, lymphadenopathy, oral candida, seborrhoeic dermatitis (severe), persistent diarrhoea, cytopenias, pneumonia, mononucleosis-like illness, lymphoma, cervical/anal dysplasia, unexplained neuropathy or dementia, and in everyone with unknown status in high-prevalence areas. **Acute HIV** (2–4 weeks after exposure): fever, sore throat, rash (maculopapular), lymphadenopathy, myalgia, mucocutaneous ulcers, diarrhoea; test with HIV RNA if antibody-negative. Early antiretroviral therapy (ART) improves outcomes and prevents transmission (U=U: undetectable = untransmittable).

### 4.2 Opportunistic infections by CD4 count

| CD4 (cells/µL) | Illnesses |
|---|---|
| > 500 | Acute retroviral syndrome, persistent generalised lymphadenopathy, candida vaginitis |
| 200–500 | TB (pulmonary), bacterial pneumonia, herpes zoster, oral thrush, Kaposi sarcoma, oral hairy leukoplakia, cervical dysplasia, ITP |
| 100–200 | *Pneumocystis jirovecii* pneumonia (PCP), oesophageal candidiasis, cryptosporidiosis, progressive multifocal leukoencephalopathy, HIV wasting, HIV-associated dementia, histoplasmosis, coccidioidomycosis |
| 50–100 | *Toxoplasma* encephalitis, cryptococcal meningitis, extrapulmonary TB, CNS lymphoma, microsporidiosis, HIV nephropathy |
| < 50 | CMV retinitis/colitis, disseminated MAC, disseminated histoplasmosis, bacillary angiomatosis, aspergillosis |

### 4.3 Management essentials

- **Start ART promptly** after diagnosis (same-day or within days, once opportunistic infections requiring special timing are excluded or treated). Timing: PCP within 2 weeks; TB within 2–8 weeks depending on CD4 (earlier if CD4 < 50); **cryptococcal meningitis: delay ART for 4–6 weeks**; CMV retinitis: immediate; toxoplasma: 2–3 weeks.
- **Check** baseline: CD4, viral load, genotype resistance, HLA-B*5701 (abacavir), hepatitis B/C, TB screen, syphilis, G6PD, lipids, glucose, renal function, pregnancy; offer vaccination.
- **Prophylaxis**: co-trimoxazole for CD4 < 200 (PCP, toxoplasma), azithromycin for MAC (only if CD4 < 50 and not starting ART), isoniazid preventive therapy/rifapentine (TB infection), fluconazole for cryptococcal antigenaemia.
- **Immune reconstitution inflammatory syndrome (IRIS)**: paradoxical worsening of an infection (TB, cryptococcus, CMV) or unmasking of a subclinical infection within weeks of starting ART, as CD4 recovers; treat the infection, continue ART, add steroids for TB-IRIS if severe.
- **Pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP)** and **post-exposure prophylaxis (PEP, within 72 hours)** for prevention; screen for STIs.

*See chapter 53 for additional detail on HIV and STIs.*

## 5. Specific opportunistic infections: recognition and first steps

### 5.1 Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia (PCP)

Subacute progressive dry cough, exertional dyspnoea, fever, hypoxia (desaturation on exertion), bilateral diffuse infiltrates; **in non-HIV patients (steroids, haematological malignancy, transplant), onset is more fulminant**. Chest X-ray: bilateral perihilar interstitial infiltrates (may be normal); CT: ground-glass opacities with sparing of subpleural regions, cysts, pneumothorax. LDH raised, β-D-glucan elevated (high sensitivity), induced sputum or BAL for PCR/IFA. **Treatment**: high-dose co-trimoxazole (15–20 mg/kg/day trimethoprim in 3–4 divided doses) for 21 days (check G6PD, monitor K, creatinine, rash, bone marrow); add **prednisolone** 40 mg twice daily for 5 days then taper if PaO₂ < 70 mmHg (9.3 kPa) or A–a gradient ≥ 35 (HIV); alternatives: primaquine + clindamycin, atovaquone (mild), IV pentamidine. Prophylaxis for those on prolonged steroids (≥ 20 mg prednisolone for ≥ 4 weeks plus another immunosuppressant or underlying condition), purine analogues, HSCT, rituximab regimens, and CD4 < 200.

### 5.2 Invasive fungal infections

- **Invasive aspergillosis**: prolonged neutropenia (> 10 days), HSCT/GVHD, steroid use, lung and liver transplant, severe influenza/COVID. Fever persistent despite antibiotics, cough, pleuritic pain, haemoptysis, sinus disease, CNS involvement. CT: nodules with halo sign (early), air-crescent (recovery), cavitation; serum galactomannan (less sensitive in prophylaxed patients and in non-neutropenic), BAL galactomannan, culture, PCR. **Treatment**: voriconazole (levels, interactions), isavuconazole, or liposomal amphotericin B; surgery for localised lesions near vessels.
- **Mucormycosis**: diabetes with ketoacidosis, iron overload (deferoxamine), neutropenia, steroids, transplant. Rhino-orbital-cerebral (facial pain, black necrotic eschar of palate or turbinate, proptosis, ophthalmoplegia), pulmonary, cutaneous. Emergency: **surgical debridement plus liposomal amphotericin B (5–10 mg/kg)**; azoles other than posaconazole/isavuconazole are inactive; correct hyperglycaemia/acidosis.
- **Invasive candidiasis**: candidaemia (all positive blood culture for *Candida* must be treated, remove catheters, ophthalmology exam, echocardiography), risk factors: broad-spectrum antibiotics, TPN, central lines, abdominal surgery, neutropenia. Echinocandin first line (caspofungin, micafungin, anidulafungin), de-escalate to fluconazole for susceptible isolates when stable; *Candida auris*: infection control, echinocandin.
- **Cryptococcosis**: meningoencephalitis in HIV (CD4 < 100), transplant, steroids; headache, fever, confusion for weeks, raised ICP; cryptococcal antigen (serum and CSF), India ink, CSF opening pressure often high. **Induction: liposomal amphotericin B + flucytosine for 2 weeks** (or single high-dose amphotericin in some settings), then fluconazole consolidation; therapeutic lumbar punctures for opening pressure > 25 cm H₂O; delay ART 4–6 weeks.
- **Histoplasmosis, coccidioidomycosis, blastomycosis, talaromycosis**: endemic mycoses in specific regions; disseminated disease in immunocompromised (fever, pancytopenia, hepatosplenomegaly, skin lesions); antigen testing (urine/serum), amphotericin then itraconazole.
- **Pneumocystis, Aspergillus, and Cryptococcus** are the key three to remember.

### 5.3 Viral infections

- **CMV**: the commonest opportunistic virus in transplant (D+/R− highest risk) and advanced HIV; syndromes: fever with leukopenia, pneumonitis, colitis, hepatitis, retinitis (floaters, visual loss; ophthalmology), oesophagitis; diagnosis by quantitative PCR in blood, tissue histology (owl's eye inclusions), BAL (viral load less specific); treatment: IV ganciclovir 5 mg/kg q12h (adjust for renal function; monitor neutropenia) or oral valganciclovir 900 mg twice daily, foscarnet if resistance or cytopenia, letermovir/maribavir prophylaxis or treatment in some settings.
- **HSV/VZV**: severe mucocutaneous HSV, oesophagitis, encephalitis, hepatitis; disseminated zoster. IV aciclovir 10 mg/kg q8h (adjust for renal function), then oral.
- **EBV and PTLD (post-transplant lymphoproliferative disorder)**: fever, lymphadenopathy, masses, GI bleeding, graft dysfunction; EBV PCR, PET/CT, biopsy; reduce immunosuppression, rituximab ± chemotherapy.
- **Adenovirus, BK virus (haemorrhagic cystitis in HSCT, nephropathy in kidney transplant), JC virus (PML: progressive neurological deficits, MRI white-matter lesions, CSF JC PCR), HHV-6, parvovirus B19 (pure red cell aplasia), hepatitis B reactivation (rituximab, chemotherapy: screen HBsAg and anti-HBc before treatment; prophylaxis with entecavir or tenofovir), hepatitis E (chronic), respiratory viruses (RSV, influenza, SARS-CoV-2, parainfluenza: prolonged shedding, severe disease; antivirals early), norovirus (chronic diarrhoea).**

### 5.4 Mycobacterial and other bacterial infections

- **Tuberculosis**: reactivation risk increased 10–100-fold in HIV, anti-TNF therapy, transplantation, steroids, silicosis, diabetes, CKD. Atypical presentation: extrapulmonary, disseminated (miliary), normal chest X-ray, negative tuberculin/IGRA (anergy). Screen before biologics, steroids, and transplant (IGRA/TST; chest X-ray) and treat latent TB (e.g., 3HP, 4R, 6–9H). See chapter 46.
- **Non-tuberculous mycobacteria (MAC)**: disseminated in advanced HIV; lung disease in structural lung disease; skin and soft-tissue after surgery.
- **Nocardia**: lung nodules/cavity, brain abscess, skin; sulfonamide-based therapy; MRI brain in any case.
- **Listeria monocytogenes**: meningitis/bacteraemia in the elderly, pregnancy, steroid, transplant; add ampicillin to empirical meningitis therapy (cephalosporins are inactive).
- **Legionella, Salmonella, Strongyloides hyperinfection** (steroids in patients from endemic areas: gut organisms → bacteraemia and meningitis; screen serology and treat empirically with ivermectin before immunosuppression in those from tropical/subtropical regions).
- **Encapsulated organism sepsis in asplenia**: see section 8.
- **Clostridioides difficile**: more frequent and severe; fidaxomicin or oral vancomycin.
- **MRSA, VRE, ESBL, CRE, *Pseudomonas*, *Stenotrophomonas*, *Acinetobacter***: knowledge of colonisation and local resistance guides empirical therapy.

### 5.5 Parasites

*Toxoplasma* (reactivation in HIV: ring-enhancing brain lesions; treat with pyrimethamine + sulfadiazine + folinic acid, or co-trimoxazole; empirical trial for 2 weeks in HIV with compatible imaging and positive serology), *Cryptosporidium* and *Cyclospora*/*Cystoisospora* (chronic diarrhoea in advanced HIV; nitazoxanide, ART), *Leishmania* (visceral in HIV and transplant), *Strongyloides*, *Giardia*, *Trypanosoma cruzi* reactivation (Chagas), malaria in asplenic or non-immune.

## 6. Syndromic approach: matching the picture to the defect

### 6.1 Pulmonary infiltrates

**Time course and pattern** guide the differential:

- **Acute (hours–days) focal consolidation**: bacterial pneumonia, aspiration, *Legionella*.
- **Subacute diffuse ground-glass with hypoxia**: PCP, CMV, viral (influenza, RSV, COVID-19), drug toxicity (amiodarone, bleomycin, methotrexate, checkpoint inhibitors), pulmonary oedema, diffuse alveolar haemorrhage, ARDS, engraftment syndrome, radiation pneumonitis.
- **Nodules/masses ± cavitation**: fungal (Aspergillus, Mucorales, Cryptococcus), *Nocardia*, TB, septic emboli, lymphoma/PTLD, metastases.
- **Pleural effusion**: parapneumonic, TB, malignancy, heart failure.
- **Bronchoscopy with BAL** early; transbronchial or surgical lung biopsy when non-diagnostic.
- **Non-infectious causes**: pulmonary embolism, heart failure, pulmonary haemorrhage, transfusion-related lung injury, drug toxicity, relapse of malignancy.

### 6.2 Neurological presentations

Headache, confusion, seizures, focal deficits, meningism: **CT/MRI first (before LP if focal signs or raised ICP)**, then lumbar puncture with opening pressure. Think: bacterial meningitis (add *Listeria* cover), cryptococcus, TB, toxoplasma abscess, PML, CMV, HSV encephalitis, VZV vasculopathy, nocardial/fungal abscess, CNS lymphoma, neurosyphilis, PRES (calcineurin inhibitors), drug toxicity, metabolic. Send CSF for: cell count, protein, glucose, Gram stain/culture, cryptococcal antigen, PCR panel (HSV, VZV, CMV, EBV, JC, enterovirus), AFB/TB PCR, VDRL, cytology/flow cytometry; serum toxoplasma IgG.

### 6.3 Gastrointestinal presentations

Diarrhoea: *C. difficile*, CMV colitis, cryptosporidium, microsporidium, norovirus, adenovirus, *Salmonella*, GVHD, drugs (mycophenolate, magnesium), neutropenic enterocolitis; odynophagia: candida, HSV, CMV oesophagitis (endoscopy and biopsy); hepatitis: HBV/HCV reactivation, CMV, adenovirus, drug-induced, veno-occlusive disease (sinusoidal obstruction syndrome), GVHD; abdominal pain: typhlitis, perforation (steroids mask signs), pancreatitis.

### 6.4 Skin

Biopsy for histology and culture (bacterial, fungal, AFB, viral). Think: zoster (disseminated or atypical), HSV, molluscum, Kaposi sarcoma, bacillary angiomatosis, cryptococcal skin lesions, ecthyma gangrenosum, mucormycosis, leukaemia cutis, drug eruption, GVHD, Sweet syndrome, vasculitis.

### 6.5 Fever without a source

Persistent fever in the immunocompromised patient: repeat cultures, imaging (CT chest/abdomen/sinuses, PET/CT), echocardiography (endocarditis), fungal markers, viral PCRs, drug review (beta-lactams, allopurinol, amphotericin, anticonvulsants), thrombosis (DVT, line thrombosis), transfusion reactions, GVHD, malignancy (lymphoma, leukaemia), haemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH: ferritin > 10,000, cytopenias, hepatosplenomegaly, triglycerides high), adrenal insufficiency, thyroiditis, autoimmune disease flare, and tumour fever.

## 7. Non-infectious complications that mimic or complicate infection

- **Graft-versus-host disease (GVHD)** after allogeneic HSCT: acute (skin rash maculopapular, cholestatic hepatitis, diarrhoea with abdominal pain), chronic (skin sclerosis, dry eyes/mouth, bronchiolitis obliterans, liver). Biopsy; steroids, ruxolitinib; infection prophylaxis.
- **Organ rejection** (transplant): graft dysfunction (creatinine rise, liver tests, dyspnoea); biopsy; adjust immunosuppression. Balancing rejection and infection is the transplant physician's task.
- **Immune reconstitution inflammatory syndrome (IRIS)**: see section 4.3; also after neutrophil recovery, or withdrawal of immunosuppressants/biologics.
- **Tumour lysis syndrome**: hyperkalaemia, hyperphosphataemia, hypocalcaemia, hyperuricaemia, AKI after chemotherapy in high-grade lymphomas and leukaemias; prevent with hydration, allopurinol/rasburicase; treat as per chapter 43.
- **Cytokine release syndrome (CRS) and neurotoxicity (ICANS)** after CAR-T cells or bispecific antibodies: fever, hypotension, hypoxia; grade-based management with tocilizumab and steroids; exclude infection.
- **Immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI) immune-related adverse events**: colitis, pneumonitis, hepatitis, thyroiditis, hypophysitis, adrenal insufficiency, myocarditis, dermatitis, nephritis, diabetes, neurotoxicity; weeks to months after start; treat with steroids ± infliximab/vedolizumab/mycophenolate; hold the drug.
- **Drug fever and drug-induced cytopenias**.
- **Thrombosis** and **transfusion complications**.
- **Adrenal insufficiency** after long-term steroids: collapse, hypotension, abdominal pain; hydrocortisone 100 mg IV then 50 mg q6h in stress.
- **Secondary malignancy** (skin cancers in transplant recipients, lymphoma, cervical and anal dysplasia in HIV).

## 8. Asplenia and hyposplenism

### 8.1 Risk

Overwhelming post-splenectomy infection (OPSI): sudden onset flu-like illness progressing to septic shock, DIC and purpura within hours, mortality 40–70%; lifetime risk ≈ 3–5% (highest in the first 2 years, children), caused by *S. pneumoniae* (50–70%), *H. influenzae* type b, *N. meningitidis*, and others (*Capnocytophaga*, *E. coli*). Also: severe malaria and babesiosis.

### 8.2 Prevention and management

- **Vaccines** (ideally ≥ 2 weeks before elective splenectomy, or 2 weeks after emergency surgery): pneumococcal (conjugate then polysaccharide, PCV20/PCV21 per local schedule), Hib, meningococcal ACWY and B, annual influenza, COVID-19; booster pneumococcal polysaccharide every 5 years where recommended.
- **Antibiotic prophylaxis**: penicillin V 250–500 mg twice daily (or amoxicillin, or macrolide if allergic) for at least 2 years after splenectomy in adults and until age 5 in children; lifelong in high-risk patients (per guideline); and a **stand-by antibiotic supply** (amoxicillin–clavulanate) for patients to self-start for fever.
- **Education**: carry a medical alert card or bracelet; any fever (≥ 38 °C), rigors or unexplained illness requires **immediate** medical attention and **immediate IV antibiotics** (ceftriaxone 2 g) — do not wait for results; animal bites require urgent antibiotics; travel to malaria areas needs rigorous prevention.

## 9. Prevention in immunosuppressed patients

### 9.1 Before starting immunosuppression: screening checklist

- **Infections**: HIV, HBV (HBsAg, anti-HBc, anti-HBs), HCV, TB (IGRA/TST, chest X-ray), VZV and CMV and EBV serostatus, syphilis, strongyloides (if endemic exposure), toxoplasma serology (transplant), measles/varicella immunity.
- **Vaccination (inactivated)**: influenza, pneumococcal, COVID-19, hepatitis B, hepatitis A, Tdap, recombinant zoster vaccine (age ≥ 18 in immunocompromised), HPV, meningococcal, RSV (older age/risk), Hib in asplenia. **Give at least 2–4 weeks before immunosuppression** where possible. **Live vaccines** (MMR, varicella, yellow fever, oral typhoid, live attenuated influenza, BCG, oral polio) are generally **contraindicated** during significant immunosuppression (and for 3 months after stopping high-dose steroids, 6–12 months after chemotherapy, 24 months after HSCT); household contacts should be vaccinated (except for specific exceptions, e.g., smallpox, oral polio).
- **Dental review**, treat infections, cervical screening.

### 9.2 Prophylaxis

| Indication | Prophylaxis |
|---|---|
| Prolonged neutropenia (ANC < 0.1 for > 7 days: AML, HSCT) | Fluoroquinolone (levofloxacin), antifungal (posaconazole, fluconazole, micafungin), antiviral (aciclovir) |
| Steroids ≥ 20 mg prednisolone for ≥ 4 weeks, haematological malignancy, transplant | *Pneumocystis*: co-trimoxazole 480–960 mg daily or three times weekly; alternatives: atovaquone, dapsone (check G6PD), pentamidine |
| Transplant (CMV risk) | Valganciclovir or letermovir prophylaxis, or pre-emptive monitoring |
| Hepatitis B-positive on chemotherapy/rituximab | Entecavir or tenofovir |
| HSV/VZV seropositive on chemo/HSCT | Aciclovir/valaciclovir |
| Latent TB | Isoniazid ± rifampicin regimens, with specialist input |
| HIV CD4 < 200 | Co-trimoxazole |
| Hypogammaglobulinaemia with recurrent infections | Immunoglobulin replacement therapy |
| Splenectomy | See above |
| Strongyloides risk | Ivermectin |

### 9.3 Avoiding exposures

Hand hygiene, safe food handling (avoid unpasteurised dairy, undercooked meat/eggs, deli meats if listeria risk), water safety, avoid construction/dust (aspergillus), gardening without gloves/masks, caution with pets (cat litter: toxoplasma; reptiles: salmonella), travel counselling, safer sex, avoid sick contacts, masks in high-risk situations, central line care.

### 9.4 Biologics and targeted therapy: specific risks

| Drug class | Examples | Key infection risks |
|---|---|---|
| Anti-TNF | infliximab, adalimumab, etanercept | TB, histoplasmosis, listeria, Legionella, HBV reactivation |
| Anti-CD20 | rituximab, ocrelizumab | HBV reactivation, PML, hypogammaglobulinaemia, poor vaccine response, severe COVID |
| JAK inhibitors | tofacitinib, baricitinib, upadacitinib | Herpes zoster, TB, VTE |
| IL-6 / IL-17 / IL-23 / IL-1 inhibitors | tocilizumab, secukinumab, ustekinumab | Bacterial infections (tocilizumab masks fever and CRP), candidiasis (IL-17) |
| Calcineurin inhibitors, mycophenolate, azathioprine | tacrolimus, ciclosporin | CMV, BK, PJP, skin cancers |
| Alemtuzumab, anti-thymocyte globulin | | Profound T-cell depletion: CMV, EBV, adenovirus |
| BTK inhibitors, venetoclax, PI3K inhibitors | ibrutinib, idelalisib | Aspergillosis, PJP, cryptococcus |
| Complement inhibitors | eculizumab | Meningococcal disease: vaccination and prophylaxis |
| Checkpoint inhibitors | pembrolizumab | Immune-related adverse events; infections from treatment of irAEs |

Hold biologics during serious infection and restart when treated, in discussion with the prescriber. **Steroids**: infection risk is dose and duration-dependent; adrenal suppression after >3 weeks; stress-dose cover.

## 10. Practical principles of empirical treatment

1. **Match empirical therapy to defect and syndrome**; broad but rational; re-evaluate in 48–72 hours with results.
2. **Use blood culture bottles properly**; ≥ 2 sets before antibiotics; line cultures; consider fungal and AFB cultures.
3. **Early imaging (CT)** and **biopsy** of lesions; tissue is the issue.
4. **Antifungal stewardship**: use biomarkers (galactomannan, β-D-glucan), CT, and risk stratification; consider interactions (azoles with tacrolimus, ciclosporin, vincristine, QT drugs).
5. **Check drug levels and interactions** (voriconazole, tacrolimus, rifampicin).
6. **Dose adjustment** for renal and hepatic function; monitor toxicity (aminoglycosides, amphotericin, vancomycin, ganciclovir).
7. **Source control**: remove infected lines, drain collections, debride necrotic tissue.
8. **Reduce immunosuppression** when safe (in discussion with specialists).
9. **Reassess goals of care** in patients with refractory disease and poor prognosis (chapter 63); avoid futile escalation but do not under-treat treatable infections.
10. **Communicate**: patient and family, and treating team.

## Worked cases

### Case 1: A chemotherapy patient with a temperature

**Presentation.** Mrs A., 52, with breast cancer, day 9 of cycle 2 of docetaxel–cyclophosphamide (no G-CSF), calls with temperature 38.6 °C and shivering. In the emergency department: HR 112, BP 108/66, RR 20, SpO₂ 97%, mild mucositis, port site non-tender. ANC 0.2 × 10⁹/L, CRP 90, lactate 1.8.

**Reasoning.** Febrile neutropenia: emergency. MASCC score: burden mild (5) + no hypotension (5) + no COPD (4) + solid tumour (4) + no dehydration (3) + age < 60 (2) + outpatient at onset (3) = 26? This is the score for a low-risk patient, but she has tachycardia, ANC <0.1 would be high-risk, and local protocols often demand IV therapy; outpatient care requires reliable access and stability.

**Action.** Blood cultures from the port and peripheral vein, urine and throat swab, and chest X-ray (normal); **start piperacillin–tazobactam IV within 60 minutes**; IV fluids; monitor; no vancomycin (no indication); G-CSF considered with oncology; admit given tachycardia and neutropenia expected to last > 5 days. Cultures: *E. coli* in blood (ESBL negative). Afebrile by 48 hours; continue antibiotics until afebrile and ANC > 0.5 or per local protocol; secondary G-CSF prophylaxis in next cycles.

**Lessons.** The first hour matters. Use risk stratification but never delay the first dose. Don't add vancomycin routinely.

### Case 2: Persistent fever and a new cough in leukaemia

**Presentation.** Mr B., 60, AML, day 14 of induction chemotherapy, febrile for 6 days on meropenem; now has pleuritic chest pain and a dry cough. ANC 0.0. Chest X-ray shows a small peripheral opacity. CT chest: multiple nodules with halo sign. Serum galactomannan index 1.8.

**Reasoning.** Persistent fever in prolonged neutropenia (> 10 days) with halo sign nodules and positive galactomannan: probable invasive pulmonary aspergillosis.

**Action.** Start voriconazole (loading 6 mg/kg q12h ×2 then 4 mg/kg q12h; check trough level; interactions) or isavuconazole; BAL when stable (PCR, galactomannan, culture); CT sinuses and brain if symptoms; continue until neutrophil recovery and radiographic improvement (at least 6–12 weeks); G-CSF; surgical consultation if nodule abuts major vessel (risk of haemoptysis). Monitor LFTs, visual hallucinations, QT.

**Lessons.** Persistent neutropenic fever > 4–7 days: think fungus; CT is far more sensitive than X-ray.

### Case 3: Breathless on exertion after a steroid course

**Presentation.** Mrs C., 68, on prednisolone 40 mg for 5 weeks for vasculitis (no PJP prophylaxis), 3 weeks of progressive dry cough and exertional breathlessness. Temperature 37.8 °C, RR 28, SpO₂ 94% at rest falling to 85% on walking, minimal crackles. LDH 640, β-D-glucan 320 pg/mL. CT: bilateral ground-glass opacities with sparing of peripheral areas.

**Reasoning.** PCP in a non-HIV immunocompromised patient (steroids + another immunosuppressant): subacute, hypoxia on exertion, LDH and β-D-glucan raised, characteristic CT.

**Action.** Admit; BAL or induced sputum for PCR (do not delay treatment); start high-dose co-trimoxazole (15 mg/kg/day TMP IV in divided doses) for 21 days with prednisolone adjunct if hypoxic (PaO₂ < 70 mmHg); monitor potassium, creatinine, rash, marrow; G6PD check; oxygen/ high-flow; after completion, secondary prophylaxis while on steroids/immunosuppression.

**Lessons.** Prophylaxis against PCP is indicated in prolonged steroid courses; the diagnosis can be made with PCR/β-D-glucan without waiting for biopsy.

### Case 4: Confusion and headache in advanced HIV

**Presentation.** Mr D., 34, newly diagnosed HIV (CD4 28 cells/µL, viral load 450,000), not on ART, 2 weeks of worsening headache, fever, vomiting, drowsiness, no neck stiffness. CT head normal. LP: opening pressure 38 cm H₂O, WBC 12/µL (lymphocytes), protein 0.7 g/L, glucose low-normal. India ink positive; serum and CSF cryptococcal antigen positive.

**Reasoning.** Cryptococcal meningitis with raised intracranial pressure (typical of advanced HIV).

**Action.** Induction: liposomal amphotericin B 3–4 mg/kg/day + flucytosine 100 mg/kg/day for 2 weeks (monitor creatinine, K, Mg, FBC); **therapeutic lumbar punctures** to reduce opening pressure to < 20 cm H₂O or by 50% (repeat daily until stable); then fluconazole 400-800 mg daily for 8 weeks, then 200 mg maintenance; **delay ART 4–6 weeks** to reduce IRIS and mortality; prophylaxis for PCP (co-trimoxazole), screen for TB; monitor for IRIS.

**Lessons.** In advanced HIV with headache, check cryptococcal antigen; measure opening pressure; manage ICP actively; time ART carefully.

### Case 5: A fatal infection after splenectomy

**Presentation.** Mr E., 41, splenectomised 8 years ago after trauma, with no prophylaxis or vaccine boosters, develops a flu-like illness: fever, headache, myalgias. Six hours later: BP 78/40, HR 140, RR 32, purpuric rash on limbs, temperature 40.2 °C, lactate 7.

**Reasoning.** Overwhelming post-splenectomy infection (OPSI) with septic shock and DIC: *S. pneumoniae* or meningococcus.

**Action.** Immediately **IV ceftriaxone 2 g** (plus vancomycin if pneumococcal resistance is suspected and meningitis is possible), cultures (do not wait), fluids 30 mL/kg, vasopressors, ICU, DIC management, steroids if refractory shock, dexamethasone considered for meningitis; notify public health if meningococcal; chemoprophylaxis for contacts. Education and vaccination for survivors; antibiotic prophylaxis.

**Lessons.** Splenectomised patients with fever need immediate antibiotics. Lifelong education and prophylaxis save lives.

### Case 6: A transplant recipient with diarrhoea

**Presentation.** Ms F., 45, kidney transplant 4 months ago on tacrolimus, mycophenolate and prednisolone, with 2 weeks of watery diarrhoea, abdominal pain, fever, and weight loss. Creatinine rising from 110 to 160 µmol/L. Tacrolimus level 18 ng/mL (high). CMV status: donor positive, recipient negative, no prophylaxis after month 3.

**Reasoning.** Diarrhoea in a transplant patient: infectious (CMV colitis, *C. difficile*, norovirus, bacterial pathogens, parasites), drug-related (mycophenolate), and can raise tacrolimus levels (leading to AKI). CMV D+/R− is high-risk.

**Action.** Stool tests (C. difficile toxin/PCR, culture, PCR panel including norovirus, parasites), blood CMV PCR (high: 85,000 IU/mL), then endoscopy with biopsies (owl's eye inclusions, immunohistochemistry); treat with IV ganciclovir (renal dose) for CMV disease, reduce mycophenolate dose, monitor tacrolimus levels, hydrate, avoid nephrotoxins, treat C. difficile if positive, monitor graft function; valganciclovir maintenance.

**Lessons.** Time since transplant, serostatus and immunosuppression determine the differential. Drug levels matter.

### Case 7: Fever on a biologic

**Presentation.** Mr G., 56, rheumatoid arthritis on adalimumab and methotrexate, 3 weeks of low-grade fever, night sweats, cough, 4 kg weight loss. No TB screening was done before starting treatment. Chest X-ray: miliary pattern. IGRA negative.

**Reasoning.** Disseminated (miliary) TB on anti-TNF therapy; IGRA can be falsely negative in immunosuppression and active disease.

**Action.** Stop adalimumab; sputum/induced sputum/bronchoscopy for AFB and PCR (GeneXpert), blood and urine cultures, HIV test, CT chest/abdomen, consider bone marrow or liver biopsy; start standard four-drug regimen (isoniazid, rifampicin, pyrazinamide, ethambutol) with pyridoxine promptly; monitor LFTs; extended duration if CNS involvement or slow response; IRIS risk when reintroducing biologics; later re-treat arthritis with non-TNF agents.

**Lessons.** Screen for latent TB before biologics; a negative IGRA does not exclude active TB in the immunosuppressed.

## Common pitfalls

1. **Delaying empirical antibiotics** in febrile neutropenia or asplenia while waiting for tests.
2. **Relying on fever, CRP, white cell count or chest X-ray** to exclude infection in steroid-treated, neutropenic or elderly patients.
3. **Ignoring non-specific symptoms** (malaise, mild confusion) as trivial.
4. **Not performing CT** early for persistent fever or respiratory symptoms.
5. **Adding vancomycin and antifungals** routinely without indications, or conversely **missing fungal infection** in prolonged neutropenia.
6. **Failing to remove an infected central line** or to drain a collection.
7. **Assuming the common cause** and ignoring opportunistic pathogens or multiple simultaneous infections.
8. **Overlooking drug interactions** (azoles with tacrolimus, rifampicin with ART).
9. **Starting ART too early in cryptococcal meningitis** or stopping steroids abruptly (adrenal crisis).
10. **Giving live vaccines** to immunosuppressed patients, or omitting inactivated vaccines before immunosuppression.
11. **Not screening for HBV, TB and strongyloides** before steroids, biologics and chemotherapy.
12. **Forgetting adrenal insufficiency** in patients on or recently on steroids.
13. **Missing non-infectious mimics** (drug fever, GVHD, CRS, IRIS, ICI toxicity, thrombosis).
14. **Digital rectal examination** or rectal temperatures in neutropenic patients.
15. **Poor communication about warning signs**, travel, and exposures; no stand-by antibiotics for asplenic patients.

## Key points

- Identify the immune defect (neutrophil, T-cell, B-cell, complement, asplenia, barrier) to predict pathogens.
- Immunocompromised patients have subtle signs: investigate early, start empirical therapy quickly, and use CT, biomarkers and tissue.
- Febrile neutropenia: antipseudomonal beta-lactam within 60 minutes, cultures, risk stratification, review at 48–72 h, antifungal work-up if fever persists > 4–7 days.
- PCP, aspergillosis, cryptococcus, CMV, TB and toxoplasma are the pathogens to remember; prophylaxis and screening prevent many.
- HIV: test widely, start ART early, time it carefully in cryptococcal and TB meningitis, watch for IRIS.
- Asplenic patients need vaccines, prophylaxis, stand-by antibiotics and immediate treatment of fever.
- Non-infectious mimics (GVHD, rejection, CRS, ICI toxicity, drug fever) are common; involve specialists early.
- Prevention: screen for HBV/TB/HIV/strongyloides, vaccinate (non-live) before immunosuppression, avoid live vaccines, counsel on exposures.

**Questions**

1. Define febrile neutropenia and state the time target for antibiotics.
2. Which immune defect is associated with recurrent encapsulated bacterial infections? Which with *Pneumocystis* and cryptococcus?
3. A patient on chemotherapy has persistent fever for 6 days despite meropenem. What do you do?
4. Describe the typical CT and laboratory findings of PCP and the first-line treatment.
5. When should ART be started in cryptococcal meningitis and why?
6. List three screening tests to perform before starting rituximab, anti-TNF or high-dose steroids.
7. What is the risk and management plan for a splenectomised patient who develops fever?
8. Name four non-infectious causes of fever in transplant or haematology patients.
9. Which vaccines are contraindicated in severe immunosuppression, and which are strongly recommended?
10. A transplant patient has tacrolimus toxicity and diarrhoea. What infections and drug interactions should you consider?

**Answers**

1. Single oral temperature ≥ 38.3 °C (or ≥ 38.0 °C sustained for ≥ 1 hour) with ANC < 0.5 × 10⁹/L (or expected to fall < 0.5 within 48 h). Give empirical IV antibiotics within 60 minutes of presentation.
2. Antibody (B-cell) deficiency, complement deficiency and asplenia; T-cell (cell-mediated) deficiency, e.g., advanced HIV, steroids, transplant.
3. Evaluate for invasive fungal infection: CT chest (±sinus/abdomen), serum galactomannan and β-D-glucan, review line and abscess, repeat cultures; start empirical or pre-emptive antifungal (liposomal amphotericin B, echinocandin or mould-active azole depending on prophylaxis); check drug fever and other causes; neutrophil recovery is key.
4. CT: bilateral ground-glass opacities (often perihilar, with subpleural sparing), cysts, pneumothorax; high LDH; high β-D-glucan; PCR on BAL/induced sputum. First-line: high-dose co-trimoxazole for 21 days plus corticosteroids for significant hypoxia.
5. Delay ART for about 4–6 weeks after starting antifungal therapy, because early ART increases mortality and IRIS in cryptococcal meningitis.
6. HBV serology (HBsAg, anti-HBc, anti-HBs), TB screening (IGRA/TST ± chest X-ray), HIV test; also HCV, VZV/CMV serology, strongyloides serology in at-risk patients, vaccination review.
7. Asplenia → risk of OPSI (encapsulated organisms); treat any fever as an emergency: immediate IV ceftriaxone, cultures, resuscitation; prevention: vaccines, prophylaxis, stand-by antibiotics, education.
8. Drug fever, acute GVHD, graft rejection, PTLD or malignancy, thrombosis, adrenal insufficiency, transfusion reaction, CRS, IRIS, pancreatitis, tumour fever, HLH (any four).
9. Contraindicated: live vaccines (MMR, varicella, yellow fever, live influenza, BCG, oral typhoid). Recommended: inactivated influenza, COVID-19, pneumococcal conjugate/polysaccharide, recombinant zoster, hepatitis B, HPV, meningococcal, Tdap, RSV.
10. Tacrolimus toxicity and diarrhoea: C. difficile, CMV, norovirus, adenovirus, bacterial pathogens, parasites; drug causes: mycophenolate; interactions: macrolides, azole antifungals, diltiazem, grapefruit raise tacrolimus; rifampicin and St John's wort lower it; dehydration/AKI raises levels.
