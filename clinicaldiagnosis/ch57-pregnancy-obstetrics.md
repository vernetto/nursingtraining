# Chapter 57. Pregnancy and Obstetrics

## Learning objectives

After studying this chapter you should be able to:

1. Take an obstetric history, calculate gestational age and expected date of delivery, and perform and interpret a basic antenatal examination.
2. Distinguish normal physiological changes of pregnancy from pathology, and interpret vital signs and laboratory values in pregnancy.
3. Assess early-pregnancy bleeding and pain, and diagnose ectopic pregnancy, miscarriage and molar pregnancy.
4. Recognise hypertensive disorders of pregnancy, diagnose pre-eclampsia and its severe features, and manage eclampsia and HELLP.
5. Assess bleeding in later pregnancy (placenta praevia, abruption, vasa praevia, uterine rupture) and postpartum haemorrhage.
6. Screen for and manage gestational diabetes, anaemia, infections, and venous thromboembolism in pregnancy.
7. Assess reduced fetal movements, preterm labour, rupture of membranes, and labour progress.
8. Recognise the sick pregnant or postpartum woman, including sepsis, amniotic fluid embolism, peripartum cardiomyopathy, and postpartum psychosis.
9. Apply principles of medication and imaging safety in pregnancy and breastfeeding.

> **Note on thresholds and doses.** Thresholds follow widely used guidelines (WHO, ACOG, RCOG, NICE, ISSHP, FIGO) at the time of writing; they differ between countries. Local protocols override figures printed here. Always verify drug safety in pregnancy in your national formulary or a reproductive toxicology service.

## 1. Obstetric history and examination

### 1.1 The obstetric history

Every woman of reproductive age with abdominal pain, collapse, vomiting, bleeding or syncope is pregnant until a test says otherwise.

- **Gravidity and parity**: Gravida (G) = number of pregnancies including the present; Para (P) = number of births ≥ 20–24 weeks (local definition). Common notation: G3 P2+0 (two births, no losses) or **GTPAL** (term, preterm, abortions/miscarriages, living). Always specify.
- **Last menstrual period (LMP)** and its certainty, cycle length and regularity, contraception use before conception, and conception method.
- **Gestational age**: from LMP, adjusted by dating ultrasound. **Expected date of delivery (EDD)** by Naegele rule: LMP + 1 year − 3 months + 7 days (assumes a 28-day cycle; adjust for longer or shorter cycles). First-trimester crown–rump length (CRL, 9–13+6 weeks) is the most accurate dating tool (± 5–7 days); redate if the discrepancy with LMP is > 5–7 days at 9–13 weeks, or > 7–10 days at 14–15+6 weeks, and > 10–14 days later.
- **Previous pregnancies**: mode of delivery, gestation, birthweight, complications (pre-eclampsia, gestational diabetes, preterm birth, stillbirth, postpartum haemorrhage, shoulder dystocia, third- or fourth-degree tears, caesarean indication, uterine scar type), and neonatal outcome.
- **Current pregnancy**: symptoms, scans, screening results, blood group and antibody status, infection serology, medications, supplements, exposures.
- **Medical, surgical, family, drug, allergy, social history**, vaccination, smoking, alcohol, substances, domestic abuse (screen privately), mental health, folic acid, diet, occupation.

### 1.2 Antenatal examination

- General: BP (appropriate cuff, seated, left arm at heart level), weight and BMI, urinalysis (protein, glucose, nitrites), oedema (mild ankle oedema is common; facial or hand oedema, rapid weight gain is concerning), reflexes, clonus.
- **Abdominal examination** (after 20–24 weeks): inspect (scars, linea nigra, striae, fetal movements), **symphysis–fundal height** (cm ≈ weeks ± 2 between 24 and 36 weeks; plot on a customised chart), palpation: lie (longitudinal/transverse/oblique), presentation (cephalic/breech), engagement (fifths palpable), fetal heart (Doppler from ≈ 10–12 weeks; Pinard from ≈ 28 weeks; normal 110–160 bpm), amniotic fluid volume.
- **Leopold manoeuvres**: fundal grip (what lies in the fundus), lateral grip (back vs limbs), pelvic grip (presenting part), deep pelvic grip (engagement and attitude).
- **Speculum and digital vaginal examination** only when indicated; **never perform vaginal examination in a woman with antepartum haemorrhage until placenta praevia is excluded by ultrasound**.

### 1.3 Routine antenatal care: schedule at a glance

| Gestation | Key actions |
|---|---|
| Booking (< 10–12 weeks) | History, BP, BMI, blood group and antibodies, FBC/ferritin, haemoglobinopathy screen, HIV, hepatitis B, syphilis, rubella/varicella immunity (per country), urine culture (asymptomatic bacteriuria), TSH if indicated, early glucose screen if risk factors, folic acid, vitamin D, aspirin plan |
| 11–14 weeks | Dating and nuchal translucency scan; combined first-trimester screening or cfDNA (NIPT); pre-eclampsia risk assessment and aspirin |
| 18–22 weeks | Anomaly scan, placental site |
| 24–28 weeks | Gestational diabetes screening; FBC; anti-D prophylaxis if RhD negative at 28 weeks (per local protocol); Tdap (27–36 weeks) |
| 28–36 weeks | Symphysis–fundal height, BP, urine, fetal movements counselling, influenza and COVID-19 vaccines, RSV vaccine (32–36 weeks, seasonal) |
| 36 weeks onward | Presentation check; birth plan; group B streptococcus (GBS) screening (35–37 weeks in countries using screening) |
| 41 weeks | Offer induction (per local policy) |

## 2. Physiological changes: the "normal" that looks abnormal

| System | Change | Diagnostic implication |
|---|---|---|
| Cardiovascular | Blood volume ↑ 40–50%; cardiac output ↑ 30–50%; heart rate ↑ 10–20 bpm; BP ↓ in second trimester (diastolic −10 mmHg), returning toward baseline by term; systemic vascular resistance ↓ | Resting HR of 100 may be normal late in pregnancy; **hypotension is a late sign of hypovolaemia** (a pregnant woman can lose 1,000–1,500 mL before BP falls). Flow murmurs (grade 1–2 systolic) and a third heart sound are common. Supine hypotension from caval compression: lie in left lateral or tilt the pelvis |
| Respiratory | Minute ventilation ↑ 40% (tidal volume), respiratory rate unchanged (12–20), PaCO₂ ↓ to 4.0 kPa (30 mmHg), mild respiratory alkalosis (pH 7.40–7.45, bicarbonate 18–22 mmol/L); functional residual capacity ↓ | Physiological dyspnoea is common (up to 70%). PaCO₂ of 5.0 kPa (38 mmHg) which would be normal in a non-pregnant woman signals impending respiratory failure. Rapid desaturation on intubation |
| Haematological | Plasma volume ↑ more than red cell mass → **dilutional anaemia**; Hb normal lower limit 110 g/L (first and third trimester) and 105 g/L (second trimester); WCC ↑ (up to 15 × 10⁹/L; 25–30 in labour); platelets slightly ↓ (gestational thrombocytopenia, platelet counts 100–150 usually benign); fibrinogen ↑ (4–6 g/L), D-dimer ↑ | Interpret FBC with pregnancy-specific ranges. Normal-range fibrinogen in PPH is a warning (<2 g/L predicts severe haemorrhage). D-dimer is less useful for VTE exclusion without pregnancy-adapted algorithms |
| Renal | GFR ↑ 50%; creatinine ↓ (40–70 µmol/L); urea ↓; mild hydronephrosis (right > left); glycosuria common | **Creatinine of 80 µmol/L may signal renal impairment.** Dilated collecting system should not be mistaken for obstruction. |
| Endocrine | hCG peaks at 8–11 weeks; TSH ↓ in first trimester (reference ranges lower); total T4 ↑; cortisol ↑; insulin resistance ↑ | Use trimester-specific TSH ranges |
| Gastrointestinal | Nausea, reflux, constipation; ALP ↑ (placental) up to 2–4 × normal; albumin ↓ | Raised ALP alone is not liver disease |
| Skeletal and musculoskeletal | Ligamentous laxity, lordosis, symphysis pubis dysfunction, carpal tunnel | Common cause of back and pelvic pain |
| Skin | Linea nigra, chloasma, spider naevi, palmar erythema, striae | Palmar erythema and spiders are not signs of liver disease in pregnancy |
| Immune | Relative immunosuppression: more severe influenza, varicella, malaria, listeriosis, hepatitis E | Lower threshold to treat |

## 3. Early pregnancy: bleeding, pain and vomiting

### 3.1 Initial assessment

In any woman of reproductive age with bleeding or abdominal pain: **pregnancy test** (urine hCG detects > 25 mIU/mL; positive about 10–14 days post-conception), haemodynamic assessment, abdominal and, if indicated, speculum and bimanual examination, blood group and Rh status, quantitative serum hCG, and **transvaginal ultrasound**.

**Discriminatory zone**: when the hCG level is above about 1,500–3,500 mIU/L a gestational sac should be visible transvaginally; a well-defined intrauterine sac with yolk sac confirms an intrauterine pregnancy. If hCG is above the zone and no intrauterine pregnancy is seen, ectopic pregnancy or miscarriage is likely. A normally rising hCG in early viable pregnancy increases by at least 49–63% in 48 h (starting values dependent); a rise < 35% in 48 h suggests abnormal pregnancy. Do not delay ultrasound for hCG if the woman is unstable.

### 3.2 Ectopic pregnancy

Implantation outside the uterine cavity (97% tubal), 1–2% of pregnancies, a leading cause of first-trimester maternal death.

- **Risk factors**: previous ectopic, tubal surgery or sterilisation, pelvic inflammatory disease (chlamydia), IUD in situ (although the absolute risk with IUD is low, a pregnancy that does occur is more likely to be ectopic), assisted reproduction, smoking, endometriosis, age > 35. Half of women have no risk factors.
- **Presentation**: amenorrhoea (6–8 weeks), unilateral lower abdominal pain, vaginal bleeding (often light and dark), shoulder-tip pain (diaphragmatic irritation), syncope, dizziness, tenderness on cervical motion, adnexal mass or tenderness; collapse with a rigid abdomen in rupture.
- **Investigations**: serum hCG; transvaginal ultrasound (empty uterus, adnexal mass, "ring of fire", fluid in pouch of Douglas or Morrison pouch); **FAST** in unstable patients.
- **Management**: unstable → resuscitate (two large-bore IV lines, crossmatch, activate massive haemorrhage protocol), emergency laparoscopy or laparotomy; **stable** → expectant management (falling hCG, low level, asymptomatic), **methotrexate** (single dose 50 mg/m² IM if hCG < 5,000 mIU/mL, no fetal cardiac activity, normal renal and liver function, reliable follow-up; avoid pregnancy for 3 months afterwards), or laparoscopic salpingectomy/salpingostomy. Anti-D immunoglobulin in RhD-negative women (per local protocol). Follow hCG to zero.
- **Pregnancy of unknown location (PUL)**: positive pregnancy test without visible intra- or extrauterine pregnancy. Needs close follow-up with serial hCG at 48 h and safety-net advice; 10–20% will be ectopic.
- **Heterotopic pregnancy**: simultaneous intra- and extrauterine; rare (1 in 30,000 spontaneous; 1 in 100 after IVF).

**Never exclude ectopic pregnancy by absence of risk factors, normal vital signs, or a normal bimanual examination.**

### 3.3 Miscarriage

Loss before viability (< 20–24 weeks), affecting 15–20% of recognised pregnancies, most due to chromosomal abnormalities.

| Type | Features |
|---|---|
| Threatened | Bleeding, closed os, viable pregnancy on scan |
| Inevitable | Bleeding with open os, products not yet passed |
| Incomplete | Open os, some products passed, retained tissue |
| Complete | Products passed, uterus empty, os closed |
| Missed (early fetal demise) | CRL ≥ 7 mm with no cardiac activity, or mean sac diameter ≥ 25 mm with no embryo |
| Septic | Fever, foul discharge, tender uterus, sepsis |
| Recurrent | ≥ 3 consecutive losses (evaluate for antiphospholipid syndrome, parental karyotype, uterine anomalies, thyroid disease, diabetes) |

Management options for confirmed early pregnancy loss: expectant (up to 2 weeks), medical (misoprostol, with or without mifepristone), or surgical (suction aspiration, manual vacuum aspiration). All appropriate; offer choice. Anti-D when indicated (≥ 12 weeks or surgical or medical management in Rh-negative women per guideline), analgesia, antiemetics, emotional support, contraception advice, and information about when to return (heavy bleeding > 2 pads/hour, fever, severe pain, foul discharge). **Septic miscarriage** needs urgent IV antibiotics, evacuation of retained products, and resuscitation.

### 3.4 Molar pregnancy and gestational trophoblastic disease

Hydatidiform mole (complete or partial): bleeding, exaggerated pregnancy symptoms, hyperemesis, early-onset pre-eclampsia (before 20 weeks), large-for-dates uterus, hyperthyroidism, very high hCG (> 100,000 mIU/mL), "snowstorm" appearance on ultrasound. Treatment: suction evacuation, histology, and hCG follow-up (registration with a trophoblastic disease centre); 15–20% (complete mole) may become invasive and need chemotherapy (methotrexate or actinomycin D). Avoid pregnancy until hCG normalises and has been monitored.

### 3.5 Nausea and vomiting of pregnancy; hyperemesis gravidarum

Nausea and vomiting affect up to 70–80% of pregnant women, peaking at 9–10 weeks. **Hyperemesis gravidarum**: persistent vomiting with > 5% weight loss, dehydration and electrolyte disturbance, ketonuria (affects 0.3–3%).

- Assess hydration, weight, ketones, electrolytes (hypokalaemia, hyponatraemia), liver function (mild abnormalities), TSH (gestational transient thyrotoxicosis).
- Exclude other causes: UTI, gastroenteritis, molar pregnancy, multiple pregnancy, thyrotoxicosis, pancreatitis, appendicitis, migraine, gastroparesis.
- Treatment: small frequent meals, ginger, vitamin B6 (pyridoxine) ± doxylamine, antiemetics (metoclopramide, ondansetron, prochlorperazine, promethazine), IV fluids (normal saline with potassium; avoid dextrose-only fluids before thiamine), **thiamine 100 mg daily/IV before glucose** to prevent Wernicke encephalopathy, thromboprophylaxis for inpatients, corticosteroids for refractory cases. Admit if unable to tolerate oral intake or ketonuria with weight loss.

## 4. Hypertensive disorders of pregnancy

Hypertension complicates 5–10% of pregnancies and is a leading cause of maternal and perinatal morbidity and mortality.

### 4.1 Definitions

Hypertension: BP ≥ 140/90 mmHg on two occasions at least 4 hours apart (or one reading ≥ 160/110 mmHg, which is severe). Measure correctly (rested, seated, appropriate cuff size, same arm); avoid relying on single readings; use home BP monitoring when appropriate.

| Disorder | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Chronic hypertension** | Present before pregnancy or before 20 weeks |
| **Gestational hypertension** | New-onset hypertension at ≥ 20 weeks without proteinuria or other features, resolves by 12 weeks postpartum |
| **Pre-eclampsia** | New-onset hypertension ≥ 20 weeks **plus** proteinuria (urine protein:creatinine ratio ≥ 30 mg/mmol (0.3 mg/mg); 24-h protein ≥ 300 mg), **or** in the absence of proteinuria, evidence of end-organ dysfunction (see below) |
| **Chronic hypertension with superimposed pre-eclampsia** | Chronic hypertension plus new proteinuria or organ dysfunction, or sudden worsening hypertension |
| **Eclampsia** | New seizures in a woman with pre-eclampsia, without another cause |
| **HELLP syndrome** | Haemolysis, Elevated Liver enzymes, Low Platelets |

**Pre-eclampsia with severe features (ACOG)**: any of: BP ≥ 160/110; platelets < 100 × 10⁹/L; liver transaminases ≥ 2 × upper limit of normal or severe persistent right upper quadrant/epigastric pain; creatinine > 97 µmol/L (1.1 mg/dL) or doubling; pulmonary oedema; new-onset headache unresponsive to medication; visual disturbances; (and in ISSHP also uteroplacental dysfunction such as fetal growth restriction, abnormal Doppler, stillbirth).

### 4.2 Clinical picture and examination

Many women are asymptomatic. Ask about severe headache, visual disturbance (scotomata, flashing lights), epigastric or right upper quadrant pain, nausea or vomiting, sudden oedema (face, hands), breathlessness, reduced fetal movements. Examine BP, reflexes (hyperreflexia, clonus), epigastric tenderness, oedema, chest (crepitations), fundal height (growth restriction), fetal heart.

### 4.3 Investigations

FBC (platelets, haemolysis), U&E (creatinine, urate), LFTs (AST, ALT, LDH, bilirubin), urine protein:creatinine ratio, coagulation if platelets are low or abruption suspected, blood film if haemolysis suspected; fetal assessment: ultrasound for growth, amniotic fluid, umbilical artery Doppler, cardiotocography (CTG). **sFlt-1/PlGF ratio** (where available) helps rule out pre-eclampsia in 24–36+6 weeks (ratio ≤ 38 has a high negative predictive value for one week).

### 4.4 Prevention

Offer **low-dose aspirin 75–150 mg nightly** (ideally 150 mg in many guidelines) from 12 weeks (before 16 weeks) until 36 weeks to women at high risk (previous pre-eclampsia, chronic hypertension, pre-existing diabetes, autoimmune disease, renal disease, multiple pregnancy) or ≥ 2 moderate risk factors (first pregnancy, age ≥ 40, BMI ≥ 35, interpregnancy interval > 10 years, family history, multiple pregnancy). Calcium supplementation (1.5–2 g/day) in populations with low dietary calcium.

### 4.5 Management

- **Antihypertensive treatment** for BP ≥ 150/100 (target 130–140/80–90): **labetalol** (first-line oral, 100–400 mg two to three times daily; avoid in asthma), **nifedipine** modified-release, **methyldopa**. Avoid ACE inhibitors, ARBs, spironolactone, and atenolol (fetal harm). **Severe hypertension (≥ 160/110) is an emergency**: IV labetalol (20 mg, doubling every 10 min to 80 mg boluses; max 300 mg), oral nifedipine immediate release (10 mg), or IV hydralazine; target a reduction to 140–150/90–100 mmHg over 30–60 minutes; aim to prevent stroke (the main cause of death).
- **Magnesium sulphate** for seizure prophylaxis in severe pre-eclampsia and treatment of eclampsia: loading dose 4–6 g IV over 15–20 min, maintenance 1–2 g/h IV (or IM regimens); continue 24 h after delivery or the last seizure. Monitor respiratory rate (> 12), tendon reflexes (loss of patellar reflex is an early sign of toxicity), urine output (> 25–30 mL/h), and serum magnesium if renal impairment. **Antidote: calcium gluconate 1 g (10 mL of 10%) IV over 10 min.** Magnesium is not an antihypertensive.
- **Fluids**: restrict (80 mL/h total) in severe pre-eclampsia, because of pulmonary oedema risk.
- **Corticosteroids** (betamethasone 12 mg IM × 2, 24 h apart) for fetal lung maturity < 34–35 weeks if delivery is anticipated within 7 days.
- **Delivery is the only cure.** Timing: pre-eclampsia with severe features at ≥ 34 weeks → deliver after stabilisation; < 34 weeks → expectant management in a tertiary centre only if stable and with intensive monitoring; deliver immediately for uncontrolled hypertension, eclampsia, pulmonary oedema, abruption, HELLP, renal failure, non-reassuring fetal status. Mild pre-eclampsia/gestational hypertension: induction at 37 weeks. Vaginal birth is preferred unless obstetric indications.
- **Eclampsia**: ABC, left lateral position, protect from injury, magnesium sulphate 4–6 g IV load then infusion (a recurrent seizure: further 2 g bolus), control BP, plan delivery once stable, avoid diazepam if magnesium is available, find other causes if atypical (focal signs, prolonged unconsciousness, onset > 48 h post-partum: CT/MRI for stroke, PRES, venous sinus thrombosis).
- **HELLP**: epigastric pain, malaise, nausea, jaundice; labs: haemolysis (↑ LDH > 600, schistocytes), ↑ AST/ALT, platelets < 100; treat as severe pre-eclampsia, deliver, monitor for hepatic haematoma/rupture, DIC, AKI. Platelet transfusion if < 20–50 before delivery/surgery. Differentiate from TTP/HUS, acute fatty liver of pregnancy, antiphospholipid syndrome, SLE, sepsis.
- **Postpartum**: BP peaks days 3–6; continue monitoring for at least 72 h; eclampsia can occur postpartum; treat BP ≥ 150/100; enalapril and nifedipine are compatible with breastfeeding. Long-term cardiovascular risk is doubled: annual BP, lipid and glucose screening, lifestyle advice.

## 5. Bleeding and complications in later pregnancy

### 5.1 Antepartum haemorrhage (APH)

Vaginal bleeding after 20–24 weeks. Causes: placenta praevia, placental abruption, vasa praevia, uterine rupture, local causes (cervical ectropion, polyps, cancer, vaginal trauma, infection), "show". Assume major until proven minor.

**Approach**:

1. **Resuscitate**: ABC, left lateral tilt, large-bore IV, bloods (FBC, coagulation, fibrinogen, crossmatch, Kleihauer–Betke), blood group and Rh, O₂, fluids/blood products.
2. Estimate blood loss (underestimated), BP and heart rate (late signs), fetal CTG, abdominal palpation (tense, tender "woody" uterus in abruption; soft non-tender uterus in praevia).
3. **Do not do digital vaginal examination** until praevia is excluded. Ultrasound for placental site.
4. Deliver for maternal compromise or fetal distress; Anti-D immunoglobulin in RhD-negative women.

| | Placenta praevia | Placental abruption |
|---|---|---|
| Pain | Painless | Painful, constant |
| Bleeding | Bright red, may be recurrent ("warning bleed") | Dark, may be concealed; amount may not reflect loss |
| Uterus | Soft, non-tender; malpresentation common | Tense, tender, "woody"; fetal parts difficult to feel |
| Fetal condition | Usually normal until severe | Fetal distress/death, DIC |
| Risk factors | Previous caesarean, prior praevia, multiple pregnancy, smoking, age, IVF | Hypertension/pre-eclampsia, trauma (including domestic violence), cocaine, smoking, prior abruption, PPROM, thrombophilia |
| Diagnosis | Ultrasound (transabdominal and transvaginal) | Clinical; ultrasound often normal |
| Management | Admit, steroids if < 34 weeks, caesarean at 36–37+6 weeks (praevia), earlier if bleeding; consider placenta accreta spectrum (suspect with anterior praevia on a caesarean scar) | Resuscitate, early delivery (vaginal if mother and fetus stable, caesarean if fetal compromise), manage DIC, prepare for PPH |

**Vasa praevia**: fetal vessels cross the membranes over the cervix; bleeding at rupture of membranes with acute fetal distress/sinusoidal CTG; very high fetal mortality; emergency caesarean. **Uterine rupture**: previous caesarean or uterine surgery, sudden severe pain, abnormal CTG (late decelerations, bradycardia), loss of station, vaginal bleeding, haemodynamic instability; emergency laparotomy. **Amniotic fluid embolism**: sudden collapse, hypoxia, hypotension, DIC, seizures during labour or soon after birth.

### 5.2 Postpartum haemorrhage (PPH)

Blood loss ≥ 500 mL after vaginal birth or ≥ 1,000 mL after caesarean (or any loss causing haemodynamic instability); **primary** within 24 h, **secondary** 24 h–12 weeks. Leading cause of maternal death worldwide. Visual estimates underestimate by 30–50%; weigh swabs and use a calibrated drape. Women tolerate loss until suddenly decompensating.

**The four Ts**: **T**one (atony, 70%), **T**rauma (tears, haematoma, rupture, inversion), **T**issue (retained placenta/products, accreta), **T**hrombin (coagulopathy, DIC, anticoagulants, abruption).

**Prevention**: active management of the third stage (oxytocin 10 IU IM, controlled cord traction, uterine massage), risk assessment, tranexamic acid.

**Management** (call for help, ABC, bimanual compression and uterine massage, empty bladder):
- **Uterotonics**: oxytocin infusion; **carboprost** (15-methyl PGF2α, 250 µg IM every 15 min, max 8; avoid in asthma); **ergometrine** (0.25 mg IM/slow IV; avoid in hypertension); misoprostol 800 µg sublingual/rectal.
- **Tranexamic acid 1 g IV within 3 hours of birth** (repeat once after 30 min if bleeding continues).
- Fluids and blood: balanced resuscitation, massive transfusion protocol (RBC:FFP:platelets 1:1:1), cryoprecipitate/fibrinogen concentrate when fibrinogen < 2 g/L, calcium, keep warm, correct acidosis.
- **Identify and treat the cause**: examination under anaesthesia; repair tears; remove retained tissue; manage inverted uterus.
- **Mechanical/surgical**: intrauterine balloon tamponade (Bakri), uterine compression sutures (B-Lynch), uterine artery ligation or embolisation, hysterectomy as life-saving last resort.
- **Secondary PPH**: infection (endometritis), retained products; ultrasound; antibiotics; evacuation.

### 5.3 Anaemia in pregnancy

WHO defines anaemia as Hb < 110 g/L (1st and 3rd trimesters), < 105 g/L (2nd). Iron deficiency is the commonest cause (approximately 50%); others: folate/B12 deficiency, haemoglobinopathies (thalassaemia, sickle cell), malaria, hookworm, chronic disease, bleeding. Screen at booking and 28 weeks. Treatment: oral iron (ferrous sulfate 65 mg elemental once daily or alternate days; improves absorption and tolerability), IV iron (ferric carboxymaltose, ferric derisomaltose) in the second/third trimesters when oral fails or intolerance, severe anaemia or late presentation; blood transfusion for Hb < 70 g/L with symptoms or near term. Folic acid 400 µg daily preconception and through 12 weeks (5 mg if high risk: previous NTD, diabetes, epilepsy treatment, BMI > 30).

### 5.4 Reduced fetal movements (RFM)

Maternal perception of reduced fetal movements after 24–28 weeks is associated with stillbirth, growth restriction and placental dysfunction. Do not rely on "kick counts"; advise women to report any change. Assess: history (pattern, duration), risk factors, maternal observations, BP, urinalysis, symphysis-fundal height, fetal heart rate by Doppler, CTG for 20–30 minutes (≥ 28 weeks), ultrasound for growth, amniotic fluid and Dopplers if indicated or persistent RFM. Never reassure without objective assessment. Persistent or recurrent RFM warrants repeated assessment and consideration of delivery.

## 6. Medical disorders and infections in pregnancy

### 6.1 Gestational and pre-existing diabetes

- **Pre-gestational diabetes**: preconception counselling (HbA1c < 48–53 mmol/mol (6.5–7%), folic acid 5 mg, stop teratogenic drugs (ACE inhibitors, statins), retinal and renal assessment); target fasting glucose < 5.3 mmol/L (95 mg/dL), 1-hour postprandial < 7.8 mmol/L, 2-hour < 6.7 mmol/L; insulin (metformin and glibenclamide adjuncts in some settings); growth scans; delivery timing 37–39 weeks.
- **Gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM)**: glucose intolerance first recognised in pregnancy, usually from 24–28 weeks. **Screening/diagnosis**: 75 g OGTT at 24–28 weeks (IADPSG/WHO: fasting ≥ 5.1 mmol/L, 1 h ≥ 10.0, 2 h ≥ 8.5 mmol/L; any one value) or two-step approach (50 g challenge ≥ 7.8–8.6 mmol/L then 100 g OGTT, in the USA); earlier testing if risk factors (previous GDM, BMI > 30, family history, ethnicity, PCOS, previous macrosomia). Treatment: diet, exercise, self-monitoring of blood glucose, metformin or insulin if targets not met in 1–2 weeks. Complications: macrosomia, shoulder dystocia, neonatal hypoglycaemia, pre-eclampsia, stillbirth. Postpartum OGTT (6–13 weeks) and lifelong screening (50% develop type 2 diabetes within 10–20 years).

### 6.2 Thyroid disease

Hypothyroidism: increase levothyroxine by 25–30% once pregnancy is confirmed; TSH target below 2.5 mIU/L (trimester-specific). Hyperthyroidism: propylthiouracil in the first trimester (carbimazole/methimazole teratogenic in the first trimester), then switch. Distinguish gestational transient thyrotoxicosis (hCG-mediated, with hyperemesis; no antibodies) from Graves disease.

### 6.3 Venous thromboembolism (VTE)

Pregnancy and the puerperium increase VTE risk 4–5-fold; the leading direct cause of maternal death in many high-income countries. Risk factors: age > 35, obesity, immobility, caesarean birth, previous VTE, thrombophilia, multiple pregnancy, pre-eclampsia, smoking, sepsis, hyperemesis, dehydration, long travel.

- **Presentation**: left-sided leg swelling (80–90% of DVT are left-sided and proximal, iliofemoral), calf pain, lower abdominal pain, dyspnoea, pleuritic chest pain, tachycardia, hypoxia, haemoptysis, collapse.
- **Diagnosis**: compression duplex ultrasound for suspected DVT; if non-diagnostic and iliac vein thrombosis is suspected, MR venography. For suspected PE: chest X-ray, ECG, ABG; **CTPA or V/Q scan** (V/Q has lower breast dose; both have low fetal doses; do not withhold imaging because of pregnancy). Pregnancy-adapted algorithms (**YEARS** with D-dimer or the pregnancy-adapted Geneva score) can safely avoid imaging in a proportion. Standard Wells score is not validated in pregnancy.
- **Treatment**: **low-molecular-weight heparin** (weight-based enoxaparin 1 mg/kg twice daily or 1.5 mg/kg daily) throughout pregnancy and at least 6 weeks postpartum (minimum 3 months in total); warfarin is teratogenic (6–12 weeks) and avoided; **DOACs are not recommended** in pregnancy or breastfeeding; unfractionated heparin for massive PE; thrombolysis or embolectomy for life-threatening PE. Plan delivery and neuraxial anaesthesia timing around LMWH dosing (24 h after therapeutic dose).
- **Prophylaxis**: risk assessment at booking and after delivery; LMWH prophylaxis for high-risk women.

### 6.4 Infections

- **Urinary tract infection**: screen for asymptomatic bacteriuria at booking and treat (increases pyelonephritis, preterm birth); pyelonephritis is a leading non-obstetric cause of sepsis, treat with IV antibiotics (ceftriaxone), admit.
- **Group B streptococcus**: colonises 20–30% of women; causes early-onset neonatal sepsis; intrapartum IV penicillin (benzylpenicillin 3 g then 1.5 g every 4 h) for GBS carriers, previous GBS-infected baby, GBS bacteriuria, preterm labour, or maternal fever (≥ 38 °C).
- **Syphilis, HIV, hepatitis B**: screen at booking; antiretroviral therapy, hepatitis B immunoglobulin and vaccine to the newborn, penicillin for syphilis (the only recommended treatment in pregnancy; desensitise if allergic).
- **Rubella** (congenital rubella syndrome), **CMV**, **toxoplasma**, **parvovirus B19**, **varicella** (varicella immunoglobulin within 10 days of exposure for non-immune women; aciclovir for chickenpox rash within 24 h if > 20 weeks), **Zika**, **listeria** (soft cheeses, deli meats; fever in pregnancy with flu-like symptoms: blood cultures; ampicillin).
- **Influenza** (severe in pregnancy: oseltamivir early and immunisation), **COVID-19**, **malaria** (severe in pregnancy; artesunate in all trimesters for severe malaria; prevention with IPTp-SP in endemic areas), **hepatitis E** (fulminant hepatic failure in third trimester), **herpes simplex** (caesarean if primary genital lesions at labour; suppressive aciclovir from 36 weeks), **chorioamnionitis** (fever, uterine tenderness, fetal tachycardia, maternal tachycardia, foul liquor: IV antibiotics (ampicillin plus gentamicin) and delivery).
- **Vaginal infections**: bacterial vaginosis, candidiasis (topical azoles only in pregnancy), trichomonas, chlamydia, gonorrhoea (screening and treatment).

### 6.5 Liver disease specific to pregnancy

| Condition | Timing | Features | Management |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Intrahepatic cholestasis of pregnancy (ICP)** | > 24 weeks (often third trimester) | Intense itch, especially palms and soles, worse at night, no rash; total bile acids ≥ 10 µmol/L, raised ALT/AST | Ursodeoxycholic acid; weekly LFT and bile acids; delivery at 35–38 weeks depending on bile acid level (≥ 100 µmol/L: higher stillbirth risk, earlier delivery); vitamin K if prolonged PT |
| **HELLP / pre-eclampsia** | Third trimester | See 4.5 | Delivery |
| **Acute fatty liver of pregnancy (AFLP)** | Third trimester | Nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, jaundice, hypoglycaemia, coagulopathy, raised ammonia, AKI, encephalopathy; Swansea criteria | Emergency; stabilise and deliver, ICU |
| **Hyperemesis-related liver dysfunction** | First trimester | Mild transaminase rise | Supportive |

### 6.6 Other conditions

- **Epilepsy**: preconception counselling; folic acid 5 mg; lamotrigine or levetiracetam preferred; **avoid valproate** (teratogenic; neurodevelopmental effects); continue treatment (seizures are riskier than most drugs).
- **Cardiac disease**: peripartum cardiomyopathy (see 8.2), valvular disease (mitral stenosis worsens in pregnancy), congenital heart disease, aortic dilatation (Marfan, bicuspid valve), mechanical valves (anticoagulation dilemmas).
- **Asthma**: continue treatment; avoid prostaglandin F2α (carboprost).
- **SLE and antiphospholipid syndrome**: hydroxychloroquine, aspirin, LMWH; increased risks of pre-eclampsia, growth restriction, loss.
- **Renal disease**: higher risk of pre-eclampsia and progression (see chapter 49).
- **Mental health**: see section 8.4 and chapter 56.
- **Domestic violence**: begins or escalates in pregnancy; ask privately.

## 7. Labour, delivery and the puerperium

### 7.1 Diagnosing labour

True labour: regular painful contractions (increasing in frequency, duration and intensity), progressive cervical dilatation and effacement, with or without show and membrane rupture. **Stages**: first (latent phase to 4–6 cm; active phase 6 cm to full dilatation), second (full dilatation to birth of baby), third (birth of baby to delivery of placenta, within 30 minutes with active management).

**Partogram** monitoring: cervical dilatation, descent, contractions, fetal heart, maternal vital signs, liquor colour, caput, moulding. Expected progress in active phase ≥ 1 cm/hour in nullipara (ACOG now uses slower thresholds, ≥ 0.5 cm/hour). Delay should prompt reassessment of **Power** (contractions), **Passenger** (size, position, presentation) and **Passage** (pelvis). Augment with oxytocin if hypocontractile; caesarean if obstructed.

### 7.2 Preterm labour and premature rupture of membranes

**Preterm birth** (< 37 weeks) affects ≈ 10% of births. Symptoms: regular contractions, pelvic pressure, backache, discharge. Assess: sterile speculum (pooling of fluid, cervical dilatation), fetal fibronectin (negative predictive value) or cervical length (< 15 mm high risk, > 30 mm low risk), infection screen (urinalysis and culture, high vaginal swab, GBS), ultrasound.

*Management of threatened preterm labour (< 34 weeks):* **antenatal corticosteroids** (betamethasone 12 mg IM × 2 doses 24 hours apart) for 24–34 weeks; **magnesium sulphate** for neuroprotection < 32 weeks if delivery anticipated; **tocolysis** (nifedipine, atosiban) for up to 48 h to allow transfer in utero and steroid action; GBS prophylaxis; transfer to a unit with neonatal intensive care.

**Preterm prelabour rupture of membranes (PPROM)**: amniotic fluid leakage before 37 weeks; confirm by speculum examination (pooling, positive nitrazine or AmniSure/PAMG-1); avoid digital examination. Erythromycin or azithromycin plus ampicillin (or per local protocol), corticosteroids, delivery at 34–37 weeks or earlier if infection, abruption, fetal distress.

**Prevention**: vaginal progesterone (short cervix), cervical cerclage (cervical insufficiency), treat UTI.

### 7.3 Fetal monitoring

Intermittent auscultation in low-risk labour (every 15 min first stage, every 5 min second stage). Continuous CTG for risk factors (meconium, bleeding, oxytocin, epidural, abnormal auscultation). Interpret using the DR C BRAVADO approach: **D**efine **R**isk, **C**ontractions, **B**aseline **RA**te, **V**ariability, **A**ccelerations, **D**ecelerations, **O**verall impression. **Normal baseline** 110–160 bpm; **variability** 5–25 bpm; **no or early decelerations**. Concerning: tachycardia (>160), bradycardia (<110), reduced variability (<5 for > 40 minutes), variable decelerations with complications, late decelerations, prolonged decelerations (>3 min: act). Resuscitative measures: left lateral position, stop oxytocin, IV fluids, treat hypotension, correct maternal hypoxia, tocolytic (terbutaline) for hyperstimulation, vaginal examination to exclude cord prolapse. Fetal scalp lactate or expedited delivery if persistent abnormalities.

### 7.4 Obstetric emergencies at delivery

- **Cord prolapse**: palpable cord, abnormal CTG after rupture of membranes; emergency caesarean; elevate the presenting part off the cord, knee–chest or exaggerated Sims position, bladder filling, avoid handling cord.
- **Shoulder dystocia**: "turtle sign", failure of restitution; **HELPERR**: Call for **H**elp, **E**valuate for episiotomy, **L**egs (McRoberts manoeuvre), **P**ressure (suprapubic), **E**nter manoeuvres (Rubin, Woods screw), **R**emove posterior arm, **R**oll (all-fours). Document timings. Risk: macrosomia, diabetes, previous dystocia, obesity.
- **Breech**: external cephalic version at 36–37 weeks; vaginal breech birth requires an experienced practitioner; planned caesarean otherwise.
- **Uterine inversion, retained placenta, perineal tears** (first–fourth degree; repair third and fourth degrees by experienced operators; antibiotics).
- **Amniotic fluid embolism**: sudden hypoxia, hypotension, cardiac arrest, DIC; ICU, resuscitation, perimortem caesarean within 5 minutes of arrest.
- **Maternal cardiac arrest**: manual left uterine displacement, early airway, resuscitative hysterotomy by 4–5 minutes if no return of circulation.

### 7.5 The puerperium

Normal: lochia (red for 3–4 days, then serous, then white, up to 6 weeks), afterpains, breast engorgement at day 3–5, uterus involutes (fundus at umbilicus, shrinking about 1 cm/day, pelvic by day 10–14), transient low-grade fever with milk coming in.

**Postpartum danger signs (any woman presenting up to 6 weeks)**: heavy or increased bleeding, foul-smelling lochia, fever ≥ 38 °C, severe headache or visual disturbance, calf pain or swelling, chest pain or breathlessness, severe abdominal pain, wound infection, urinary retention or incontinence, mood change.

- **Puerperal sepsis**: endometritis (fever, uterine tenderness, foul lochia), wound infection, UTI, mastitis, group A streptococcus (rapidly progressive, toxic shock, high mortality). Treat with IV broad-spectrum antibiotics (e.g., piperacillin–tazobactam, or ampicillin + gentamicin + metronidazole; clindamycin for toxin suppression if GAS suspected), source control, resuscitation per sepsis guideline (chapter 39); escalate early because pregnant women compensate until they collapse.
- **Mastitis and breast abscess**: continue breastfeeding, flucloxacillin, ultrasound-guided drainage.
- **VTE, PPH, pre-eclampsia, peripartum cardiomyopathy, thyroiditis (postpartum thyroiditis in 5–10%), mental illness.**

## 8. The sick pregnant or postpartum woman

### 8.1 Using early warning scores

Use an obstetric-modified early warning score (MEOWS), because standard adult scores under-detect deterioration. Warning triggers include respiratory rate > 20–24 or < 10, SpO₂ < 95%, heart rate > 100–120 or < 50, SBP < 90 or > 160, DBP > 100 or > 90 (pre-eclampsia), temperature > 38 or < 36 °C, altered consciousness, oliguria, and pain. Escalate promptly.

### 8.2 Causes of maternal collapse and critical illness

The "4 Hs and 4 Ts" apply, with pregnancy-specific causes: haemorrhage (APH, PPH, ruptured ectopic, uterine rupture, splenic artery aneurysm), embolism (amniotic fluid, PE), hypertension (eclampsia, stroke), sepsis, cardiac (peripartum cardiomyopathy, MI from coronary dissection, arrhythmia, aortic dissection), anaesthetic complications (high spinal, local anaesthetic toxicity, aspiration), drugs (magnesium overdose, opioids), anaphylaxis, trauma/abuse, and pre-existing disease (diabetic ketoacidosis, sickle crisis, adrenal crisis).

- **Peripartum cardiomyopathy**: new heart failure with LVEF < 45% in the last month of pregnancy or within 5 months postpartum; risk factors: age > 30, multiparity, pre-eclampsia, multiple pregnancy, African ancestry. Orthopnoea, oedema, cough; BNP raised; echo diagnostic. Treat as heart failure with pregnancy-compatible drugs (beta-blocker, hydralazine/nitrates, diuretics; ACE inhibitors/ARBs/ARNI after delivery); bromocriptine as adjunct (evidence limited; with anticoagulation); avoid subsequent pregnancy if LVEF remains low.
- **Aortic dissection**: tearing chest or back pain; risk in Marfan, bicuspid valve, Turner, vascular Ehlers–Danlos.
- **Pulmonary embolism**: see 6.3.
- **Stroke**: severe headache, focal deficits; cerebral venous thrombosis (headache, seizures, papilloedema), PRES, reversible cerebral vasoconstriction syndrome; imaging by MRI.

### 8.3 Resuscitation modifications

Left lateral tilt or manual uterine displacement after 20 weeks, 100% oxygen, early intubation by experienced operator (difficult airway), IV access above the diaphragm, standard defibrillation and drugs, consider resuscitative hysterotomy at 4 minutes, treat reversible causes, call obstetric, neonatal and anaesthetic teams.

### 8.4 Perinatal mental health

- Screen at booking and postnatally with the **EPDS** (Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale; ≥ 10–13 positive; item 10 = self-harm) or PHQ-9; ask directly about suicidal thoughts. Suicide is a leading cause of late maternal death.
- **Baby blues**: tearfulness days 3–5, resolves by day 10. **Postnatal depression**: 10–15% of women (also fathers), peaks 4–12 weeks.
- **Postpartum psychosis** (1–2 per 1,000 births): onset within days to 2 weeks; rapid confusion, mania, delusions, hallucinations, insomnia, risk to self and infant; a psychiatric emergency; admission (mother-and-baby unit); recurrence risk is high, with strong association with bipolar disorder.
- **Anxiety and OCD**, **PTSD after traumatic birth**, **tokophobia**.
- **Medication**: weigh risks; sertraline is commonly preferred in breastfeeding; lithium requires careful monitoring; avoid valproate; see chapter 56.

## 9. Drugs and imaging in pregnancy and lactation

### 9.1 Principles

- Treat pregnant women who need treatment: untreated illness often harms the fetus more than the drug.
- Use the **lowest effective dose, the best-studied drug, monotherapy**; avoid new drugs; check teratogenic windows (organogenesis 3–10 weeks post-conception).
- Resources: national teratology information services, LactMed, UKTIS, MotherToBaby, product labelling.

| Generally avoided | Reason |
|---|---|
| ACE inhibitors, ARBs, aliskiren | Fetal renal dysgenesis, oligohydramnios, skull defects (2nd/3rd trimester) |
| Warfarin | Embryopathy (6–12 weeks), bleeding; replace with LMWH |
| Valproate | Neural tube defects, neurodevelopmental disorders |
| Isotretinoin, acitretin | Severe malformations |
| Methotrexate, mycophenolate, leflunomide | Teratogenic |
| Statins | Historically avoided; evidence is evolving; discuss |
| Tetracyclines | Teeth and bone staining |
| Fluoroquinolones | Cartilage concerns; use only if necessary |
| NSAIDs (especially ≥ 20–30 weeks) | Premature ductal closure, oligohydramnios; avoid after 30 weeks |
| Misoprostol, mifepristone | Pregnancy loss (except when intended) |
| Thalidomide, lenalidomide | Phocomelia |
| Carbimazole (first trimester) | Aplasia cutis; use PTU in first trimester |
| Live vaccines (MMR, varicella, yellow fever unless high risk) | Theoretical risk |

| Generally acceptable | |
|---|---|
| Paracetamol, penicillins, cephalosporins, erythromycin/azithromycin, nitrofurantoin (avoid near term), labetalol, nifedipine, methyldopa, levothyroxine, insulin, metformin, low-dose aspirin, LMWH, folic acid, iron, doxylamine/pyridoxine, ondansetron (small cleft risk, discuss), sertraline, lamotrigine, levetiracetam, inactivated vaccines | |

### 9.2 Imaging

- **Ultrasound and MRI (without gadolinium)** are safe and preferred.
- **Ionising radiation**: fetal doses from chest X-ray (<0.01 mGy), CT head, and extremity imaging are negligible; CT abdomen/pelvis delivers 10–50 mGy; fetal deterministic effects occur only above ≈ 100 mGy. **Do not withhold necessary imaging** (CTPA, CT for trauma, appendicitis assessed by ultrasound/MRI first). Iodinated contrast and gadolinium are given only when essential. Document counselling.

### 9.3 Breastfeeding

Most drugs pass in small amounts; relative infant dose < 10% is generally acceptable. Avoid: cytotoxics, radioactive iodine, amiodarone, lithium (unless monitored), high-dose benzodiazepines, codeine (ultra-rapid metabolisers), tramadol (caution), ergot derivatives, combined hormonal contraceptives (early postpartum). Safe: most antibiotics, antihypertensives (labetalol, nifedipine, enalapril), antidepressants (sertraline), LMWH and warfarin, insulin and metformin, inhaled medications, antiepileptics (monitor infant for sedation).

## 10. Contraception, abortion and gynaecological issues in brief

- **Emergency contraception**: ulipristal acetate 30 mg (up to 120 hours, more effective than levonorgestrel 1.5 mg up to 72 hours, esp. >72 hours or high BMI), copper IUD (most effective, up to 5 days after ovulation).
- **Contraception counselling**: LARC (IUD, implant) are most effective; combined hormonal contraception is contraindicated with migraine with aura, VTE history, smoking > 35 years, uncontrolled hypertension, breastfeeding < 6 weeks, major surgery with immobilisation, and various other conditions (WHO MEC).
- **Abortion**: provide non-judgemental counselling; medical abortion (mifepristone + misoprostol) up to 10–12 weeks (at home in many settings); surgical abortion. Follow local law.
- **Pelvic pain and abnormal uterine bleeding**: PALM-COEIN classification; always pregnancy test first.

## Worked cases

### Case 1: Lower abdominal pain in a young woman

**Presentation.** Ms C., 27, presents to the emergency department with 6 hours of left lower abdominal pain and light dark vaginal spotting. LMP was 7 weeks ago. She had chlamydia 3 years ago. HR 108, BP 104/62, pale, tenderness in left iliac fossa with guarding, cervical motion tenderness. Urine hCG positive.

**Reasoning.** Pregnancy of unknown location until proven otherwise; ectopic pregnancy is the top diagnosis (risk factor: previous PID). Tachycardia and borderline BP suggest haemorrhage. A normal BP does not exclude major intraperitoneal bleeding.

**Action.** Two large-bore IVs, bloods (FBC, group and crossmatch, quantitative hCG, Rh), FAST/transvaginal ultrasound: empty uterus, left adnexal mass, moderate free fluid in the pouch of Douglas and Morrison pouch. Gynaecology called; transfer to theatre for laparoscopic left salpingectomy; RhD-positive. Follow-up: counselling, contraception, early scan in next pregnancy.

**Lessons.** In a woman of reproductive age, pain and tachycardia = ectopic until excluded. Do not wait for hCG results in unstable patients.

### Case 2: A headache at 34 weeks

**Presentation.** Mrs N., 31, primigravida at 34 weeks, calls with severe frontal headache and flashing lights for 6 hours, with epigastric discomfort. BP at home 162/108. In hospital: BP 168/112 (repeated), brisk reflexes with 3 beats of clonus, protein:creatinine ratio 85 mg/mmol, platelets 82, ALT 210, creatinine 98 µmol/L. CTG normal.

**Reasoning.** Pre-eclampsia with severe features (severe hypertension, symptoms, thrombocytopenia, liver involvement) possibly evolving HELLP.

**Action.** Admit to a unit with obstetric and neonatal ICU. IV labetalol to target 140–150/90–100 mmHg; magnesium sulphate 4 g load over 20 min then 1 g/h; monitor reflexes, RR, urine output; fluid restriction 80 mL/h; betamethasone first dose. Repeat bloods (LDH, haptoglobin, film, coagulation). Plan delivery within 24–48 h after stabilisation; if worsening (HELLP progression, abruption, fetal compromise) deliver immediately. Postpartum: continue magnesium 24 h, monitor BP and labs for at least 72 h, antihypertensives compatible with breastfeeding, counsel on long-term cardiovascular risk.

**Lessons.** Visual disturbance and epigastric pain are pre-eclampsia until proven otherwise. Treat severe hypertension quickly to prevent stroke.

### Case 3: Bleeding at 33 weeks

**Presentation.** Ms D., 36, G3 P2, previous caesarean, presents at 33 weeks with painless bright red vaginal bleeding of about 300 mL, soft non-tender uterus, and an oblique fetal lie. BP 118/72, HR 92, CTG reactive. Scan 2 weeks ago noted a low-lying anterior placenta.

**Reasoning.** Placenta praevia, with a previous caesarean scar (risk of accreta spectrum). No digital vaginal examination.

**Action.** Admit; IV access, FBC, group and crossmatch (2 units), Kleihauer if RhD negative; ultrasound confirms major anterior praevia overlying the scar with loss of the retroplacental clear space (suspected accreta). Betamethasone course, anti-D if indicated, MRI/specialist ultrasound for accreta, plan delivery by caesarean at a unit with blood bank, interventional radiology, ICU and a multidisciplinary team (at 34–36 weeks or earlier if heavy bleeding), tranexamic acid, cell salvage. Counsel regarding hysterectomy.

**Lessons.** Painless bright bleeding in third trimester → praevia; check the placental site before touching. Previous caesarean + anterior praevia → think accreta.

### Case 4: Itching at 32 weeks

**Presentation.** Ms F., 29, 32 weeks, reports intense itch on palms and soles, worse at night, for 2 weeks. No rash apart from excoriations. Normal BP and urine. Previous pregnancy had similar itch.

**Reasoning.** Intrahepatic cholestasis of pregnancy until proven otherwise; exclude pre-eclampsia, hepatitis, gallstones, dermatoses.

**Action.** Fasting bile acids (50 µmol/L), ALT 140 U/L, ALP raised (placental, not diagnostic), bilirubin normal, hepatitis A/B/C and EBV/CMV negative, liver ultrasound normal. Start ursodeoxycholic acid 15 mg/kg/day, emollients, weekly LFT and bile acids, antenatal fetal surveillance per local policy; offer delivery at 37–38 weeks (earlier if bile acids ≥ 100). After delivery, repeat LFTs (normalise within 6 weeks), counsel about avoiding oestrogen-containing contraception, and recurrence (45–90%).

**Lessons.** Itch without rash in third trimester warrants bile acid and LFT testing; ICP increases risk of stillbirth in proportion to bile acid level.

### Case 5: Collapse after delivery

**Presentation.** Mrs P., 33, delivered a 4.2 kg infant vaginally 40 minutes ago after oxytocin augmentation. The placenta was delivered intact. She is pale, anxious and sweaty, with a soft, boggy uterus above the umbilicus and steady bright red bleeding. HR 128, BP 88/50, RR 26. Measured loss: 1,800 mL.

**Reasoning.** Primary PPH, uterine atony (Tone) most likely: macrosomia, augmented labour. Haemorrhagic shock grade III. Check Trauma, Tissue, Thrombin.

**Action.** Call for help (PPH protocol). Rub up uterine contraction, empty bladder, bimanual compression; oxytocin 5–10 IU slow IV then infusion; tranexamic acid 1 g IV; carboprost 250 µg IM (not asthmatic); two large-bore IVs, bloods (including fibrinogen), warm crystalloids/blood (O negative until crossmatched), massive transfusion protocol, calcium; examine for tears; consider EUA and Bakri balloon; theatre if bleeding persists; escalate to hysterectomy if fails. Fibrinogen 1.6 g/L → cryoprecipitate. Document, debrief, and follow-up for anaemia (IV iron) and Sheehan syndrome in severe cases.

**Lessons.** Early, simultaneous action. Maternal tachycardia and normal BP means significant loss. Fibrinogen is the earliest coagulation marker.

### Case 6: Breathless three weeks after birth

**Presentation.** Ms R., 38, emergency caesarean 3 weeks ago, now has 3 days of calf pain and 1 day of pleuritic chest pain and dyspnoea. HR 112, RR 24, SpO₂ 92%, BP 118/70, right calf swollen and tender. BMI 36.

**Reasoning.** Pulmonary embolism (risk factors: obesity, caesarean, age). Peripartum cardiomyopathy is also on the differential (breathlessness, tachycardia) but calf signs support VTE.

**Action.** Start therapeutic LMWH immediately if no contraindication (while arranging imaging), compression ultrasound (right femoral DVT) confirms; CTPA: bilateral segmental PE. ECG: sinus tachycardia, S1Q3T3. Echo: right ventricle normal. Continue LMWH for at least 3 months and a minimum of 6 weeks postpartum (total 3–6 months; warfarin acceptable postpartum and compatible with breastfeeding; DOACs avoided during breastfeeding). Advise on future pregnancies (prophylactic LMWH), avoid oestrogen contraception.

**Lessons.** Postpartum dyspnoea has a broad differential: PE, cardiomyopathy, pneumonia, anaemia, pre-eclampsia with pulmonary oedema. Treat suspected VTE while waiting for imaging.

### Case 7: "I can't stop crying" or something worse?

**Presentation.** Day 6 after her first baby, Ms K., 26, has not slept for three nights. Her husband reports that she believes the baby is "marked by the devil", talks rapidly, cannot be reassured, and last night tried to leave the house with the baby at 3 a.m. She is disoriented to time, laughs inappropriately, and then weeps.

**Reasoning.** Postpartum psychosis (acute onset within days of delivery, mania/delusions/confusion with risk to the infant) until proven otherwise. Exclude organic causes: infection, eclampsia, thyroid storm, electrolyte disturbance, cerebral venous thrombosis, substance use.

**Action.** Emergency psychiatric admission (mother-and-baby unit if available); never leave mother alone with the infant; safe arrangements for the baby and father; bloods (FBC, U&E, calcium, glucose, TSH, CRP), urine drug screen, examination, BP, neuroimaging if focal findings. Treatment: antipsychotic (olanzapine, quetiapine, haloperidol) ± lithium or benzodiazepine for sleep; ECT for severe cases. Counsel on breastfeeding and drug choice; recurrence risk in future pregnancies 30–50%: plan prophylaxis.

**Lessons.** Postpartum psychosis is rare but is an emergency; ask about thoughts of harming the baby; do not confuse with baby blues.

## Common pitfalls

1. **Failing to consider pregnancy** in any woman of reproductive age with abdominal pain, collapse, seizures, vomiting, or before imaging and prescribing.
2. **Excluding ectopic pregnancy because the BP is normal** or there are no risk factors.
3. **Relying on a single hCG** without ultrasound correlation; misusing the discriminatory zone to rule out ectopic.
4. **Digital vaginal examination in antepartum haemorrhage** before placenta praevia is excluded.
5. **Reading non-pregnant normal ranges** for creatinine, PaCO₂, haemoglobin, platelets, ALP, D-dimer and TSH.
6. **Treating hypotension as the first sign of haemorrhage**; the pregnant woman compensates until late.
7. **Visual estimation of blood loss** in PPH; delay in calling for help or giving tranexamic acid.
8. **Underestimating pre-eclampsia** in the absence of proteinuria or in the postpartum period.
9. **Fluid overload in pre-eclampsia**, leading to pulmonary oedema.
10. **Using magnesium without monitoring**, or forgetting calcium gluconate.
11. **Prescribing ACE inhibitors, warfarin, valproate, methotrexate or doxycycline** to women who might be or become pregnant.
12. **Withholding necessary imaging or treatment** because of fear of radiation or drug effects.
13. **Reassuring reduced fetal movements** without CTG and ultrasound.
14. **Missing perinatal depression or psychosis**, and not asking about suicidal thoughts and domestic abuse.
15. **Using standard early warning scores** that fail to detect pregnancy-related deterioration.
16. **Neglecting RhD status** and anti-D prophylaxis.
17. **Overlooking the postpartum period** as a high-risk time for VTE, hypertension, sepsis and mental illness.

---

## Key points

- Pregnancy changes physiology: blood pressure falls, heart rate rises, PaCO₂ falls, creatinine falls, haemoglobin falls; use pregnancy-specific ranges and expect late signs of shock.
- In early pregnancy, ask "where is it?" Ectopic pregnancy is a life-threatening diagnosis that needs hCG and transvaginal ultrasound, and resuscitation if unstable.
- Pre-eclampsia is a multisystem disorder: treat severe hypertension, give magnesium, deliver at the right time, and monitor postpartum.
- In antepartum haemorrhage, no digital examination until praevia is excluded; abruption may be concealed.
- PPH: the four Ts, uterotonics, tranexamic acid, early transfusion with fibrinogen replacement.
- Screen for gestational diabetes, anaemia, bacteriuria, and infections; prevent VTE; use LMWH.
- Reduced fetal movements require objective assessment.
- Medication and imaging decisions should balance maternal and fetal risks; do not withhold necessary care.
- Perinatal mental illness is common and sometimes an emergency; ask, screen and plan.

## Self-assessment

**Questions**

1. A woman with a positive pregnancy test, 7 weeks amenorrhoea, mild left iliac fossa pain and a normal BP has a transvaginal scan showing an empty uterus and no adnexal mass. Her hCG is 1,100 mIU/mL. What is the diagnosis and plan?
2. List the normal changes in creatinine and PaCO₂ in pregnancy and what a "normal" non-pregnant value would imply.
3. A 35-week pregnant woman has BP 164/110 and severe frontal headache. Outline immediate management, including drugs and monitoring.
4. A pregnant woman is bleeding painlessly at 34 weeks. Why must you avoid vaginal examination, and what is the first imaging test?
5. State the four Ts of PPH and give first-line drugs for atony.
6. Which antihypertensives are contraindicated in pregnancy, and which are first-line?
7. What are the signs of magnesium toxicity and the antidote?
8. When should a woman with reduced fetal movements be assessed, and what investigations are needed?
9. A woman receives LMWH for postpartum PE. Can she breastfeed? Which anticoagulants are acceptable?
10. What is the difference between "baby blues", postnatal depression and postpartum psychosis?

**Answers**

1. Pregnancy of unknown location (hCG below the discriminatory zone), with ectopic pregnancy still possible. Stable: safety-net, repeat hCG at 48 hours and review; ensure she knows to return with worsening pain, dizziness or collapse; early gynaecology follow-up; if hCG rises appropriately (>63% in 48 h from low starting values), repeat scan; if plateau or fall, evaluate for failing intrauterine pregnancy vs ectopic; methotrexate only after confirming no viable intrauterine pregnancy.
2. Creatinine falls to about 40–70 µmol/L (GFR increases 50%), so 90–100 µmol/L may signal renal impairment; PaCO₂ falls to about 4.0 kPa (30 mmHg) from compensated respiratory alkalosis, so a "normal" 5.0–5.5 kPa suggests respiratory fatigue and impending failure.
3. Severe hypertension with symptoms = severe pre-eclampsia until proven otherwise: admit to a high-dependency obstetric setting; IV labetalol (or oral nifedipine / IV hydralazine) to reduce BP to 140–150/90–100; magnesium sulphate 4–6 g load, then 1–2 g/h; bloods (FBC, LFT, U&E, urate, LDH, coagulation, urine PCR); CTG; fluid restriction; corticosteroids for fetal lung maturity; deliver after stabilisation at ≥ 34 weeks.
4. Because placenta praevia may be disturbed by digital examination, leading to torrential haemorrhage. First test: ultrasound for placental location (transabdominal and transvaginal).
5. Tone, Trauma, Tissue, Thrombin. For atony: uterine massage and bimanual compression, empty bladder, oxytocin, ergometrine (if not hypertensive), carboprost (if not asthmatic), misoprostol, tranexamic acid.
6. Contraindicated: ACE inhibitors, ARBs, direct renin inhibitors, atenolol (growth restriction), spironolactone (anti-androgenic effects), nitroprusside long-term. First-line: labetalol, nifedipine, methyldopa.
7. Loss of patellar reflexes, respiratory depression (RR < 12), oliguria, drowsiness, hypotension, arrhythmia, cardiac arrest. Stop the infusion, give calcium gluconate 1 g IV over 10 minutes, support ventilation.
8. Immediately on report (same day); investigations: history and risk factors, maternal observations, urine, fetal heart rate by Doppler, CTG for ≥ 20–30 min, ultrasound for growth/liquor/Doppler if persistent or risk factors; deliver if abnormal.
9. Yes; LMWH and warfarin are compatible with breastfeeding; DOACs are generally avoided because of insufficient data; continue at least 6 weeks postpartum and 3 months in total.
10. Baby blues: mild tearfulness days 3–10, self-limited; postnatal depression: persistent low mood, anhedonia, lasting > 2 weeks, needs treatment; postpartum psychosis: acute onset within days to weeks, with mania, delusions, confusion and risk to the infant, an emergency requiring admission.
