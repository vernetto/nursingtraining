# Chapter 67. Ultrasound at the Bedside

## Learning objectives

After studying this chapter you should be able to:

1. Explain the physical principles of ultrasound (reflection, attenuation, echogenicity, artefacts) and choose the correct probe, depth and mode.
2. Define point-of-care ultrasound (POCUS) as a focused, question-driven extension of the physical examination, and state its limits and the training and governance it requires.
3. Perform and interpret the core focused examinations: cardiac (FoCUS), lung and pleura, inferior vena cava and volume status, abdominal aorta, FAST/eFAST, biliary, renal/bladder, deep vein thrombosis (compression), early pregnancy, and ocular/soft-tissue/musculoskeletal applications.
4. Recognise normal appearances and the key abnormal signs: pericardial effusion and tamponade, LV and RV dysfunction, A-lines and B-lines, lung sliding and the lung point, consolidation and effusion, free fluid, aneurysm, hydronephrosis, gallstones and cholecystitis, DVT, ectopic pregnancy.
5. Combine ultrasound findings into protocols for undifferentiated shock (RUSH), dyspnoea (BLUE, lung–heart–IVC), cardiac arrest and trauma.
6. Use ultrasound to guide procedures (vascular access, thoracentesis, paracentesis, lumbar puncture, nerve blocks) safely.
7. Avoid common errors: anchoring on one finding, treating a "negative" scan as exclusion, over-reading artefact, operating beyond competence.

> **Note.** POCUS answers focused yes/no questions at the bedside; it does not replace comprehensive imaging by trained sonographers or radiologists. Competence requires supervised practice, documented images and local governance (credentialing, archiving, quality review).

## 1. Principles

### 1.1 Physics in practice

- Ultrasound uses sound waves of 2–15 MHz generated by piezoelectric crystals. Echoes return when sound crosses an interface between tissues of different acoustic impedance. The machine converts the time of return into depth and amplitude into brightness.
- **Frequency–depth trade-off**: higher frequency = better resolution but less penetration.

| Probe | Frequency | Use |
|---|---|---|
| **Phased array** (small footprint) | 1–5 MHz | Cardiac (intercostal windows), also lung/abdomen in a hurry |
| **Curvilinear** (convex) | 2–5 MHz | Abdomen, pelvis, obstetrics, FAST, kidneys, deep lung |
| **Linear** | 5–15 MHz | Vascular access, DVT, lung pleura, soft tissue, nerve blocks, eye, MSK |
| **Endocavitary** | 5–9 MHz | Transvaginal |

- **Echogenicity**: *anechoic* (black: fluid in simple cysts, blood, urine, bile), *hypoechoic* (darker than neighbouring tissue), *isoechoic*, *hyperechoic* (bright: bone, gas, calcification, fat/fascia). Bone and calcifications produce **posterior acoustic shadowing**; fluid-filled structures produce **posterior acoustic enhancement**.
- **Artefacts** are central in lung ultrasound: reverberation (A-lines), comet tails (B-lines), mirror image, side lobes, refraction.
- **Modes**: B-mode (2D), M-mode (motion over time: lung sliding "seashore sign", IVC collapse, TAPSE, pericardial motion), colour/power Doppler (flow, torsion, vein patency), spectral Doppler (velocities; advanced).
- **Image orientation convention**: In general, the screen marker (dot) corresponds to the probe indicator. For abdominal and general imaging, the indicator points to the patient's right (transverse) or head (longitudinal). Cardiac imaging in some countries uses the opposite (cardiology) convention; know your machine's settings.

### 1.2 The scanning habit

1. **Ask a focused question** ("Is there free fluid? Is the LV hyperdynamic? Is there lung sliding?").
2. Position the patient and ensure privacy; gel; start with preset and depth/gain adjustment.
3. **Two planes** for every finding (transverse + longitudinal; two windows) to avoid artefacts.
4. **Scan through the structure** (sweeping, fanning), not just a snapshot.
5. Interpret in clinical context and integrate with examination and other tests.
6. Record: save image/clip, document findings, limitations, and who performed.
7. **Escalate** to formal imaging if uncertain, discordant with clinical picture, or negative with high pre-test probability.

### 1.3 Strengths, limits and safety

Strengths: immediate, repeatable, no radiation, can be done during resuscitation, guides procedures. Limits: operator-dependent, limited by body habitus, bowel gas, subcutaneous emphysema, dressings, and by lack of comparators; a negative result in a focused scan rarely excludes disease. No known harm at diagnostic power, but ALARA applies (obstetric and ocular scans: minimise output and time; avoid Doppler unnecessarily in the first trimester).

## 2. Focused cardiac ultrasound (FoCUS / limited echo)

### 2.1 Windows and views

1. **Parasternal long axis (PLAX)**: 3rd–4th left intercostal space, indicator towards the right shoulder. Shows RV (anterior), LV, LA, aortic root, mitral and aortic valves, descending aorta, pericardium.
2. **Parasternal short axis (PSAX)**: rotate 90° clockwise; "doughnut" LV at the papillary muscle level; assess septal flattening (D-shaped LV in RV pressure overload).
3. **Apical four-chamber (A4C)**: apex beat, indicator to the left (varies by convention); compares RV:LV size, AV valves, global function, tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion (TAPSE).
4. **Subcostal four-chamber** and **subcostal IVC**: useful in COPD, ventilated patients, trauma and CPR.

### 2.2 Focused questions

| Question | Findings |
|---|---|
| **Is there a pericardial effusion? Tamponade?** | Anechoic fluid between the visceral and parietal pericardium; measured at end-diastole (small < 10 mm, moderate 10–20, large > 20 mm). Tamponade is a **clinical diagnosis** supported by echo: right atrial systolic collapse, RV diastolic collapse, plethoric non-collapsing IVC, respiratory variation > 25% in mitral inflow. Pleural fluid appears posterior to the descending aorta; pericardial fluid is anterior to it (PLAX). Fat pad can mimic loculated effusion |
| **Is LV systolic function reduced?** | Qualitative: normal (vigorous contraction, wall thickening > 30%), reduced (poor thickening), hyperdynamic (cavity obliterates in systole; hypovolaemia, sepsis, vasodilatation). E-point septal separation (EPSS) < 7 mm (mitral valve anterior leaflet to septum in PLAX) correlates with normal EF; > 10–12 mm suggests EF < 40–50% |
| **Is the RV dilated / strained?** | RV:LV ratio > 1 in A4C, McConnell's sign (RV free wall hypokinesis with preserved apex), D-shaped septum in PSAX, TAPSE < 16 mm, plethoric IVC: suggestive of acute PE, pulmonary hypertension, RV infarct |
| **Are there regional wall motion abnormalities?** | Segmental hypokinesis/akinesis suggests ischaemia but is operator-dependent; formal echo needed |
| **Gross valvular disease / vegetations** | Severe AS (calcified, immobile leaflets), flail mitral leaflet; formal echo required; vegetations need TOE/TTE |
| **Cardiac activity in arrest** | Organised cardiac motion (pseudo-PEA) vs true standstill; do not prolong CPR pauses (< 10 s; use subcostal view during rhythm check) |
| **Aortic root dilatation / dissection flap** | Root > 4 cm, flap in root; limited sensitivity; use CT angiography |

### 2.3 Cautions

Normal FoCUS does not exclude PE, ACS or valvular disease. EF visually estimated is crude. Left pleural effusion, pericardial fat pad and a hiatus hernia can mislead.

## 3. Lung and pleural ultrasound

### 3.1 Technique

Linear (pleural line detail) or curvilinear/phased array (depth); scan in each zone bilaterally: 2 anterior, 2 lateral, 2 posterior per side (BLUE points: upper and lower BLUE points, PLAPS point). Probe perpendicular to ribs (bat sign: two ribs with the pleural line between).

### 3.2 Normal and abnormal patterns

| Finding | Description | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| **Pleural line, lung sliding** | Hyperechoic line between ribs with to-and-fro shimmer; M-mode "seashore sign" | Visceral and parietal pleura in contact: pneumothorax unlikely at that point |
| **A-lines** | Horizontal reverberation echoes equal to pleural-line to skin distance | Aerated lung (normal, asthma, COPD, PE), or pneumothorax if sliding absent |
| **B-lines** | Vertical, hyperechoic, laser-like lines from the pleural line to the screen edge, erasing A-lines, moving with sliding | ≥ 3 per field in ≥ 2 zones bilaterally: interstitial syndrome (cardiogenic pulmonary oedema, ARDS, pneumonia, fibrosis, lymphangitis); symmetrical, "wet" lung; isolated B-lines (1–2) may be normal at bases |
| **Absent sliding + A-lines + lung point** | Absent sliding in M-mode ("stratosphere/barcode sign"), with the **lung point** (transition between sliding and non-sliding) | Pneumothorax (lung point pathognomonic; sensitivity ~90%, specificity ~99%). Mimics of absent sliding: main-stem intubation, apnoea, severe bullae, pleural adhesions, ARDS, pneumonectomy |
| **Consolidation** | Tissue-like (hepatisation) pattern, shred sign, dynamic air bronchograms (bright, moving with respiration), lower pole | Pneumonia (sensitivity ~90%), atelectasis (static air bronchograms, volume loss), infarct (wedge-shaped peripheral hypoechoic) |
| **Pleural effusion** | Anechoic or complex space above the diaphragm, spine sign (spine visible above the diaphragm), "jellyfish" lung floating | Estimate depth/volume (separation of pleural layers at end-expiration > 20 mm indicates ≥ 500 mL in supine ventilated patients); echogenic/septated suggests exudate, empyema, haemothorax; guide thoracentesis |
| **Diaphragm** | Hyperechoic line moving with respiration | Excursion, paralysis, weaning prediction |

### 3.3 Clinical protocols

- **BLUE protocol** (Bedside Lung Ultrasound in Emergency) for acute respiratory failure: A-profile (normal anterior sliding with A-lines) + DVT → pulmonary embolism; A-profile without DVT, with PLAPS → pneumonia; A-profile with no DVT and no PLAPS → asthma/COPD; B-profile (bilateral anterior B-lines with sliding) → pulmonary oedema; B′ profile (B-lines with absent sliding) → pneumonia; A/B profile (B-lines on one side, A on the other) → pneumonia; C profile (anterior consolidation) → pneumonia; A-profile + absent sliding + lung point → pneumothorax. Diagnostic accuracy about 90% in the original series; interpret with caution in different populations.
- **Lung ultrasound in COVID-19 and viral pneumonia**: bilateral, patchy, peripheral B-lines and subpleural consolidations, thickened irregular pleural line, spared areas.
- **Pulmonary oedema**: bilateral diffuse B-lines, often gravitational distribution; response to diuretics visible within hours.
- **Pulmonary embolism**: lung ultrasound may show peripheral wedge-shaped infarct; normal lung ultrasound does not exclude; combine with leg compression ultrasound and RV assessment.

## 4. Inferior vena cava and volume assessment

**Technique**: subcostal long-axis view; IVC entering the right atrium, 2 cm caudal to the hepatic veins; measure the diameter in M-mode or B-mode.

- Normal in a spontaneously breathing patient: diameter ≤ 2.1 cm and collapses > 50% on a sniff = RA pressure ~3 mmHg (0–5). Diameter ≤ 2.1 cm with < 50% collapse, or > 2.1 cm with > 50% collapse = ~8 mmHg (intermediate). Diameter > 2.1 cm with < 50% collapse = RA pressure ~15 mmHg (high).
- Small, collapsing IVC (< 1 cm) suggests hypovolaemia (or hyperdynamic state); but a plethoric IVC suggests raised right-sided pressure (tamponade, RV failure, PE, tricuspid regurgitation, mechanical ventilation, intra-abdominal hypertension) rather than "fluid overload".
- **Limitations**: unreliable in ventilated patients with high PEEP, raised intra-abdominal pressure, pulmonary hypertension, athletes, chronic right heart disease. Dynamic measures (IVC distensibility > 18% in controlled mechanical ventilation without arrhythmia; passive leg raise with velocity-time integral change > 10–12%) predict fluid responsiveness better than a static IVC diameter. Integrate with lung ultrasound (B-lines) before giving fluid: "fluid tolerance" matters as much as "fluid responsiveness".

## 5. Undifferentiated shock and cardiac arrest

### 5.1 RUSH protocol (Rapid Ultrasound in Shock and Hypotension): "pump, tank, pipes"

| Step | Question | Findings and diagnoses |
|---|---|---|
| **Pump** (heart) | Pericardial effusion/tamponade; LV contractility; RV size | Tamponade; cardiogenic shock (poor LV, dilated); obstructive shock from PE (dilated RV, septal flattening); hyperdynamic LV with small cavity → hypovolaemic/distributive |
| **Tank** (fluid status) | IVC; lungs (B-lines, pneumothorax, effusion); abdomen (free fluid in Morison's pouch, splenorenal, pelvis; FAST) | Small collapsing IVC + A-lines: hypovolaemia; plethoric IVC + B-lines: cardiogenic; free fluid: haemorrhage/ascites/peritonitis; pneumothorax (tension if shock) |
| **Pipes** (vessels) | Abdominal aorta (aneurysm, flap); IVC; femoral/popliteal compression for DVT | AAA (> 3 cm), dissection flap, DVT supporting PE |

Obstructive causes (tamponade, tension pneumothorax, massive PE) are rapidly diagnosable; treat immediately (pericardiocentesis, decompression, thrombolysis/embolectomy).

### 5.2 Cardiac arrest

In the pause for rhythm checks (< 10 seconds; preferably subcostal view), look for organised cardiac activity, tamponade, RV dilatation (PE), hyperdynamic empty heart (hypovolaemia), pneumothorax. POCUS must not delay compressions or defibrillation; absence of cardiac activity after prolonged ALS is associated with poor outcome but cannot be the sole basis for stopping resuscitation. Cardiac activity with no pulse is PEA/pseudo-PEA: treat reversible causes (4Hs and 4Ts).

## 6. Abdomen

### 6.1 FAST and eFAST (trauma)

**Focused Assessment with Sonography in Trauma** detects free fluid (presumed blood) in dependent spaces:
1. **Right upper quadrant**: hepatorenal space (Morison's pouch), right paracolic gutter, liver tip.
2. **Left upper quadrant**: splenorenal recess, subphrenic space (fluid collects here first), left paracolic gutter.
3. **Pelvis** (full bladder helpful): rectovesical pouch (men), pouch of Douglas (women).
4. **Pericardium** (subxiphoid).
5. **eFAST**: add lung views for pneumothorax (anterior sliding) and haemothorax.

Interpretation: free fluid appears anechoic (clotted blood echogenic) as a black stripe or triangle. **Positive FAST** in a hypotensive trauma patient = go to theatre/ressuscitative laparotomy or REBOA/angioembolization pathway. A **negative FAST** does not exclude injury (retroperitoneal, hollow viscus, early, solid organ without free fluid); a stable patient with mechanism needs CT. Repeat at intervals. Fluid in a non-trauma patient may be ascites, urine, pelvic fluid (physiologic in women).

### 6.2 Abdominal aorta and AAA

Transverse and longitudinal views from the xiphoid to the bifurcation (L4); measure outer wall to outer wall (transverse, perpendicular). **Aneurysm** ≥ 3.0 cm; > 5.5 cm (men) or > 5.0 cm (women) or rapid growth: surgical referral. In a hypotensive patient with abdominal/back/flank pain and a pulsatile mass, **a visible AAA with suspicion of rupture = immediate vascular surgery** (do not delay for CT in unstable patients). POCUS detects aneurysm (sensitivity ~99%) but not rupture (retroperitoneal blood poorly seen). Screening programmes use ultrasound for men aged 65.

### 6.3 Hepatobiliary

- **Gallstones**: hyperechoic mobile foci with posterior shadowing, gravity-dependent; sludge (low-level echoes, no shadow).
- **Acute cholecystitis**: stones + wall thickening > 3 mm (also from ascites, hepatitis, heart failure), pericholecystic fluid, gallbladder distension (> 4 cm short axis), **sonographic Murphy sign** (maximal tenderness over the gallbladder under the probe). Positive predictive value high when stones + sonographic Murphy + wall thickening. Gallstones alone with pain doesn't prove cholecystitis.
- **Common bile duct** (CBD) diameter: ≤ 6 mm normal in adults (add 1 mm per decade over 60; post-cholecystectomy up to 10 mm); dilatation suggests obstruction (stone, tumour). Intrahepatic duct dilatation ("shotgun sign"). POCUS cannot see distal CBD stones reliably.
- **Liver**: echogenicity (fatty liver bright, diaphragm obscured), nodular contour (cirrhosis), ascites, focal lesions.
- **Ascites**: anechoic fluid between bowel loops and abdominal wall; mark site for paracentesis (>3 cm pocket, away from epigastric vessels).

### 6.4 Kidneys, bladder, urinary tract

- **Hydronephrosis**: dilated calyces/pelvis (grades mild to severe); causes: stone, obstruction at PUJ/VUJ, prostate, tumour, pregnancy. Ultrasound finds hydronephrosis with sensitivity ~85–90% for stones needing intervention but misses most ureteric stones themselves; use low-dose CT when needed. **Obstruction with infection (pyonephrosis) is a urological emergency** (fever + hydronephrosis: decompress by nephrostomy or stent).
- **Bladder**: volume (retention; bladder scanner formula), clot, tumour, catheter balloon location; post-void residual (> 100–150 mL abnormal in adults).
- **Renal cysts, masses, size** (< 9 cm small kidneys in CKD; cortical thinning, increased echogenicity); polycystic kidneys.
- **Doppler**: renal artery stenosis and transplant evaluation by specialists.

### 6.5 Other abdominal applications

- **Appendicitis**: graded compression: non-compressible blind-ending tubular structure > 6 mm with surrounding fat echogenicity; first-line in children and pregnancy; CT otherwise.
- **Bowel obstruction**: dilated fluid-filled loops with to-and-fro peristalsis, "keyboard sign" (valvulae conniventes), collapsed colon; gives faster answer than X-ray; accuracy improves with operator experience.
- **Pyloric stenosis** (infants), **intussusception** (target/doughnut sign), **testicular torsion** (absent or reduced Doppler flow; do not delay surgical exploration if clinical suspicion is high), **ovarian torsion** (enlarged ovary, whirlpool sign; Doppler flow may be preserved).
- **Free air**: reverberation artefacts below the anterior abdominal wall, "enhanced peritoneal stripe sign"; unreliable; use CT.

## 7. Early pregnancy and pelvis

**Transabdominal (full bladder) and transvaginal** ultrasound: confirm **intrauterine pregnancy (IUP)**: gestational sac with yolk sac (definite IUP) ± embryo with cardiac activity. Discriminatory zone (β-hCG 1500–3500 IU/L) varies: do not rely on it to dismiss an ectopic. **Ectopic pregnancy**: no IUP, adnexal mass ("tubal ring"), free fluid in pelvis/Morison's pouch (haemoperitoneum) with positive hCG; pseudosac (fluid in the endometrial cavity without yolk sac). POCUS in the unstable patient with positive hCG and free fluid → emergency surgery. POCUS cannot exclude ectopic: formal early pregnancy unit review for pain/bleeding in early pregnancy. **Failed pregnancy/miscarriage**: empty sac > 25 mm, embryo ≥ 7 mm without heartbeat (definitive criteria); cautious with early labelling. **Heterotopic pregnancy** in assisted reproduction.

## 8. Vascular ultrasound: DVT and access

### 8.1 Compression ultrasound for DVT

Linear probe; **two-point (femoral vein at the common femoral/saphenofemoral junction and popliteal fossa) or whole-leg compression**: the normal vein collapses completely with gentle pressure; a **non-compressible vein** is diagnostic of DVT (sensitivity ~95% for proximal DVT). Colour Doppler flow and augmentation support. Superficial thrombophlebitis and calf veins require specific approaches. Pre-test probability (Wells) determines the strategy: low probability + negative D-dimer rules out; high probability + negative 2-point ultrasound should be followed by repeat scan at 5–7 days or whole-leg scan. Compare asymmetric leg swelling to alternative causes (Baker cyst, haematoma, cellulitis, lymphoedema).

### 8.2 Ultrasound-guided vascular access

Reduces failure and complication rates and is recommended for central venous (internal jugular), peripheral difficult access and arterial lines. **Technique**: Identify vein (compressible, non-pulsatile, thin wall) versus artery (pulsatile, thick wall, not easily compressed); use a sterile probe cover; **short-axis (out-of-plane) for orientation** or long-axis (in-plane) to track the needle tip; confirm guidewire in the vein before dilation; check lung sliding after subclavian/IJ access; confirm tip position (bubble test, ultrasound visualisation of right atrium) where available.

### 8.3 Procedures

- **Thoracentesis**: mark a site with effusion depth ≥ 1.5 cm, avoid diaphragm; pneumothorax risk reduced.
- **Paracentesis**: select a pocket ≥ 3 cm away from epigastric vessels; left lower quadrant is typical.
- **Pericardiocentesis**: subxiphoid or apical path with shortest distance and safest trajectory.
- **Lumbar puncture**: identify the interspinous space in obese or difficult cases.
- **Nerve blocks**: fascia iliaca block for hip fracture analgesia (reduces opioid requirements in elderly), serratus anterior/erector spinae for rib fractures, regional blocks.
- **Abscess drainage**: ultrasound distinguishes abscess (fluid pocket, cobblestoning) from cellulitis (cobblestone pattern with no collection).
- **Foreign body localisation** (wood, glass).

## 9. Other applications

- **Ocular**: retinal detachment (hyperechoic undulating membrane), vitreous haemorrhage, lens dislocation, **optic nerve sheath diameter** (> 5.0–5.7 mm, measured 3 mm behind the globe, suggests raised intracranial pressure; adjunct only), central retinal artery occlusion ("spot sign"). Do not apply pressure on the globe if rupture is suspected; use minimal pressure and generous gel.
- **Musculoskeletal**: fractures (cortical break, step-off) in children (forearm, clavicle) as an alternative to X-ray; joint effusion (knee, hip), tendon rupture (Achilles), muscle tears, foreign bodies, septic arthritis assessment (not a replacement for aspiration).
- **Soft tissue**: cellulitis vs abscess, necrotising fasciitis (gas, fluid along fascia: surgical emergency; do not delay for imaging), lymph nodes, hernias (reducible/incarcerated), testicular and scrotal pathology.
- **Thyroid, neck**: nodules (formal imaging), peritonsillar abscess (transcutaneous or intraoral), airway: tracheal ring identification, cricothyroid membrane localisation, confirmation of endotracheal tube placement (bilateral lung sliding, tracheal "double tract" sign), gastric content/volume assessment (aspiration risk).
- **Neonatal and paediatric**: hip dysplasia, cranial ultrasound through the anterior fontanelle (intraventricular haemorrhage, hydrocephalus), pneumonia/pneumothorax.
- **Transcranial Doppler**, **echocardiography in ICU**, **cardiac output monitoring** (advanced).
- **Pleural and chest wall**: pneumothorax after procedure, rib fractures (point of tenderness).

## 10. Training, governance and documentation

- **Competence**: structured courses, supervised scans (e.g., 25–50 per application depending on guidelines), logbook, assessment; maintain skills.
- **Scope**: define clinical questions and limits; label the scan "focused" or "limited" in the record.
- **Documentation**: indication, views obtained, findings, limitations, decision, archived clips; follow data protection.
- **Quality assurance**: random audit, peer review, comparison with formal imaging.
- **Communication**: tell the patient the scan is a focused bedside test; request formal study when uncertain; never discharge based on a negative POCUS alone where clinical suspicion persists.
- **Infection control**: clean probe and cable after each patient (low-level disinfection; high-level/sterile cover for invasive procedures and transvaginal probes).
- **Ergonomics**: avoid repetitive strain; adjust bed height, seating and screen position.

## Worked cases

### Case 1: Shock after a fall

**Presentation.** Mr A., 38, motorcyclist hit by a car, BP 78/40, HR 128, GCS 14, pale, abdominal tenderness. Primary survey completed, IV access obtained, 1 L crystalloid given with minimal response.

**Reasoning.** Hypotension in blunt trauma is haemorrhage until proven otherwise (chest, abdomen, pelvis, long bones, scalp). eFAST rapidly localises intracavitary blood.

**Action.** eFAST: anechoic free fluid in Morison's pouch and splenorenal recess; no pericardial effusion; lung sliding present bilaterally; no haemothorax. Positive FAST with unresponsive hypotension → activate massive transfusion protocol, permissive hypotension per protocol, tranexamic acid, immediate surgical team for laparotomy/theatre; pelvic binder if pelvic injury suspected; do not send to CT.

**Lessons.** A positive FAST in an unstable trauma patient directs to the operating room. A negative FAST would not have excluded retroperitoneal or pelvic bleeding.

### Case 2: Breathlessness and a full chest

**Presentation.** Mrs B., 80, acute breathlessness, SpO₂ 86%, RR 32, bibasal crackles, history of hypertension. Lung ultrasound: bilateral anterior and lateral multiple B-lines with sliding; A-lines absent; small bilateral effusions; heart: global LV hypokinesis, IVC plethoric (2.4 cm, < 30% collapse). No DVT.

**Reasoning.** BLUE protocol B-profile with reduced LV function and plethoric IVC: cardiogenic pulmonary oedema.

**Action.** Sit up, oxygen/CPAP, IV furosemide (and nitrates if hypertensive), ECG and troponin (ACS trigger), repeat lung ultrasound after treatment to track B-line clearing; cardiology for echo.

**Lessons.** Bedside ultrasound shortens the time to the correct diagnosis and gives objective response monitoring.

### Case 3: The same breathlessness with A-lines

**Presentation.** Mr C., 45, post-operative day 4, pleuritic chest pain, tachycardia 118, SpO₂ 90%. Lung ultrasound: A-lines everywhere, normal sliding. Heart (A4C): RV larger than LV, septal flattening in PSAX, TAPSE 11 mm. Leg compression: non-compressible left popliteal vein.

**Reasoning.** A-profile + DVT in the setting of RV strain: PE with right heart strain; avoids a CT dilemma in an unstable patient.

**Action.** Start therapeutic anticoagulation; if hypotension/shock → systemic thrombolysis (or catheter-directed/embolectomy) per PE response team; otherwise confirm with CTPA when stable; risk stratify (sPESI, troponin, RV).

**Lessons.** Combining lung, heart and leg ultrasound can reach the diagnosis even when each view alone is non-specific.

### Case 4: Chest pain and low blood pressure after cardiac surgery

**Presentation.** Ms D., 62, 5 days after valve surgery, becomes hypotensive (80/50), tachycardic, with raised JVP, muffled heart sounds, reduced chest drain output. Subcostal view: moderate circumferential pericardial effusion with posterior clot compressing the right atrium; RV diastolic collapse; plethoric IVC.

**Reasoning.** Post-operative tamponade (localised clot may not look like a classic effusion; TTE can miss posterior collections).

**Action.** Urgent cardiothoracic surgery for re-exploration/drainage; maintain preload with fluids, avoid positive-pressure ventilation where possible, avoid diuretics; needle pericardiocentesis is inappropriate for clotted blood. TOE if TTE is inconclusive and the patient is stable.

**Lessons.** Tamponade is a clinical and echo diagnosis; loculated postoperative effusions need surgical drainage.

### Case 5: Flank pain and fever

**Presentation.** Mrs E., 55, severe left flank pain, vomiting, fever 38.9 °C, BP 100/60, HR 112. Ultrasound: moderate left hydronephrosis with dilated proximal ureter, echogenic debris in the collecting system.

**Reasoning.** Obstructive pyelonephritis (infected hydronephrosis, possible pyonephrosis): urological emergency with sepsis risk.

**Action.** Sepsis bundle: blood and urine cultures, IV broad-spectrum antibiotics (e.g., piperacillin–tazobactam or ceftriaxone per local resistance), fluids, analgesia; urgent decompression (retrograde stent or percutaneous nephrostomy); CT KUB to identify the stone; definitive stone treatment after the infection resolves.

**Lessons.** Obstruction + infection is time-critical; ultrasound identifies it quickly, even if it does not show the stone.

### Case 6: Pelvic pain with a positive pregnancy test

**Presentation.** Ms F., 29, 7 weeks amenorrhoea, sudden lower abdominal pain, dizziness, BP 90/55, HR 120, positive urine hCG. POCUS: empty uterus, free fluid in Morison's pouch and the pouch of Douglas.

**Reasoning.** Ruptured ectopic pregnancy with haemoperitoneum until proven otherwise; unstable.

**Action.** Two large-bore IVs, group and crossmatch/blood, emergency gynaecology, theatre for laparoscopy/laparotomy; give anti-D if Rh-negative; do not delay for formal scan.

**Lessons.** In an unstable woman with positive hCG, an empty uterus and free fluid mandate surgery.

### Case 7: A "pneumothorax" that is not

**Presentation.** Mr G., 70, COPD with severe bullous disease, acute dyspnoea. Lung ultrasound on the right: absent sliding, A-lines, no lung point; CXR: hyperlucent area with a bulla, no pleural line.

**Reasoning.** Absent sliding can be caused by bullae and pleural adhesions; the absence of a lung point and the CXR suggest a bulla rather than pneumothorax. CT chest is needed if uncertainty persists.

**Action.** Avoid inserting a chest drain into a bulla (risk of bronchopleural fistula); CT chest to confirm; treat exacerbation (bronchodilators, steroids, antibiotics as indicated, NIV if hypercapnic respiratory failure).

**Lessons.** Absent sliding is not specific; look for the lung point and use another modality when the result will change management.

## Common pitfalls

1. **Not asking a focused question** and performing a "scan for everything".
2. **Over-interpreting artefacts** (B-lines in normal bases; mirror image; side lobes).
3. **Treating a negative POCUS as exclusion** of PE, injury, appendicitis, ectopic.
4. **Static IVC diameter** as a sole guide to fluid administration.
5. **Delaying CPR or resuscitation** to obtain images.
6. **Poor technique**: single plane, wrong depth/gain, no sweeping through the organ, wrong orientation.
7. **Confusing pleural and pericardial fluid** (descending aorta landmark), or fat pad with effusion.
8. **Attributing hydronephrosis to a stone** without considering other causes and infection.
9. **Ignoring patient factors**: obesity, gas, subcutaneous emphysema, dressings.
10. **Operating beyond competence** without supervision, documentation or follow-up.
11. **Failing to escalate** when POCUS and the clinical picture disagree.
12. **Poor infection control** (probe cleaning, sterile sheath).
13. **Not recording** images and findings.
14. **Using ultrasound to gain false reassurance** and delaying definitive imaging or surgery.

## Key points

- POCUS is an extension of the examination that answers focused questions; integrate findings with clinical data.
- Learn four core applications first: lung (sliding, A/B-lines, effusion, consolidation), cardiac (effusion, LV/RV function), IVC/volume, and FAST/free fluid; then aorta, DVT, biliary, renal, pelvic.
- Use protocols (RUSH, BLUE, eFAST) to structure the scan in shock, dyspnoea and trauma.
- A positive finding often changes management immediately (tension pneumothorax, tamponade, free fluid in unstable trauma, ruptured AAA, hydronephrosis with sepsis).
- A negative focused scan rarely excludes disease; escalate to formal imaging.
- Ultrasound guidance makes procedures safer (central lines, thoracentesis, paracentesis, nerve blocks).
- Competence, documentation and governance are mandatory.

## Self-assessment

**Questions**

1. Which probe would you use for a central line, for the heart, and for the abdomen?
2. Describe the ultrasound findings in pneumothorax and the pitfalls.
3. What B-line pattern indicates pulmonary oedema and what other conditions produce it?
4. List four echocardiographic signs suggesting acute PE with right heart strain.
5. How do you identify tamponade physiologically on echo, and why is it also a clinical diagnosis?
6. Describe the views of eFAST and how to interpret a negative FAST in a stable trauma patient.
7. What IVC findings suggest high versus low right atrial pressure, and what are the limits for fluid responsiveness?
8. How is acute cholecystitis diagnosed on ultrasound?
9. A woman has a positive pregnancy test, hypotension and free fluid in Morison's pouch. What is the diagnosis and the action?
10. What are the criteria for diagnosing DVT with compression ultrasound?

**Answers**

1. Linear (central line); phased array (heart); curvilinear (abdomen).
2. Absent lung sliding (stratosphere sign on M-mode), A-lines without B-lines, lung point (pathognomonic); pitfalls: bullae, adhesions, main-stem intubation, apnoea can also abolish sliding.
3. Bilateral diffuse (≥ 3 per field in ≥ 2 zones each side) B-lines with preserved sliding; also ARDS, pneumonia/viral pneumonitis, fibrosis, lymphangitis.
4. Dilated RV (RV:LV > 1), septal flattening (D-shaped LV), McConnell's sign, reduced TAPSE (< 16 mm), plethoric IVC; also thrombus in transit.
5. RA systolic and RV diastolic collapse, plethoric IVC, respiratory variation in inflow; the diagnosis rests on haemodynamic compromise (hypotension, raised JVP, pulsus paradoxus) because size of effusion and echo signs vary.
6. Views: RUQ (Morison's), LUQ (splenorenal), pelvis, pericardium, plus lung views for pneumothorax/haemothorax; a negative FAST does not exclude injury: repeat and CT in stable patients with significant mechanism.
7. IVC ≤ 2.1 cm collapsing > 50% suggests low RA pressure (~3 mmHg); > 2.1 cm and < 50% collapse suggests high (~15 mmHg); static diameter is a poor predictor of fluid responsiveness, especially in ventilated patients; use dynamic measures and lung ultrasound.
8. Gallstones, wall thickening > 3 mm, pericholecystic fluid, distension, sonographic Murphy sign (combined with clinical presentation).
9. Ruptured ectopic pregnancy with haemoperitoneum; emergency resuscitation, blood, gynaecology/surgery; anti-D if Rh-negative.
10. Non-compressible vein under probe pressure (also intraluminal echogenic thrombus, absent flow and augmentation on Doppler).
