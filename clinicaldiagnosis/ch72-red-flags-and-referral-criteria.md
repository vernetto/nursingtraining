# Chapter 72. Red Flags and Referral Criteria

## Learning objectives

After studying this chapter you should be able to:

1. Define a red flag and explain its limits: red flags raise the probability of serious disease but are individually non-specific, and their absence does not exclude it.
2. Recognise the red flags for the major symptom groups (chest pain, dyspnoea, headache, abdominal pain, back pain, neurological, genitourinary, gynaecological/obstetric, paediatric, psychiatric, skin and eye) and respond proportionately.
3. Classify the urgency of a problem into emergency (minutes), urgent (hours to days), soon (weeks) and routine, and match each to the correct route (ambulance/ED, same-day assessment, rapid-access clinic, suspected-cancer pathway, routine outpatient).
4. Apply suspected-cancer referral principles, including symptom combinations, age thresholds, safety-netting and the avoidance of unnecessary delay.
5. Recognise safeguarding, mental health and capacity red flags, and know when to escalate or act without consent.
6. Write an effective referral or handover (SBAR) containing the question, the key findings, the urgency, the actions already taken and the patient's wishes.
7. Combine red flags with context (age, comorbidity, immunosuppression, pregnancy) and avoid both under-referral and over-referral.
8. Document red flags and decisions, including shared decisions to observe.

> **Note.** Referral criteria differ between countries and health systems. The thresholds below follow widely used guidance (for example NICE suspected-cancer recognition and referral, ESC, AHA/ACC, BTS and national paediatric guidance) at the time of writing; always follow local pathways.

## 1. Principles

### 1.1 What is a red flag?

A red flag is a symptom, sign, test result or risk factor that increases the probability of a serious, time-sensitive condition (cancer, infection, vascular catastrophe, neurological compromise, fracture, surgical emergency) enough to change management.

Properties and limits:

- **Low individual positive predictive value.** Most patients with a red flag do not have serious disease (for example, unintentional weight loss is caused by cancer in a minority); most patients with serious disease have at least one flag, but some have none.
- **Likelihood ratios are modest** (often 2–5); combinations and context carry more weight than single items.
- **Absence is not exclusion.** Early, atypical or masked presentations (older adults, steroid-treated, immunosuppressed, children) may have none.
- **Context alters weight**: "new headache" at 25 and at 65 mean different things; "fever" in a neutropenic patient is an emergency.
- **Evidence base is uneven.** Many lists are consensus-based; use them as prompts, not as proofs.

### 1.2 Red flags, yellow flags and other flags

| Flag | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Red** | Possible serious pathology | Cauda equina features in back pain |
| **Orange** | Psychiatric risk | Suicidal ideation, psychosis |
| **Yellow** | Psychosocial barriers to recovery | Fear-avoidance, catastrophising, work dissatisfaction |
| **Blue/Black** | Work and compensation context | Perceived job strain, litigation |
| **Safeguarding** | Risk to child or vulnerable adult | Injuries inconsistent with history |

### 1.3 Urgency framework

| Category | Time frame | Examples | Route |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Emergency** | Minutes–1 hour | Cardiac arrest, STEMI, anaphylaxis, stroke within the treatment window, sepsis with shock, suspected bacterial meningitis, status epilepticus, massive haemorrhage, ruptured AAA/ectopic, tension pneumothorax, suicide attempt in progress | Resuscitate; 999/112/ambulance; ED/ICU; direct to cath lab/theatre |
| **Urgent / same day** | Hours | NSTEMI, PE, DKA, acute kidney injury with hyperkalaemia, cauda equina, testicular torsion, acute limb ischaemia, obstructed infected kidney, GCA, threatened miscarriage with pain, new AF with instability, deep vein thrombosis, acute psychosis | Same-day admission/assessment unit/ED; telephone discussion with on-call team |
| **Rapid access / soon** | Days–2 weeks | Suspected cancer (2-week pathways), new TIA (within 24 h), unstable angina outpatient, first seizure, new atrial fibrillation (stable), haemoptysis, rectal bleeding in older adults, breast lump, postmenopausal bleeding, jaundice | Fast-track clinics, diagnostic hubs, direct-access imaging |
| **Routine** | Weeks–months | Stable chronic disease, benign symptoms | Routine outpatient referral or primary-care management |

## 2. Red flags by presentation

### 2.1 Chest pain

| Red flag | Concern |
|---|---|
| Pain at rest > 15–20 minutes, crescendo angina, radiation to jaw/arm with sweating or dyspnoea | ACS |
| Syncope, hypotension, new heart failure, ventricular arrhythmia | High-risk ACS, PE, tamponade |
| Sudden tearing pain radiating to the back, pulse/BP difference, neuro deficit | Aortic dissection |
| Pleuritic pain with dyspnoea, tachycardia, DVT signs, recent surgery/immobility/cancer | PE |
| Dyspnoea, absent breath sounds, tracheal deviation | Tension pneumothorax |
| Vomiting then severe pain, subcutaneous emphysema | Oesophageal rupture |
| Fever, productive cough, pleuritic | Pneumonia/empyema |
| Pain after cocaine, young patient | Coronary vasospasm/dissection |
| Pain with weight loss, haemoptysis, supraclavicular nodes, smoking | Lung cancer |

**Referral**: ST elevation or equivalents, haemodynamic compromise, ongoing ischaemic pain or positive/rising troponin → emergency ambulance to the ED/cath lab. Possible ACS with a negative initial work-up → rapid chest pain clinic or CT coronary angiography. See chapters 31, 65, 71.

### 2.2 Dyspnoea and cough

| Red flag | Concern |
|---|---|
| Stridor, drooling, hoarse voice, rapid onset after allergen | Airway obstruction, anaphylaxis, epiglottitis |
| Unable to speak in sentences, silent chest, cyanosis, exhaustion, confusion | Life-threatening asthma/COPD |
| SpO₂ < 92% (<90% for ED), RR ≥ 30, HR > 120, new confusion | Severe pneumonia, PE, HF |
| Orthopnoea, paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnoea, oedema | Heart failure |
| Haemoptysis, weight loss, persistent cough > 3 weeks (smoker), finger clubbing, hoarseness | Lung cancer, TB, bronchiectasis |
| Asymmetric leg swelling, recent travel/surgery | PE |
| Cough with night sweats, contacts, immunosuppression | TB |
| Foreign body history in a child, sudden choking | Airway foreign body |

**Referral**: respiratory distress → emergency; persistent haemoptysis or suspicious CXR → 2-week respiratory pathway; chronic cough > 8 weeks → CXR and spirometry.

### 2.3 Headache and neurological symptoms

| Red flag | Concern |
|---|---|
| Thunderclap onset (peak within 1 minute), worst ever | SAH, RCVS, dissection, CVT, pituitary apoplexy |
| Fever, neck stiffness, rash, photophobia, reduced consciousness | Meningitis/encephalitis |
| New headache > 50 years, jaw claudication, scalp tenderness, visual loss, raised ESR/CRP | Giant cell arteritis |
| Progressive headache, morning vomiting, papilloedema, focal deficit, seizures, personality change | Space-occupying lesion/raised ICP |
| Headache after trauma, anticoagulation | Intracranial haemorrhage |
| Headache with systemic features (cancer, HIV, immunosuppression, pregnancy/postpartum) | Metastasis, abscess, toxoplasmosis, pre-eclampsia, CVT |
| Positional (worse lying) or Valsalva/exertion | Raised ICP, Chiari, tumour |
| Red eye with halos, vomiting | Acute angle-closure glaucoma |
| Multiple household members, winter | Carbon monoxide |

| Neurological | Concern and response |
|---|---|
| Sudden unilateral weakness, facial droop, speech disturbance, visual loss, ataxia | Stroke: FAST/BE-FAST → ambulance, stroke unit (thrombolysis/thrombectomy window) |
| TIA symptoms resolved | Urgent specialist assessment within 24 h; aspirin 300 mg unless contraindicated |
| First seizure; seizure with fever, head injury, prolonged (> 5 min) | ED if ongoing/complicated; first seizure clinic within 2 weeks |
| Progressive weakness over days, areflexia | Guillain–Barré: admit (respiratory monitoring) |
| Rapid cognitive decline, hallucinations, myoclonus | Encephalitis, CJD, delirium: urgent neurology |
| New focal deficit with cancer | Metastasis, cord compression |
| Diplopia, ptosis, fatigable weakness | Myasthenia: bulbar/respiratory involvement is an emergency |
| Visual loss: painless sudden (retinal artery/vein occlusion, retinal detachment), painful (optic neuritis, glaucoma, GCA) | Same-day ophthalmology |

### 2.4 Abdominal pain and gastrointestinal

| Red flag | Concern |
|---|---|
| Peritonism (rigidity, guarding, rebound), shock | Perforation, ruptured AAA/ectopic, mesenteric ischaemia |
| Pain out of proportion to signs, AF, vasculopathy | Acute mesenteric ischaemia |
| Colicky pain, vomiting, distension, absolute constipation | Bowel obstruction |
| Fever, rigors, jaundice, RUQ pain | Cholangitis |
| Haematemesis, melaena, haematochezia with instability | GI bleed |
| Pulsatile mass, back/flank pain, hypotension | AAA |
| Severe pain with diabetes/vomiting/Kussmaul breathing | DKA |
| Young woman with pain + amenorrhoea | Ectopic pregnancy |
| Testicular pain in adolescent | Torsion (surgical emergency, within 6 hours) |
| Dysphagia, odynophagia, weight loss, vomiting, persistent dyspepsia > 55 years | Oesophagogastric cancer (2-week) |
| Change in bowel habit > 6 weeks, rectal bleeding, iron deficiency anaemia, abdominal mass, weight loss (age > 50) | Colorectal cancer (2-week/FIT pathway) |
| Persistent bloating, early satiety, pelvic/abdominal pain (women > 50) | Ovarian cancer (CA-125, ultrasound) |
| Painless jaundice, new-onset diabetes with weight loss (age > 60) | Pancreatic cancer |
| Chronic diarrhoea, nocturnal, blood, weight loss, family history | IBD, coeliac, cancer |

### 2.5 Back and musculoskeletal

| Red flag | Concern |
|---|---|
| Saddle anaesthesia, urinary retention/incontinence, faecal incontinence, bilateral leg weakness | Cauda equina syndrome: emergency MRI |
| Progressive neurological deficit, band-like pain, upper motor neuron signs | Cord compression (metastasis, infection) |
| Fever, IV drug use, recent bacteraemia, diabetes, immunosuppression | Spinal infection (discitis, epidural abscess) |
| Age > 50 with cancer history, weight loss, night/rest pain | Metastasis, myeloma |
| Trauma, osteoporosis, steroids, age > 70 | Vertebral fracture |
| Age < 20 or > 55 at onset, non-mechanical pain, morning stiffness > 30 min improving with exercise (age < 40) | Inflammatory spondyloarthritis (routine/soon referral) |
| Severe unilateral hot swollen joint with fever | Septic arthritis (aspirate the same day) |
| Limb pain, pallor, pulselessness, paraesthesia, paralysis, perishingly cold | Acute limb ischaemia |
| Severe pain with swelling, tense compartment, pain on passive stretch | Compartment syndrome |
| Painful swollen calf | DVT: Wells, ultrasound |
| Limp, hip pain, fever in child | Septic arthritis, SUFE, Perthes |

### 2.6 Genitourinary and renal

| Red flag | Concern |
|---|---|
| Visible haematuria without infection; non-visible haematuria > 45–60 years | Urological cancer (urgent referral) |
| Loin pain + fever + hydronephrosis | Obstructed infected kidney: urgent decompression |
| Anuria, oliguria, rapidly rising creatinine, hyperkalaemia | AKI |
| Frank haematuria with clots, retention | Urology urgent |
| Testicular lump, scrotal swelling | Testicular cancer (2-week) |
| Raised PSA with abnormal MRI/DRE, bone pain | Prostate cancer (urgent) |
| Priapism > 4 hours | Emergency (sickle cell, drugs) |
| Painful swollen testis in young man | Torsion vs epididymitis: exploration if torsion not excluded |

### 2.7 Gynaecological and obstetric

| Red flag | Concern |
|---|---|
| Positive pregnancy test, abdominal pain, shoulder-tip pain, bleeding, collapse | Ectopic pregnancy |
| Heavy bleeding with clots, dizziness | Miscarriage, haemorrhage |
| Pregnancy: severe headache, visual disturbance, epigastric pain, hypertension, proteinuria | Pre-eclampsia/eclampsia/HELLP |
| Reduced fetal movements | Fetal compromise: same-day assessment |
| Third-trimester bleeding with pain/rigid uterus | Abruption |
| Painless bleeding, previa | Placenta praevia |
| Fever, offensive discharge, uterine tenderness postpartum | Sepsis |
| Postpartum: unilateral leg pain, dyspnoea; severe mood change, psychosis, thoughts of harm | VTE; postpartum psychosis |
| Postmenopausal bleeding | Endometrial cancer (urgent) |
| Persistent intermenstrual/post-coital bleeding with abnormal cervix | Cervical cancer |
| Breast lump, skin tethering, nipple discharge/retraction, axillary mass | Breast cancer (2-week) |

### 2.8 Paediatric red flags (illustrative)

| Red flag | Concern |
|---|---|
| Infant < 3 months with fever ≥ 38 °C | Serious bacterial infection: admit/septic work-up |
| Non-blanching rash with fever | Meningococcal disease: IM/IV antibiotics immediately |
| Poor feeding, lethargy, bile-stained vomiting, abdominal distension | Obstruction, sepsis, volvulus |
| Respiratory distress: grunting, nasal flaring, retractions, RR > 60, SpO₂ < 92%, apnoeas | Bronchiolitis, pneumonia, asthma |
| Drooling, stridor, toxic appearance | Epiglottitis, croup, foreign body |
| Prolonged fever > 5 days with rash, conjunctivitis, lymphadenopathy | Kawasaki disease |
| Bilious vomiting in neonate | Malrotation/volvulus (emergency) |
| Currant jelly stool, intermittent drawing up of legs | Intussusception |
| Headache with morning vomiting, ataxia, head tilt | Posterior fossa tumour |
| Failure to thrive, developmental regression | Metabolic, neurological, chronic illness |
| Unexplained bruising, fractures, burns inconsistent with history/delay in presentation | Child abuse (safeguarding) |
| Limp with fever | Septic arthritis, osteomyelitis |
| Polyuria, polydipsia, weight loss, abdominal pain, deep breathing | New-onset diabetes/DKA |
| Dehydration, sunken fontanelle, reduced urine output | Gastroenteritis with shock |
| Parental concern ("something is not right") | Strongly predictive of serious illness |

*Use traffic-light (NICE) systems for feverish children: red features (pallor/mottled/ashen, no response to social cues, grunting, RR > 60, reduced skin turgor, non-blanching rash, bulging fontanelle, neck stiffness, status epilepticus, focal signs) require urgent referral.*

### 2.9 Older adults

Atypical presentation of serious illness (sepsis with confusion or falls, MI without chest pain, absent fever). Red flags: new delirium; unexplained falls; new incontinence; rapid functional decline; unintentional weight loss; syncope with injury; pressure sore or evidence of neglect; polypharmacy with sedatives (see chapter 61). Escalate promptly: "off legs" is an indication to find the cause.

### 2.10 Skin and eye

| Red flag | Concern |
|---|---|
| Non-blanching purpuric rash with fever | Meningococcaemia, sepsis |
| Painful skin out of proportion, rapidly spreading erythema, bullae, crepitus | Necrotising fasciitis (emergency) |
| Widespread blistering, mucosal involvement, fever after new drug | SJS/TEN, DRESS |
| Changing pigmented lesion (ABCDE), nodular, ulcerating, bleeding | Melanoma (2-week) |
| Non-healing ulcer or scaly lesion on sun-exposed skin | SCC/BCC (urgent/soon) |
| Erythroderma | Drug reaction, lymphoma, psoriasis (admit) |
| Sudden painless visual loss, curtain, flashes/floaters | Retinal detachment, occlusion: same-day ophthalmology |
| Painful red eye with reduced acuity, halos, fixed mid-dilated pupil | Acute glaucoma |
| Chemical burn | Immediate irrigation |
| Orbital cellulitis: proptosis, painful eye movement, fever | Emergency admission, CT |
| Leukocoria (white pupillary reflex) in a child | Retinoblastoma: urgent |

## 3. Suspected cancer: principles of recognition and referral

### 3.1 Principles

1. **Positive predictive value (PPV) thresholds**: guidance (NICE NG12) moved to a 3% PPV threshold for urgent investigation or referral: where a symptom or combination has a ≥ 3% chance of cancer, refer on a suspected-cancer pathway or perform a direct-access test.
2. **Symptom combinations, persistent symptoms, and risk factors (age, smoking, family history, previous cancer, immunosuppression)** increase the probability.
3. **Safety-netting** for non-specific symptoms: review at defined intervals; do not rely on a single negative test.
4. **Direct-access diagnostic tests** (CXR, ultrasound, endoscopy, CT, FIT) reduce delays.
5. **Non-specific symptom pathways** (for example, unexplained weight loss, fatigue, pain) may be served by diagnostic centres.
6. **Never delay referral because of a normal screening test, a normal CXR, or a normal blood test.**
7. **Document** the suspicion, communicated urgency and the patient's understanding.

### 3.2 Examples of symptom triggers (adult; illustrative, check local pathway)

| Cancer | Referral/test triggers |
|---|---|
| **Lung** | Age ≥ 40 with haemoptysis; CXR suggestive of lung cancer; age ≥ 40 with two or more of: cough, fatigue, dyspnoea, chest pain, weight loss, appetite loss (≥ 1 if smoker/asbestos exposure); persistent/recurrent chest infection; finger clubbing; supraclavicular nodes; thrombocytosis |
| **Colorectal** | Age ≥ 40 with unexplained weight loss and abdominal pain; age ≥ 50 with unexplained rectal bleeding; age ≥ 60 with change in bowel habit or iron deficiency anaemia; palpable rectal or right-sided abdominal mass; FIT ≥ 10 µg Hb/g faeces in symptomatic patients |
| **Upper GI** | Dysphagia at any age; age ≥ 55 with weight loss and upper abdominal pain, reflux or dyspepsia; haematemesis; treatment-resistant dyspepsia |
| **Pancreatic** | Age ≥ 40 with jaundice (urgent imaging); age ≥ 60 with weight loss plus diarrhoea, back pain, abdominal pain, nausea, vomiting, constipation or new-onset diabetes |
| **Breast** | Age ≥ 30 with unexplained breast lump with or without pain; age ≥ 50 with nipple discharge, retraction or changes; skin changes suggestive of cancer; axillary lump |
| **Gynaecological** | Postmenopausal bleeding (≥ 1 year after periods stopped); persistent bloating/early satiety/pelvic pain in women ≥ 50 (CA-125 first, then ultrasound); vulval lump, ulcer or bleeding |
| **Urological** | Visible haematuria (age ≥ 45); non-visible haematuria with dysuria/raised WCC (≥ 60); abnormal prostate feel on DRE or raised age-specific PSA with symptoms; testicular non-painful swelling or mass |
| **Skin** | Pigmented lesion with a weighted 7-point checklist score ≥ 3; suspected SCC (nodule, ulcer, scaly) |
| **Haematological** | Unexplained lymphadenopathy > 6 weeks with B symptoms; splenomegaly; persistent unexplained fever/night sweats/weight loss; FBC with blasts (same-day); unexplained bone pain with hypercalcaemia/anaemia/renal impairment (myeloma: protein electrophoresis, free light chains) |
| **Brain/CNS** | Progressive subacute loss of neurological function, new-onset seizures in adults, persistent headache with neurological signs |
| **Head and neck** | Unexplained neck lump, persistent hoarseness > 3 weeks (age ≥ 45), unexplained mouth ulceration > 3 weeks, unilateral nasal obstruction with discharge, oral red/white patch |
| **Thyroid** | Unexplained thyroid lump |
| **Sarcoma** | Soft-tissue lump > 5 cm, growing, deep, painful; bone pain/swelling |
| **Childhood** | Persistent headache with neurological signs, bone pain, leucocoria, unexplained mass, lymphadenopathy, petechiae with cytopenias, prolonged fever with weight loss (use specialist paediatric guidance) |

### 3.3 Spinal cord compression, SVC obstruction and hypercalcaemia in cancer

Oncological emergencies are recognised and treated the same day (chapter 63). **Suspected metastatic spinal cord compression**: immediate MRI whole spine and oncology contact. **Neutropenic sepsis**: antibiotics within 60 minutes (chapter 62).

## 4. Mental health, safeguarding and capacity

### 4.1 Mental health red flags

| Red flag | Response |
|---|---|
| Active suicidal ideation with plan, intent or means; recent attempt; hopelessness after a loss | Do not leave alone; remove means; emergency mental health assessment (liaison psychiatry/crisis team); consider Mental Health Act if lacking capacity and high risk |
| Psychosis: command hallucinations, paranoid delusions with risk to others | Urgent psychiatric assessment; risk assessment; mental health legislation |
| Mania with reduced need for sleep, risk behaviour | Urgent assessment |
| Catatonia, neuroleptic malignant syndrome (rigidity, fever, autonomic instability), serotonin syndrome (clonus, hyperthermia) | Emergency medical admission |
| Eating disorder with BMI < 15, bradycardia, electrolyte disturbance, syncope | Admission/medical stabilisation |
| Withdrawal: delirium tremens, seizures | Admit; benzodiazepines, thiamine |
| New psychiatric symptoms after 40 years, abrupt onset, visual hallucinations, cognitive deficits | Organic cause (delirium, tumour, encephalitis, dementia with Lewy bodies, substances) |
| Perinatal: thoughts of harming the baby or self, psychosis | Same-day specialist perinatal mental health |
| Self-harm | Assess risk, treat injuries, psychosocial assessment, not "attention seeking" |

Ask directly about suicide: it does not increase risk and it opens the conversation.

### 4.2 Safeguarding

- **Children**: injuries inconsistent with explanation or developmental stage; bruising in non-mobile infants; patterned injuries; delay in seeking care; repeated presentations; poor attachment; neglect; sexual abuse indicators; fabricated/induced illness; disclosure. Follow local safeguarding procedures: discuss with the named safeguarding lead, document verbatim, refer to children's social care, share information when child safety is at stake, even without parental consent.
- **Adults at risk**: unexplained injuries, pressure ulcers, malnutrition, financial exploitation, restricted access, fear of the carer, controlling behaviour. Duty to report as per local law.
- **Domestic violence**: injuries (face, neck, abdomen), repeated "accidents", pregnancy-related presentations, controlling partner; ask privately; safety plan; confidentiality; referral to specialist services; risk assessment (DASH).
- **Female genital mutilation, forced marriage, modern slavery/trafficking**: know mandatory reporting duties in your jurisdiction.
- **Radicalisation, self-neglect, homelessness**.

### 4.3 Capacity and consent red flags

When a patient refuses urgent assessment or treatment (self-discharge, refusing admission): assess capacity (understand, retain, weigh, communicate); consider delirium, intoxication, psychosis, hypoglycaemia; if lacking capacity act in best interests; if capacity is present respect refusal after full information, document, offer alternatives and safety net.

## 5. Choosing the route and level of referral

### 5.1 Questions to ask

1. **How dangerous is the delay?** (minutes, hours, days, weeks)
2. **What is the question** (diagnosis, treatment, opinion, procedure, admission)?
3. **Who is best placed to answer it** (ED, specialty on-call, rapid-access clinic, community service)?
4. **What can I do while arranging it** (analgesia, antibiotics, oxygen, anticoagulation, tests)?
5. **What does the patient want, and can they get there safely** (transport, escort, carers, language)?

### 5.2 Routes

| Route | Use for |
|---|---|
| **999/112 ambulance; ED resus** | Immediate threat to life |
| **Direct admission to specialty/assessment unit (after telephone discussion)** | Stable but needs same-day specialist care (cellulitis needing IV antibiotics, DVT pending scan, early pregnancy complications, acute psychiatric crisis) |
| **Ambulatory care / same-day emergency care (SDEC)** | Pulmonary embolism low-risk, cellulitis, minor head injury, renal colic |
| **Rapid-access clinics** | TIA, chest pain, first seizure, haematuria, breast, early pregnancy, DVT, cancer |
| **Suspected-cancer pathway (e.g., 2-week wait/fast-track)** | Symptoms meeting criteria |
| **Direct-access tests** | Chest X-ray, ultrasound, FIT, endoscopy, CT |
| **Telephone advice / e-consult** | Specialist guidance without a clinic visit |
| **Routine outpatient** | Stable, non-urgent |
| **Community services / social care / mental health crisis team** | Functional, psychosocial |

### 5.3 Referral content: the SBAR

- **S**ituation: who you are, the patient, the reason and urgency ("I'd like to refer Mrs X, 72, with suspected cauda equina, for emergency MRI").
- **B**ackground: relevant history, comorbidity, medication (anticoagulants!), allergies, baseline function, social circumstances, capacity.
- **A**ssessment: findings (vital signs, key examination, results, scores), your impression and differential.
- **R**ecommendation / request: what you want (admit, see within 2 weeks, advise on management), what you have done, what is pending, the patient's wishes and understanding, contact details.

Add the **safety net** you gave and who is responsible for follow-up. Avoid vague ("please see and advise") requests. Record the time and name of the person accepted the referral.

### 5.4 When the answer is "no, not needed"

If a specialist declines because criteria are not met, ask what additional information or tests would change that; document the discussion; ensure a safety net and plan; escalate respectfully if clinical concern persists (senior-to-senior).

### 5.5 Transport and handover

Unstable patients travel with a trained escort, monitoring and equipment; give written handover; confirm receipt by the receiving team; check that the patient, family and carers know where to go. Do not delay emergency transfer for tests.

## 6. Avoiding under- and over-referral

| Under-referral (missed or delayed diagnosis) | Over-referral (waste, harm) |
|---|---|
| Rare dangerous conditions with subtle red flags; false reassurance from a normal test; "wait and see" without a safety net; unclear ownership of follow-up; pressure to avoid admissions | Referring every red flag as an emergency; unnecessary imaging and specialist visits; patient anxiety; cascade of incidental findings; consuming scarce capacity |

Tools: use clinical rules (chapter 70), explicit **thresholds**, **second opinions**, **decision support**, **audit of missed cancers/delayed diagnoses**, shared decision-making (chapter 69), and **safety-netting** (chapter 73). Reflect on cases where the red flag was absent (false negatives) and present (false positives).

## Worked cases

### Case 1: Back pain with a missing symptom

**Presentation.** Mr A., 56, 3 days of low back pain, now with difficulty urinating (needs to strain), reduced sensation when wiping, and a "numb bottom" he did not mention until asked; mild bilateral leg weakness.

**Reasoning.** Retention of urine, saddle sensory disturbance and bilateral leg symptoms are red flags for cauda equina syndrome. Treat as an emergency irrespective of pain severity; delays beyond 24–48 hours worsen sphincter outcomes.

**Action.** Examine perianal sensation, anal tone (by trained examiner), bladder scan (post-void residual); arrange emergency MRI lumbosacral spine and contact neurosurgery/orthopaedics; send by ambulance to the receiving centre; document times; do not wait for outpatient MRI.

**Lessons.** Ask about bladder, bowel and saddle sensation in every back pain consultation; phrase questions plainly.

### Case 2: The headache that was not a migraine

**Presentation.** Mrs B., 68, 3 weeks of right temporal headache, jaw pain on chewing, scalp tenderness when brushing hair, fatigue, brief blurring of vision in the right eye yesterday. ESR 88, CRP 65.

**Reasoning.** Giant cell arteritis until proven otherwise: risk of permanent visual loss (and stroke) within days. Treatment must not wait for biopsy.

**Action.** Start prednisolone 40–60 mg daily (higher/IV methylprednisolone if visual symptoms), same-day or next-day specialist assessment (rheumatology/ophthalmology), temporal artery ultrasound or biopsy within 1–2 weeks, bone and gastric protection, monitor glucose and BP; counsel about visual symptoms (return immediately).

**Lessons.** Recognise the combination (age, new headache, jaw claudication, visual symptoms, high ESR); treat first.

### Case 3: Progressive hoarseness in a smoker

**Presentation.** Mr C., 62, 40 pack-year smoker, hoarse voice for 5 weeks, no sore throat, no fever, mild weight loss.

**Reasoning.** Persistent hoarseness > 3 weeks in a smoker aged ≥ 45 meets suspected-cancer criteria (laryngeal or lung cancer with recurrent laryngeal nerve involvement). He is not an emergency but must not be managed with repeated antibiotics.

**Action.** CXR (same-week), suspected-cancer referral to ENT (laryngoscopy within 2 weeks), consider CT chest; smoking cessation support; safety-net for stridor, haemoptysis, dysphagia; document the urgency and give written information; follow up to confirm the appointment occurred.

**Lessons.** Persistence plus risk factors triggers referral; track the referral outcome.

### Case 4: A child with a fever and a rash

**Presentation.** A 2-year-old, 6 hours fever, vomiting, cold hands and feet, drowsy; parents report "a few spots", which do not fade with the glass test; HR 170, RR 40, capillary refill 4 seconds.

**Reasoning.** Non-blanching rash with fever and signs of poor perfusion: meningococcal septicaemia until proven otherwise. This is an emergency.

**Action.** Call for ambulance; give benzylpenicillin IM/IV or ceftriaxone immediately if available (do not delay for transport); high-flow oxygen; IV/IO access; fluid bolus 10–20 mL/kg (reassess); pre-alert the ED/PICU; notify public health; chemoprophylaxis for contacts.

**Lessons.** Antibiotics at the earliest point of recognition; communication and escalation save lives.

### Case 5: Weight loss and "non-specific" symptoms

**Presentation.** Mrs D., 64, 4 months fatigue, 6 kg weight loss, vague abdominal discomfort, normal examination, normal FBC, CRP 18, ferritin 40, LFT normal, FIT negative, CXR normal.

**Reasoning.** Unintentional weight loss with fatigue and abdominal symptoms has a PPV above the 3% threshold for cancer when combined (age ≥ 60). Negative first-line tests do not remove concern; pancreatic, ovarian, gastric, lymphoma, lung and renal cancers remain possible, as do non-malignant causes (depression, thyroid, coeliac, TB).

**Action.** Direct-access CT chest/abdomen/pelvis or referral to a non-specific symptom diagnostic pathway; TSH, HbA1c, calcium, coeliac serology, HIV, CA-125 (women with abdominal symptoms); review medications and depression; arrange follow-up in 2–4 weeks; safety-net.

**Lessons.** A normal first-line panel should not end the work-up when risk is high and symptoms persist.

### Case 6: Capacity, safeguarding and a bruise

**Presentation.** Ms E., 81, mild dementia, brought by her son with a bruise on her arm and weight loss; she says she is "fine" but flinches when he speaks; he answers questions for her; pressure sore on heel.

**Reasoning.** Combination of unexplained injury, weight loss, pressure sore and controlling behaviour raises a safeguarding concern (neglect or abuse of an adult at risk). The patient may have capacity for some decisions; assess it for specific questions.

**Action.** Interview her alone; examine and document injuries (body map, photographs with consent); medical review for causes of weight loss; capacity assessment for disclosure and accommodation decisions; discuss with the safeguarding lead; refer to adult social care per policy (with her consent where possible; without consent if capacity lacking, others at risk, or crime suspected); plan safe discharge.

**Lessons.** Patterns and context trigger action; separate interviews; document objectively; follow local safeguarding policy.

### Case 7: Over-referral and the fear of missing something

**Presentation.** Mr F., 29, 2 days of mild chest wall pain after gym, reproducible on palpation, normal vitals and ECG, no risk factors. He is anxious and asks for "a full cardiac work-up and an MRI".

**Reasoning.** Probability of serious pathology is very low; red flags are absent. Excess testing would cause false positives, anxiety and cascade.

**Action.** Explain the reasoning; offer reassurance with specific reasons; analgesia/heat; advise on features that would require review (exertional chest pain, breathlessness, syncope, fever, calf swelling); document discussion and shared decision; no referral.

**Lessons.** Careful assessment and explanation are often the most effective, safest intervention; safety-net rather than over-test.

---

## Common pitfalls

1. **Not asking** about red flags (bladder/saddle symptoms, visual change, drug use, weight loss).
2. **Treating red flags as diagnoses**, or absence as exclusion.
3. **Delaying treatment for referral processes** (antibiotics in meningococcal disease, steroids in GCA, anticoagulation in suspected PE).
4. **Wrong urgency or route** (routine letters for urgent problems; ED for what could be fast-tracked).
5. **Poor referral content**: missing medications, urgency, question or patient's wishes.
6. **No safety net and no ownership** of follow-up or pending results.
7. **Anchoring on a normal test** and missing persistent symptoms.
8. **Ignoring parental or carer concern** ("something is not right").
9. **Not considering capacity or safeguarding** in vulnerable patients.
10. **Fear-driven over-referral and over-testing**.
11. **Failure to adjust for age, immunosuppression or pregnancy.**
12. **Not documenting reasoning, patient information, or discussions with specialists.**

---

## Key points

- Red flags increase probability but have low individual predictive value; combinations, persistence and context matter.
- Match urgency to danger of delay: emergency, urgent, soon, routine; use the correct route.
- Treat time-critical conditions on suspicion before referral completes (meningococcal disease, GCA, sepsis, ACS, cauda equina pathway).
- Suspected-cancer referral relies on symptom combinations, age, risk factors and persistence; a normal first test does not close the case.
- Explicitly assess suicide risk, psychosis, safeguarding and capacity.
- Make referrals clear using SBAR, with question, urgency, actions taken and the patient's wishes; confirm receipt.
- Avoid both under- and over-referral by using rules, thresholds, safety-netting and shared decisions.

**Questions**

7. List four elements of a good referral (SBAR).
8. A 22-year-old woman has lower abdominal pain, vaginal spotting and a positive pregnancy test. What is the concern and the route?
9. Give three safeguarding red flags in a child presenting with an injury.
10. How can you avoid over-referral without missing disease?

**Answers**

1. Most patients with a red flag have benign disease because the flag is also common in the benign population; combined with low prevalence of serious disease this gives a low PPV. Absence reduces but does not exclude serious disease (early, atypical presentations; older, immunosuppressed, children).
2. Saddle anaesthesia/paraesthesia, urinary retention or incontinence, faecal incontinence, bilateral leg weakness or sciatica, sexual dysfunction, reduced anal tone: emergency MRI and neurosurgical/spinal referral the same day.
3. Meningococcal septicaemia suspected: give IM/IV benzylpenicillin or ceftriaxone immediately, high-flow oxygen, IV/IO access, fluids, call 999/pre-alert, PICU, public health notification.
4. Age > 50, new temporal headache, jaw claudication, scalp tenderness, visual symptoms, polymyalgia, raised ESR/CRP; start prednisolone 40–60 mg (higher with visual loss) immediately and arrange urgent specialist and biopsy/ultrasound.
5. Guidance recommends urgent investigation/referral when a symptom or symptom combination has a ≥ 3% chance of cancer (PPV), supporting earlier diagnosis, direct-access tests and non-specific symptom pathways.
6. A patient with capacity may refuse after being fully informed; only when they lack capacity (and treatment is in their best interests or legally mandated, such as mental health legislation) may you act without consent; always assess capacity decision-specifically and document.
7. Situation (who/what/urgency), Background (history, medication, comorbidity, function, social), Assessment (findings, results, scores, impression), Recommendation (what you want, what you have done, patient's wishes, contact) plus safety net/follow-up.
8. Ectopic pregnancy (until proven otherwise); same-day early pregnancy unit/ED assessment (emergency if unstable): β-hCG, transvaginal ultrasound, group and save, anti-D if indicated.
9. Injury inconsistent with the explanation or developmental stage, bruising in a non-mobile infant, delay in seeking care, repeated injuries or presentations, patterned injuries, inappropriate parental behaviour or fabricated illness, disclosure.
10. Use validated rules and thresholds, assess and document pre-test probability, explain the reasoning to patients, safety-net with explicit return criteria, use e-consult/telephone advice, and audit outcomes.
