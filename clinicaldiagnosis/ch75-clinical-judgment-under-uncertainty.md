# Chapter 75. Clinical Judgment Under Uncertainty

## Learning objectives

After studying this chapter you should be able to:

1. Distinguish the sources and types of uncertainty in medicine: biological variability, incomplete information, test error, unknown prognosis, and uncertainty about patient values.
2. Describe dual-process reasoning (intuitive and analytic), explain the role of heuristics, and name the common cognitive biases that cause diagnostic error, with a practical countermeasure for each.
3. Calibrate probability estimates, use base rates, and avoid overconfidence and underconfidence.
4. Apply decision-analytic thinking: weigh the probabilities and consequences (utilities) of actions, set thresholds, and consider regret.
5. Use sequential, hypothesis-driven strategies: working diagnosis, "worst-case" and "most-likely" lists, diagnostic time-outs, and trial-of-treatment or observation with safety nets.
6. Recognise situations that increase error risk (fatigue, interruptions, handover, rare conditions, atypical presentations, diagnostic momentum) and apply system-level defences.
7. Communicate uncertainty honestly to patients and colleagues, and make shared decisions when evidence is limited or values differ.
8. Develop reflective habits: feedback, follow-up of outcomes, calibration practice, and learning from errors and near misses.
9. Know when to ask for help, escalate, or say "I don't know."

## 1. The nature of uncertainty

### 1.1 Uncertainty is a feature, not a failure

Medical decisions are made with incomplete information and limited time. Even a perfect clinician cannot reach certainty in many encounters: early disease looks like normal variation, tests have false positives and negatives, and prognosis is probabilistic. The goal is **not certainty but calibrated, proportionate action**: to know what you know, what you do not know, what could hurt the patient, and what to do next.

### 1.2 Types of uncertainty

| Type | Source | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Diagnostic** | Overlapping presentations, incomplete data | Fever and headache: viral illness or early meningitis? |
| **Test-related** | Imperfect sensitivity/specificity, pre-analytical error | Negative rapid antigen test in high-probability influenza |
| **Prognostic** | Variable natural history | Will this lung nodule grow? |
| **Therapeutic** | Uncertain benefit/harm for this patient | Anticoagulation in an 88-year-old with falls |
| **Epistemic (evidence gaps)** | Lack of data for the patient's group (older, multimorbid, pregnant) | Trial exclusion criteria |
| **Personal/values** | What matters to the patient | Quality vs length of life |
| **Systems** | Resources, access, continuity | Delayed scan, lost results |

### 1.3 Aleatory vs epistemic

*Aleatory* uncertainty (irreducible randomness: who among 100 low-risk patients will have an event) vs *epistemic* uncertainty (reducible with more information or time). Good judgment asks: **can additional data reduce my uncertainty enough to change the decision?** If not, stop testing and act (or observe).

## 2. How clinicians think

### 2.1 Dual-process model

| System 1 (intuitive) | System 2 (analytic) |
|---|---|
| Fast, automatic, pattern-based | Slow, deliberate, effortful |
| Draws on illness scripts and experience | Uses rules, probabilities, differential lists |
| Efficient, usually right in familiar contexts | Needed for atypical, high-stakes, or novel cases |
| Susceptible to bias and fatigue | Susceptible to overload and time pressure |

Experts rely heavily on System 1 but know when to **switch to System 2** ("cognitive forcing"): when the presentation is unusual, the patient fails to respond as expected, the stakes are high, or a discrepancy exists. Good diagnosticians combine both: intuition generates hypotheses; analysis tests them.

### 2.2 Heuristics and biases

Heuristics are mental shortcuts that usually work and sometimes fail.

| Bias | Description | Countermeasure |
|---|---|---|
| **Anchoring** | Locking onto the initial impression | Generate at least three alternatives; ask "what else?" |
| **Premature closure** | Stopping the search when one diagnosis fits | Ask "does it explain all findings?"; list what does not fit |
| **Confirmation bias** | Seeking and interpreting data that support the favoured diagnosis | Look for disconfirming evidence; "what would prove me wrong?" |
| **Availability** | Overweighting recent, vivid or memorable cases | Check base rates; use data |
| **Representativeness** | Judging probability by similarity to the prototype, ignoring prevalence | Include base rates; remember atypical presentations |
| **Base-rate neglect** | Ignoring prevalence | Estimate the pre-test probability explicitly |
| **Diagnostic momentum** | A label gets passed along and gains credibility | Re-examine from scratch; treat inherited diagnoses as hypotheses |
| **Framing effect** | Presentation of information alters decisions ("90% survival" vs "10% mortality") | Reframe both ways; use absolute numbers |
| **Search satisficing** | Stopping after finding the first abnormality | Complete systematic review; check review areas |
| **Overconfidence** | Believing you are more accurate than you are | Seek feedback and calibrate |
| **Triage cueing / blame** | Triage label or patient characteristics shape thinking (frequent attender, psychiatric history) | Evaluate afresh; "even frequent attenders get sick" |
| **Posterior probability error** | Assuming that because the last patient had X, this one will | Judge each case independently |
| **Sunk-cost fallacy** | Persisting with a plan because of prior investment | Re-evaluate at decision points |
| **Omission bias** | Preferring inaction over action with the same expected harm | Compare outcomes explicitly |
| **Commission bias** | Doing something when watchful waiting is better | Consider "first, do no harm" |
| **Affective bias** | Emotional reaction to the patient influences judgment | Name the emotion; seek a second view |
| **Hindsight bias** | After the outcome, believing it was obvious | Judge decisions by information available at the time |
| **Gambler's fallacy** | Expecting a streak to reverse | Probabilities do not "owe" |
| **Yin-yang / diagnosis-by-exclusion over-use** | Believing exhaustive tests prove health | Accept irreducible uncertainty |

### 2.3 Cognitive load and context

Error risk rises with **fatigue, sleep deprivation, multitasking, interruptions, time pressure, emotional strain, crowding, handovers, and unfamiliar settings**. Defences: protected time for complex cases, checklists, second look, structured handover, and cultural permission to ask for help.

### 2.4 Metacognition

Thinking about your thinking: "How sure am I? Why? What am I missing? What would change my mind?" Practical triggers: unexplained findings, poor response to therapy, patient or family concern, a gut feeling that something is wrong ("sense of alarm" is predictive of serious illness, especially in children), and the third visit for the same problem.

## 3. Probability and calibration

### 3.1 Using numbers

Verbal terms ("likely", "unlikely") mean different things to different people (from 20% to 70% for "possible"). Use **approximate numerical ranges**, at least privately: < 1% remote; 1–5% unlikely; 5–25% possible; 25–50% reasonable chance; 50–75% likely; > 75% probable; > 95% near-certain.

### 3.2 Calibration

A clinician is **well-calibrated** if, among all cases where they say "70%", about 70% turn out to be true. Most clinicians are overconfident, particularly at high confidence. To calibrate: record predictions for a series of cases (diagnosis probability, outcome), follow up, and compare; review results in groups (30–40%, 60–70%, etc.). Feedback loops (discharge diagnoses, pathology, follow-up calls) are rare in practice and must be created deliberately.

### 3.3 Base rates and the denominator

- Think "out of 100 patients like this one, how many have the disease?"
- Rare diseases remain rare even with a "classic" feature; "common things are common" but **the dangerous, treatable rare ones must be actively considered when the cost of missing is high**.
- Balance two heuristics: *Occam's razor* (one diagnosis explains all) and *Hickam's dictum* ("patients can have as many diseases as they please"). Use Occam for young, previously healthy patients; consider multiple diagnoses in older, multimorbid patients.

### 3.4 Bayesian updating in the head

Start with a prior (base rate adjusted by presentation), update with each finding using approximate likelihood ratios (chapter 69). Avoid "double counting" correlated findings. Use the **worst-case / most-likely / most-treatable** triad: for any symptom, list the most dangerous diagnosis, the most probable, and the one that, if missed, is most easily treated.

## 4. Decision-making under uncertainty

### 4.1 Expected value and utility

A decision has outcomes with probabilities and values (utilities). The better option maximises **expected utility**, which includes patient preferences. Example: starting anticoagulation for a 79-year-old with AF and a CHA₂DS₂-VASc of 4: stroke risk reduction vs bleeding risk and patient's attitudes toward stroke (disabling stroke may be considered worse than death by some).

### 4.2 Thresholds and regret

Decision thresholds (test, treatment) shift with the **cost of errors**: missing a treatable meningitis (high harm) lowers the treatment threshold; unnecessary invasive testing in low-risk patients (harm without benefit) raises the test threshold. **Regret** (anticipated emotional cost of a wrong decision) legitimately influences decisions, but it should be separated from probability: "I'm afraid of regret, not of the disease". Discussing regret openly with patients can help them choose.

### 4.3 Strategies when uncertainty cannot be resolved

1. **Watchful waiting with safety-net** (chapter 73): use time as a test.
2. **Trial of treatment**: e.g., PPI for suspected GERD, oral iron for suspected deficiency; define success and failure criteria and stop if there is no response.
3. **Test-and-treat thresholds**: act on probability when test results would not change management.
4. **Hedging with low-harm, high-benefit measures** (aspirin for suspected stroke before imaging, antibiotics in suspected sepsis).
5. **Reversible vs irreversible decisions**: prefer reversible options when uncertain; delay irreversible ones (surgery, withdrawal of life-sustaining treatment) until information improves, unless time-critical.
6. **Second opinion and group decisions** (MDT).
7. **Explicit uncertainty in the plan**: "If X happens, we will do Y."

### 4.4 Diagnostic time-outs and cognitive forcing strategies

At pre-defined points (before discharge, before invasive procedures, when the patient is not responding, at the third visit), pause and ask:

- What is my working diagnosis and my confidence (0–100%)?
- What else could it be? What is the worst thing it could be?
- What findings do not fit?
- What tests or observations would change my mind?
- What is the safety net?

Checklists such as the **DEFT-style or "differential diagnosis checklist"** (e.g., "VINDICATE": Vascular, Infectious, Neoplastic, Degenerative/Drugs, Iatrogenic, Congenital, Autoimmune, Trauma, Endocrine/Metabolic) can reduce omissions; use them after generating your own list, not instead of it.

### 4.5 Diagnostic reasoning styles

- **Hypothetico-deductive**: generate hypotheses early, test them. Efficient; risk of anchoring.
- **Pattern recognition**: instant recognition (rash of shingles). Rapid but risky for atypical cases.
- **Exhaustive (Method of Sherlock)**: systematic collection before conclusion. Useful in novel cases but inefficient.
- **Algorithmic / rule-based**: structured decision rules (chapters 70–71).
- **Probabilistic/Bayesian**: updating probabilities.
- **Causal reasoning**: reasoning from pathophysiology; powerful for atypical cases.
- **Diagnostic by treatment response**: valid but beware of placebo, natural history, and regression to the mean.

Skilled clinicians flexibly combine these.

## 5. Situations of high uncertainty

### 5.1 Undifferentiated symptoms

Fatigue, dizziness, chest discomfort, abdominal pain, headache and "not right": the majority are benign or self-limited. Approach: elicit the patient's concerns and context, rule out red flags, make a provisional working diagnosis (including "symptom without disease"), offer reassurance with specifics, plan follow-up, and avoid over-investigation. Retain curiosity: persistent or evolving symptoms require re-evaluation (chapter 73).

### 5.2 Medically unexplained or functional symptoms

Symptoms without a clear organic cause are common (up to 20–30% of primary-care visits). Avoid dismissal and avoid repeated tests that add little. Strategy: positive diagnosis where possible (functional neurological disorder, IBS, fibromyalgia), acknowledge the reality of symptoms, explain mechanisms (nervous system sensitisation), agree a limited plan, treat comorbid anxiety/depression, set regular review, avoid iatrogenic harm; remain alert to evolving organic disease.

### 5.3 Rare disease and diagnostic odyssey

When multiple specialists fail, step back: re-take history and reconsider the basics (medications, toxins, infections, family history, travel); look for a unifying diagnosis (the "zebra" is rare, but the problem is real); use specialist networks and genetic testing when appropriate; avoid labelling as psychogenic prematurely; support the patient through uncertainty; coordinate care.

### 5.4 The elderly and multimorbid patient

Atypical presentations, multiple causes, polypharmacy, and unclear goals. Use geriatric assessment; focus on treatable contributors and on what matters to the patient; accept incomplete diagnostic clarity when further tests will not change management; consider time-to-benefit for preventive therapies.

### 5.5 Critical illness

Decisions must be made in minutes. Use "treat-and-diagnose" in parallel; reassess frequently; avoid anchoring on the first sepsis source; seek senior and multidisciplinary input; define goals-of-care early.

### 5.6 Prognostic uncertainty at the end of life

Offer ranges, use time-limited trials, revisit estimates (chapter 63). Be honest about limits.

### 5.7 Pandemics and novel conditions

Evidence evolves quickly; rely on principles (infection control, supportive care, trial participation), update with reputable sources, communicate openly about changing knowledge.

## 6. Communicating uncertainty

### 6.1 To patients

- Use plain language and **natural frequencies**: "Out of 100 people like you, about 5 have this problem."
- Distinguish **what we know, what we think, what we do not know, and what we will do next**.
- Avoid both false reassurance and over-alarming. A good phrase: "I think this is most likely X. I can't be 100% sure, and here is what would make me worry."
- Explore **how much uncertainty the patient can tolerate** and what they fear. Some patients need more information; others, less.
- Use **teach-back** and written notes; agree on the follow-up plan (chapter 73).
- Acknowledge emotion: "It's hard not having a clear answer."
- Share **decision aids** for preference-sensitive choices.

### 6.2 To colleagues

State confidence, assumptions and the next decision point: "I'm 70% sure it's pneumonia; the main alternative is PE; I've sent a D-dimer; if positive, I'll arrange CTPA." Invite challenge. At handover: "If X, then Y."

### 6.3 Documenting uncertainty

Record the differential, rationale, probability estimate (verbally if not numerically), what was ruled out, what is pending, and the plan. Documentation supports continuity and medico-legal defensibility.

### 6.4 Disclosure of errors

If a diagnostic error occurs, apply the principles of candour: inform the patient promptly, explain facts without speculation, apologise sincerely, outline remedial steps, support the patient and staff, report and learn (chapter 76).

## 7. Learning to be better

### 7.1 Feedback

Without feedback, experience can entrench errors. Strategies: call-backs or chart review on patients discharged; track outcomes for a sample of cases; discuss misses and near-misses; subscribe to a diagnostic audit; peer review; ask the patient: "Did this go as expected?" Use **individual calibration journals**: write predicted probability and outcome.

### 7.2 Deliberate practice

Work through unfolding cases (chapter 74), practise probability estimates, rehearse communicating uncertainty, and study errors (M&M). Learn "diagnostic pivots": findings that should force reconsideration (poor response to antibiotics, new symptom, discrepancies in vital signs).

### 7.3 System approaches to diagnostic safety

- Reliable follow-up of tests and referrals (chapter 73).
- Decision support embedded in workflows (without alert fatigue).
- Standardised handovers; second-checker for high-risk decisions.
- Diagnostic stewardship: appropriate testing, avoiding cascades.
- Safe staffing, protected thinking time, psychological safety to speak up.
- Learning health system: audit, trigger tools (return visits within 72 h, unplanned ICU transfers).

### 7.4 Emotions, stress and resilience

Uncertainty is stressful, and intolerance of uncertainty correlates with over-testing, defensive medicine and burnout. Develop tolerance: normalise uncertainty with peers, share decisions, use debriefs, maintain well-being, and rely on systems (safety nets) to carry some of the burden.

## 8. Practical toolkit

1. **Name the problem** (one-liner) and your confidence.
2. **Write a three-item differential**: most likely, worst case, most treatable.
3. **Ask the four questions**: What else? What doesn't fit? What would change my mind? What's the safety net?
4. **Estimate the pre-test probability**; decide whether a test could cross a threshold.
5. **Choose the reversible, low-harm option** if uncertain.
6. **Set a decision point** (time or event) to reassess.
7. **Communicate uncertainty** clearly; **document** reasoning.
8. **Ask for help** early: senior, specialist, colleague. "I don't know, let me find out" is a professional statement.
9. **Follow up** and learn from outcomes.

## Worked cases

### Case 1: Three visits, one symptom

**Presentation.** Mr A., 48, third visit in 5 weeks for "indigestion": epigastric burning, mild nausea. Previous visits: PPI started, then switched; normal examination and basic bloods. Today he mentions that he gets breathless climbing stairs and that a friend died of "heartburn that was a heart attack".

**Reasoning.** The diagnosis of dyspepsia has been carried forward without being re-examined (diagnostic momentum, anchoring). The third visit triggers a diagnostic time-out: What else could this be? Exertional breathlessness with epigastric symptoms raises angina (anginal equivalent), anaemia from GI bleeding, gastric cancer (age 48, persistent), pancreatic or biliary disease, heart failure. The fear expressed by the patient ("friend died") is a clinical clue and a patient concern.

**Action.** Re-take history for exertional pattern, risk factors, family history; ECG; FBC and ferritin, LFT, lipase, HbA1c, lipids; if exertional symptoms and risk factors → rapid-access chest pain clinic (CT coronary angiography or stress test); if alarm features or persistent symptoms → upper GI endoscopy; document reasoning and safety net. ECG shows T-wave inversion in V4–V6; troponin normal; angiography shows 80% LAD stenosis.

**Lessons.** The "rule of three": repeated visits for the same symptom call for a fresh look. Listen to patient concerns and avoid anchoring.

### Case 2: Probability, regret and the CT scan

**Presentation.** Ms B., 35, mild headache for 2 days following a minor fall at home (no loss of consciousness), no neurological symptoms, normal examination, not anticoagulated. She insists on a CT scan "to be sure", fearing a bleed like her uncle's.

**Reasoning.** By the Canadian CT head rule, she is low risk (probability of clinically important brain injury < 0.5%). The benefit of CT is tiny; harms include radiation (about 2 mSv), possible incidental findings and anxiety. Her stated reason is regret avoidance and fear derived from family experience.

**Action.** Acknowledge fear; explain risk with natural frequencies ("fewer than 1 in 200 people like you have a bleed"); explain the rule, what symptoms would change the plan (vomiting, drowsiness, weakness, worsening headache), arrange someone to stay with her; offer review if worried. She accepts. If she still insisted after informed discussion, a CT is not unreasonable (shared decision) if documented.

**Lessons.** Distinguish probability from regret; shared decisions can lower the pressure to test.

### Case 3: The wrong anchor in sepsis

**Presentation.** Mr C., 69, with fever, hypotension and abdominal discomfort; a rapid chest X-ray shows a small right basal opacity; the team labels "pneumonia" and starts a narrow-spectrum antibiotic. After 12 hours, lactate rises, urine output falls, and the abdomen becomes more tender.

**Reasoning.** Initial anchoring on the CXR. The poor response is a diagnostic pivot. A broader differential: intra-abdominal sepsis (cholangitis, diverticulitis, perforation), urosepsis, endocarditis, necrotising soft-tissue infection.

**Action.** Escalate antimicrobial spectrum, source search (CT abdomen/pelvis with contrast, ultrasound of biliary tree), surgical review, blood cultures; the CT reveals perforated sigmoid diverticulitis with a pelvic abscess; laparotomy and drainage.

**Lessons.** Treat the patient, not the film; poor response to therapy prompts re-evaluation; avoid premature closure.

### Case 4: Honest uncertainty with a patient

**Presentation.** Mrs D., 54, with six months of fatigue, widespread aches, poor sleep. Examination and standard tests, including thyroid, ferritin, vitamin B12, CRP, ANA, CK, coeliac serology, are normal. She says, "Everyone tells me nothing is wrong. I know something is wrong."

**Reasoning.** Persistent symptoms with normal tests produce uncertainty for both clinician and patient. The risk is dismissal, over-testing and iatrogenic harm. The best strategy is a positive working formulation, honest acknowledgment of limits, a limited and agreed plan, and regular review.

**Action.** Validate: "Your symptoms are real and affect your life." Explain: "We have excluded the serious conditions we can test for. Many people with this pattern have a condition in which the nervous system and sleep are involved (often called fibromyalgia or chronic fatigue). I can't promise a single test for it, but we have effective approaches." Plan: sleep, graded activity, treatment of low mood, pain-modulating drugs if appropriate, review in 4–6 weeks with a symptom diary; say clearly what would prompt re-evaluation (weight loss, fever, focal symptoms). She feels heard and engages with the plan.

**Lessons.** Acknowledge limits; combine validation, explanation, and structure; remain open to revision.

### Case 5: Choosing among imperfect options

**Presentation.** Mr E., 82, frail, atrial fibrillation (CHA₂DS₂-VASc 5), two falls in the past year, mild anaemia, lives alone. He asks, "Should I take a blood thinner?" Estimated annual stroke risk without treatment about 7%; major bleeding risk on a DOAC about 3–4% per year; falls with intracranial haemorrhage is a concern.

**Reasoning.** The decision is preference-sensitive. The expected benefit favours anticoagulation (stroke reduction ~60–70% relative), but disabling stroke vs bleeding trade-offs depend on his values. Modifiable bleeding factors should be addressed (anaemia work-up, falls prevention).

**Action.** Use a decision aid; present numbers (per 100 people over a year); explore his priorities (fear of disabling stroke vs fear of bleeding/falls); correct modifiable risks; offer a time-limited trial with review at 3 months; consider left atrial appendage occlusion if long-term anticoagulation is unacceptable; document the shared decision. He chooses anticoagulation with fall-prevention measures.

**Lessons.** Express risks in numbers, elicit values, mitigate modifiable factors, and review.

### Case 6: When to say "I don't know"

**Presentation.** Ms F., 17, daily fevers, rash, joint pains, hepatosplenomegaly over three weeks, hospital day 5, no diagnosis despite broad tests (cultures, serology, imaging, bone marrow normal); ferritin 14,000 µg/L.

**Reasoning.** The team has cycled through infection, malignancy and autoimmune diseases. Persistent fever with very high ferritin raises haemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH) and adult/juvenile-onset Still disease/macrophage activation syndrome. The clinicians acknowledge uncertainty, call a multidisciplinary meeting with haematology, rheumatology and infectious diseases, and re-evaluate with the HScore (cytopenias, triglycerides, fibrinogen, AST, hepatosplenomegaly, ferritin).

**Action.** Honest conversation with the family: "We do not yet have a name for this illness. We have excluded several serious causes. The pattern suggests an over-active immune response; we are testing for specific markers and will start treatment if criteria are met." HLH markers (soluble CD25, NK function) sent; empiric high-dose steroids started after discussion; response confirms hyperinflammation; later diagnosed with systemic juvenile idiopathic arthritis with MAS; treated with IL-1 blockade.

**Lessons.** Admitting uncertainty and gathering expertise accelerate diagnosis; pattern recognition across disciplines helps; treat while diagnosing when risk is high.

## Common pitfalls

1. **Demanding certainty** from tests; escalating investigations without a decision-relevant question.
2. **Anchoring and premature closure**; not revisiting the diagnosis when the course deviates.
3. **Ignoring base rates** and overusing rare diagnoses (or ignoring dangerous rare ones).
4. **Overconfidence**; no feedback or calibration.
5. **Hindsight bias** in reviewing cases.
6. **Failing to discuss uncertainty** with patients; false reassurance.
7. **Defensive medicine** driven by fear of regret or litigation.
8. **Ignoring patient values and tolerance of uncertainty.**
9. **Working alone** on difficult cases; reluctance to ask for help.
10. **Neglecting the system**: lost results, handover failures, fatigue.
11. **Treating functional symptoms as "nothing" or as purely organic**.
12. **Using irreversible actions prematurely** under uncertainty.

---

## Key points

- Uncertainty is intrinsic; the task is calibrated, proportionate action, not certainty.
- Intuition (System 1) generates hypotheses; analysis (System 2) checks them: switch when cases are atypical, high-stakes or not responding.
- Know the main biases (anchoring, premature closure, confirmation, availability, diagnostic momentum, framing) and apply specific countermeasures.
- Estimate probabilities explicitly, calibrate against outcomes, use base rates and the "worst-case/most-likely/most-treatable" triad.
- Use thresholds, reversible options, trials of treatment, watchful waiting with safety nets, diagnostic time-outs and second opinions.
- Communicate uncertainty with natural frequencies, honesty and a plan; document reasoning.
- Create feedback loops, learn from errors, and support system defences.
- It is professional to say "I don't know yet, and here is how we will find out."

**Questions**

2. Describe System 1 and System 2 reasoning and when to switch from one to the other.
3. Name five cognitive biases and a countermeasure for each.
4. What does it mean to be well-calibrated and how can a clinician improve calibration?
5. Explain the "worst-case / most-likely / most-treatable" approach.
6. What is a diagnostic time-out and when would you use it?
7. How do regret and probability differ in decision-making?
8. Give three strategies for managing uncertainty that cannot be resolved by further testing.
9. How can you communicate a 5% risk to a patient?
10. Why is feedback crucial for diagnostic skill?

**Answers**

1. Aleatory: which of 100 low-risk patients will have an event; variability in drug response. Epistemic: not knowing whether the abdominal pain is appendicitis until imaging; lack of trial data for older multimorbid patients.
2. System 1: fast, intuitive, pattern-based; System 2: slow, analytic, rule-based. Switch when the case is atypical or high-stakes, findings don't fit, the patient doesn't respond as expected, or you notice a feeling of unease.
3. Examples: anchoring (generate alternatives), premature closure (check what doesn't fit), confirmation bias (seek disconfirming evidence), availability (use base rates), diagnostic momentum (re-examine afresh), framing (reframe in both directions).
4. Predicted probabilities match observed frequencies (70% predictions come true ~70% of the time). Improve by recording predictions, following outcomes, and reviewing in confidence bands; seek feedback.
5. For each presentation list the most dangerous diagnosis, the most probable, and the most easily treated; ensure that dangerous and treatable conditions are considered even if unlikely.
6. A structured pause at set points (e.g., before discharge, at third visit, when the patient fails to respond) to review the working diagnosis, alternatives, discordant findings and safety net.
7. Probability reflects the likelihood of an outcome; regret is the emotional cost of a wrong decision. Distinguish them; discuss regret openly; avoid letting fear of regret drive decisions without consideration of probabilities and harms.
8. Watchful waiting with safety net, trial of treatment with defined success criteria, choosing reversible and low-harm actions, second opinions/MDT, shared decision-making.
9. "About 5 out of 100 people like you; 95 will not." Provide absolute numbers, context (what else carries similar risk) and visual aids; avoid relative risks alone.
10. Without feedback, clinicians do not learn whether their judgments were right and may become overconfident; feedback loops (follow-up, audit, calibration) correct errors and improve accuracy.
