# Chapter 54. Dermatology

## Learning objectives

After studying this chapter you should be able to:

1. Describe a skin lesion in a standard vocabulary and use distribution and morphology to build a differential diagnosis.
2. Diagnose and manage atopic eczema and the other common dermatitides, and recognise eczema herpeticum.
3. Recognise psoriasis in its common and dangerous forms, and counsel on systemic associations.
4. Distinguish bacterial, viral, fungal and parasitic skin infections, and identify cellulitis mimics and necrotising infection.
5. Recognise acne, rosacea and other common facial eruptions, and the main hair and nail disorders.
6. Identify mild versus severe cutaneous drug reactions, including DRESS, SJS/TEN, and act at once on the latter.
7. Apply the ABCDE rule and "ugly duckling" sign to pigmented lesions, and distinguish basal cell carcinoma, squamous cell carcinoma and melanoma.
8. Use topical corticosteroids safely by potency, site and quantity.
9. Know the skin signs that point to systemic disease and the dermatological emergencies.

> **Note on thresholds and doses.** Drug names, potencies and regimens reflect commonly used guidelines (BAD, AAD, NICE, EADV) at the time of writing. Check your national formulary and local protocols before prescribing.

## 1. Seeing skin: description before diagnosis

Skin diagnosis is primarily visual, and the most common error is naming a diagnosis before describing what is there. Chapter 18 introduced the vocabulary; this chapter assumes it. In summary, describe:

- **Distribution**: localised or generalised; symmetrical or not; flexural or extensor; photo-exposed or covered; dermatomal; acral; follicular.
- **Primary lesion**: macule (< 1 cm flat colour change), patch (> 1 cm), papule (< 1 cm raised), plaque (> 1 cm raised, flat-topped), nodule, vesicle (< 1 cm fluid), bulla (> 1 cm fluid), pustule, wheal, burrow, comedone, purpura (non-blanching), petechiae, telangiectasia.
- **Secondary change**: scale, crust, erosion, ulcer, excoriation, lichenification, fissure, scar, atrophy.
- **Colour**: erythema (blanches), violaceous, hyperpigmentation, hypopigmentation, depigmentation. Erythema is harder to see on dark skin; look for violaceous or hyperpigmented change, warmth and texture.
- **Configuration**: linear, annular, arcuate, grouped (herpetiform), reticulate, target, serpiginous.
- **Surface and palpation**: warm, tender, indurated, fluctuant, scaly, smooth, umbilicated.
- **Symptoms**: itch, pain, burning, numbness.
- **Time course**: hours (urticaria), days (viral exanthems, drug rash), weeks (eczema, psoriasis), years (BCC).

Ask about: onset and evolution, itch versus pain, triggers (new drug, plant, cosmetic, food, sun), atopy, family history, occupation, contacts with similar rash, travel, sexual history, and treatments tried (including over-the-counter steroid or antifungal creams, which change morphology).

Always **examine the whole skin** with good light, including scalp, nails, mucosae, genitalia, and the web spaces. A patient who complains of a single lesion may have a diagnostic clue elsewhere (pits in psoriatic nails, burrows in the finger webs, a pigmented lesion on the back).

> **Skin of colour.** Textbook images over-represent pale skin. In darker skin, inflammation often appears violaceous, brown or grey rather than red; post-inflammatory pigment change can be the patient's main concern and may last longer than the rash itself; keloid scarring is more common; certain conditions (lichen planus, sarcoidosis, discoid lupus, pityriasis rosea) can look different. Melanoma in people with darker skin more often arises on acral sites (palms, soles, nail bed) and mucosae and is therefore diagnosed late. Seek images from diverse atlases and use palpation and dermoscopy.

## 2. Eczema and dermatitis

### 2.1 Atopic eczema

**Epidemiology and pathogenesis.** Affects 15–20% of children and 2–10% of adults in high-income countries. A combination of skin-barrier dysfunction (filaggrin mutations), type 2 immune activation, and environmental triggers. Associated with asthma, allergic rhinitis and food allergy ("atopic march").

**Diagnosis.** Itchy skin plus three or more of: history of flexural involvement (or cheeks and extensor surfaces in infants), personal or family history of atopy, generalised dry skin in the past year, onset below age 2 (only used for age > 4 years), visible flexural eczema. Typical distribution: face and extensors in infants; antecubital and popliteal fossae, neck, wrists and ankles in children; hands, eyelids, flexures and neck in adults. Acute: erythema, papules, vesicles, weeping, crusting. Chronic: dry, thickened, lichenified, excoriated skin.

**Management: the ladder.**

1. **Emollients**, used liberally and often (at least twice daily, in quantity: 250–500 g per week for a child, more for an adult), as soap substitute, and applied after bathing. Choose by preference; ointments for very dry skin, creams for weeping or hot weather.
2. **Topical corticosteroids** for flares, applied once or twice daily to affected areas until clear (see section 12 on potency). Mild potency on the face, moderate on the body in children, potent in thick chronic plaques in adults. "Weekend" proactive application of a moderate steroid to frequent-flare sites reduces relapses.
3. **Topical calcineurin inhibitors** (tacrolimus, pimecrolimus) for the face, eyelids, flexures and steroid-sparing maintenance.
4. **Bandaging and wet wraps** for severe or refractory disease in children.
5. **Treat infection**: flucloxacillin (or local equivalent) for impetiginised eczema; consider *Staphylococcus aureus* when eczema suddenly worsens, weeps or crusts.
6. **Phototherapy** (narrowband UVB) and **systemic agents** (ciclosporin, methotrexate, azathioprine, short courses of oral steroids only exceptionally) for severe disease.
7. **Biologics and small-molecule agents**: dupilumab and tralokinumab (IL-4/IL-13 or IL-13 targeted), JAK inhibitors (upadacitinib, abrocitinib, baricitinib) for moderate-to-severe disease, with the usual JAK safety checks (cardiovascular risk, thrombosis risk, malignancy, infection, lipid and blood count monitoring).

Education counts as much as the cream: itch–scratch cycle, avoid irritants (soap, bubble bath, biological detergents), keep nails short, cotton clothing, bathing in lukewarm water. Do not routinely recommend food elimination without a clear history of immediate reaction.

**Complications.**

- **Bacterial superinfection** (weeping, crusting, pustules).
- **Eczema herpeticum**: dermatological emergency. Sudden onset of punched-out monomorphic vesicles or erosions on eczematous skin, fever, malaise, pain more than itch. Start **aciclovir immediately** (oral 400 mg five times daily or IV in the unwell, children, or periocular involvement) without waiting for swab results; ophthalmology review if near the eye.
- Sleep loss and psychosocial burden; ask about both.

### 2.2 Other eczemas

| Type | Features | Key management |
|---|---|---|
| **Allergic contact dermatitis** | Delayed (24–72 h) type IV reaction; sharply demarcated, may take the shape of the contact (watch strap, earrings, shoes, cosmetics, plants); nickel, fragrance, preservatives, rubber, hair dye, topical neomycin | Identify and avoid; patch testing; topical steroid |
| **Irritant contact dermatitis** | Hands in wet work (nurses, hairdressers, caterers); dryness, fissures, burning | Protection, emollients, glove advice, steroid |
| **Seborrhoeic dermatitis** | Scalp scaling, eyebrows, nasolabial folds, central chest; yeast (*Malassezia*) related; common in Parkinson disease and HIV | Ketoconazole shampoo/cream, mild steroid |
| **Discoid (nummular) eczema** | Coin-shaped, itchy, weeping plaques on limbs | Potent steroid, emollients, treat infection |
| **Asteatotic eczema** | Older adults, shins, cracked "crazy-paving" dry skin, winter | Emollients, moderate steroid |
| **Stasis (venous) eczema** | Lower leg, haemosiderin staining, varicose veins, oedema | Compression, emollients, steroid; beware of contact sensitisation and ulcer |
| **Dyshidrotic (pompholyx) eczema** | Deep tapioca-like vesicles on palms, soles and sides of fingers; very itchy | Potent steroid, soaks; look for fungal infection and contact allergy |
| **Lichen simplex chronicus** | Localised thickened plaque from habitual rubbing (nape, ankles, genital); itch is paroxysmal | Break itch–scratch cycle; potent steroid under occlusion; treat anxiety |

## 3. Psoriasis

### 3.1 Presentation

A chronic immune-mediated inflammatory disease affecting 2–3% of people, with peaks at ages 20–30 and 50–60, and a strong genetic component (*HLA-C\*06:02*). Triggers: streptococcal throat infection (guttate), skin trauma (Koebner phenomenon), drugs (beta-blockers, lithium, antimalarials, ACE inhibitors, NSAIDs, interferon, TNF inhibitors paradoxically), stress, smoking, alcohol, abrupt cessation of systemic steroids.

**Subtypes.**

- **Chronic plaque**: well-demarcated, erythematous plaques with silvery-white scale, on extensor surfaces (elbows, knees), scalp, lower back, umbilicus, natal cleft. Auspitz sign (pinpoint bleeding on scale removal). Less red in darker skin: plaques appear violaceous or brown with thick grey scale.
- **Guttate**: sudden eruption of small "raindrop" plaques on the trunk 1–3 weeks after streptococcal infection, mostly in children and young adults. Often resolves in weeks to months.
- **Flexural (inverse)**: smooth, shiny, red patches in axillae, groin, inframammary and genital skin; no scale because of moisture. Differentiate from candida and tinea.
- **Scalp**: thick adherent scale extending beyond the hairline.
- **Palmoplantar and pustular**: sterile pustules on palms and soles (palmoplantar pustulosis, strongly smoking related).
- **Nail**: pitting, onycholysis, oil-drop discolouration, subungual hyperkeratosis; present in 50% of psoriasis and up to 80% of psoriatic arthritis.
- **Erythrodermic** and **generalised pustular** (von Zumbusch) psoriasis: emergencies. Widespread erythema > 90% of body surface, with or without pustules, fever, hypothermia or hyperthermia, fluid and protein loss, high-output cardiac failure, infection. Needs admission, fluid and nutritional support, wound care, and systemic therapy. Common triggers: abrupt withdrawal of steroids or other systemic therapy, infection, drugs.

### 3.2 Psoriatic arthritis and comorbidity

Ask every patient with psoriasis about **joint symptoms** (morning stiffness lasting > 30 min, swollen fingers or toes "sausage digits" (dactylitis), heel pain (enthesitis), back stiffness). Psoriatic arthritis occurs in up to 30% and may precede skin disease in 15%. Screen with the PEST or similar questionnaires; refer early to rheumatology as erosive damage occurs in the first two years.

Psoriasis is a systemic inflammatory disease associated with **metabolic syndrome, obesity, cardiovascular disease, depression, inflammatory bowel disease, uveitis, non-alcoholic fatty liver disease, and alcohol misuse**. Assess cardiovascular risk and lifestyle in everybody with moderate-to-severe disease.

### 3.3 Management

1. **Topical therapy for limited disease**: emollients; vitamin D analogues (calcipotriol; calcipotriol–betamethasone combination for 4 weeks followed by maintenance; maximum weekly dose limits to avoid hypercalcaemia); potent corticosteroids for 4 weeks on the body, mild on face and flexures; coal tar, dithranol (specialist), salicylic acid for scale; calcineurin inhibitors for face and flexures; scalp preparations.
2. **Phototherapy**: narrowband UVB two or three times per week; PUVA is used less now.
3. **Conventional systemic therapy** for moderate-to-severe disease (PASI > 10, BSA > 10, or significant impact on life: Dermatology Life Quality Index > 10, or involvement of face, hands, feet, genitalia, nails): methotrexate (weekly, with folic acid; check FBC, LFTs, creatinine; avoid with alcohol excess and in pregnancy), ciclosporin (short term, blood pressure and renal monitoring), acitretin (teratogenic; contraception for women of child-bearing potential for 3 years after), apremilast (PDE4 inhibitor), fumarates.
4. **Biologics**: TNF inhibitors (adalimumab, etanercept, infliximab, certolizumab), IL-17 inhibitors (secukinumab, ixekizumab, bimekizumab), IL-23 inhibitors (guselkumab, risankizumab, tildrakizumab), IL-12/23 inhibitor (ustekinumab). Screen for TB, hepatitis B/C and HIV before starting; check vaccines; note caution with IL-17 inhibitors in inflammatory bowel disease and TNF inhibitors in demyelinating disease or heart failure. Oral TYK2 inhibitors are now also available.

Do not use oral systemic steroids for psoriasis: stopping them can precipitate pustular or erythrodermic flare.

## 4. Skin infections and infestations

### 4.1 Bacterial

**Impetigo.** Honey-coloured crusts around nose and mouth, in children; bullous impetigo (*Staphylococcus aureus* exfoliative toxin) has flaccid blisters. Localised: topical antibiotics (fusidic acid, now increasingly resistance-limited, or mupirocin; or hydrogen peroxide 1% cream where available); widespread or systemically unwell: oral flucloxacillin (or cefalexin; clarithromycin/doxycycline if allergic). Exclude from school until lesions are crusted or 48 h after starting antibiotics. Beware: the infant with widespread peeling and tenderness may have **staphylococcal scalded skin syndrome**; admit.

**Cellulitis and erysipelas.** Acute spreading, hot, red, tender, oedematous skin, usually on the lower leg, with systemic features in more severe cases. Streptococci (Group A) or *S. aureus*. Entry point: tinea pedis, leg ulcer, trauma, eczema. Treatment: oral flucloxacillin 500 mg–1 g four times daily (or cefalexin, doxycycline, clindamycin per allergy and local resistance) for 5–7 days; IV antibiotics for systemic illness (fever > 38.3 °C, tachycardia, hypotension), rapid progression, facial or periorbital involvement, immunosuppression, or failure of oral therapy. Mark the margin, elevate the limb, treat predisposing conditions, compression once acute episode settles. For recurrent cellulitis (≥ 2 episodes a year), consider prophylactic penicillin V.

**Cellulitis mimics** make up 30% of "cellulitis" admissions: **venous stasis dermatitis** (bilateral, itchy, scaly), **lipodermatosclerosis**, **acute gout or septic arthritis**, **deep vein thrombosis**, **contact dermatitis**, **drug eruption**, **panniculitis (erythema nodosum)**, **lymphoedema**, **superficial thrombophlebitis**. Bilateral "cellulitis" is almost never infection.

**Necrotising fasciitis.** Pain out of proportion to the appearance, rapidly spreading erythema, bullae, dusky or anaesthetic skin, crepitus, systemic toxicity, rising lactate. Mortality is 20–30% even with treatment. **Emergency surgical exploration and debridement within hours, broad-spectrum IV antibiotics (including anaerobic and Gram-positive coverage with toxin suppression, e.g., clindamycin) and resuscitation.** Do not wait for imaging if suspicion is high. The LRINEC score is not sensitive enough to exclude it.

**Abscess and furuncle.** Warm, fluctuant, tender swelling. **Incision and drainage is the treatment**; antibiotics only if surrounding cellulitis, systemic features, immunosuppression, or lesions in a high-risk site. MRSA is common in some regions; consider swabs and decolonisation in recurrent disease.

**Erythema migrans** (early Lyme disease): expanding annular erythema with or without central clearing, at least 5 cm, 3–30 days after a tick bite; doxycycline for 10–14 days. Do not wait for serology (often negative at this stage).

### 4.2 Viral

- **Herpes simplex**: grouped vesicles on erythematous base; primary infection (painful gingivostomatitis in children, genital in adults) and recurrent (cold sores, genital). Aciclovir, valaciclovir or famciclovir; early treatment shortens duration. Look for eczema herpeticum, herpetic whitlow, herpes keratitis, immunocompromised patients and neonatal infection.
- **Varicella-zoster**: chickenpox (centripetal, successive crops of vesicles at different stages) and **herpes zoster** (painful unilateral dermatomal vesicular eruption; prodrome of burning pain). Start antiviral (valaciclovir, aciclovir or famciclovir) within 72 h in those over 50, with ophthalmic involvement (nasociliary branch: tip of nose, "Hutchinson sign"), Ramsay Hunt syndrome (ear vesicles, facial palsy), severe pain, immunosuppression. Post-herpetic neuralgia affects up to 10–20% of older patients (gabapentinoids, tricyclics, topical lidocaine/capsaicin). Recombinant zoster vaccine is recommended for adults ≥ 50 and for immunocompromised adults ≥ 18 in many countries.
- **Molluscum contagiosum**: umbilicated pearly papules, children; resolves spontaneously within 6–18 months; treat if bothersome or in the immunosuppressed (extensive lesions suggest HIV).
- **Warts (HPV)**: common, plantar, plane, genital. Salicylic acid, cryotherapy; most resolve in 2 years in children. Persistent or atypical lesions in the immunosuppressed need biopsy.
- **Hand, foot and mouth disease**: Coxsackie; oral vesicles and acral papulovesicles, fever, in children.
- **Measles, rubella, parvovirus, roseola, others**: covered in chapter 53.

### 4.3 Fungal

- **Tinea corporis** (ringworm): annular scaly plaque with raised, active edge and central clearing. Tinea cruris (groin, spares scrotum), tinea pedis (interdigital maceration or moccasin distribution), tinea capitis (children: scaly patches, hair loss, kerion; **needs oral therapy**), tinea unguium (onychomycosis). Confirm with skin scrapings, nail clippings or hair plucks (microscopy with KOH and culture) before oral therapy. Topical terbinafine or imidazoles for 2–4 weeks for skin disease; oral terbinafine for extensive, nail, or scalp disease (griseofulvin in children for tinea capitis in some countries; terbinafine increasingly used). **Tinea incognito** results from topical steroid use, making the rash less scaly and more diffuse.
- **Candidiasis**: intertrigo (bright red, satellite pustules, flexures), oral thrush, vulvovaginitis, napkin dermatitis. Topical imidazole or nystatin. Persistent candidiasis suggests diabetes or immunosuppression.
- **Pityriasis versicolor**: hypo- or hyperpigmented, finely scaly macules on trunk in hot and humid conditions (*Malassezia*); topical ketoconazole shampoo or selenium sulfide, oral itraconazole for recurrence. Pigment may take months to return.

### 4.4 Infestations

- **Scabies.** Intensely itchy (worse at night), burrows in finger webs, wrists, genitalia, axillae, areolae, with secondary excoriation and nodules on genitalia; family members affected. Diagnosis is clinical; dermoscopy shows "delta-wing" jet sign. Treatment: permethrin 5% cream applied from neck down (head too in infants, older adults and immunocompromised), left on 8–12 h, repeated after 7 days; or oral ivermectin 200 µg/kg, repeated after 1–2 weeks. **Treat all household and close contacts simultaneously, even if asymptomatic**, wash bedding and clothes at 50 °C or above. Itch persists for 2–4 weeks after successful treatment (post-scabetic itch). Crusted (Norwegian) scabies in the immunosuppressed, elderly or institutional patients is highly contagious and requires specialist input.
- **Head lice**: itchy scalp, nits; treat with dimeticone or physical methods, repeated at 7 days; wet-combing.
- **Bed bugs, fleas**: grouped itchy bites (the "breakfast, lunch and dinner" line), environmental treatment.
- **Cutaneous larva migrans**: serpiginous creeping eruption on feet after beach travel in tropical areas; ivermectin or albendazole.

## 5. Acne, rosacea and other inflammatory conditions

### 5.1 Acne vulgaris

Affects most adolescents and persists into adulthood, especially women. Comedones (open/closed), papules, pustules, nodules, cysts; scarring and post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation (especially in darker skin).

Stepwise treatment (grade by severity):

- **Mild**: topical retinoid (adapalene, tretinoin), benzoyl peroxide, azelaic acid. Combination products (e.g., adapalene–benzoyl peroxide) are more effective than monotherapy.
- **Moderate**: add topical antibiotic *with* benzoyl peroxide (never alone, to limit resistance), or an oral antibiotic (lymecycline, doxycycline; usually 3 months maximum, always combined with topical retinoid ± benzoyl peroxide). In women, combined oral contraceptive (cyproterone/ethinylestradiol or drospirenone-based) or spironolactone (monitor potassium; avoid in pregnancy) can help.
- **Severe or scarring, or failure of two courses of antibiotics**: **isotretinoin** (specialist supervised). Teratogenic: pregnancy prevention programme with pregnancy tests, contraception one month before to one month after; monitor lipids and liver function; counsel on dry skin and lips, mood effects (review mood each visit), photosensitivity, and avoidance of tetracyclines concurrently (raised intracranial pressure).

Do not forget the psychological burden; early treatment prevents scarring. Consider polycystic ovary syndrome in women with acne, hirsutism and irregular menstruation.

### 5.2 Rosacea

Central facial erythema, flushing, telangiectasia, papules and pustules (no comedones), ocular involvement (blepharitis, conjunctivitis), and rhinophyma in advanced disease. Triggers: alcohol, hot drinks, spicy food, sun, heat, emotional stress. Treatment: sun protection and trigger avoidance, topical metronidazole, azelaic acid or ivermectin; brimonidine gel for erythema; oral doxycycline (low-dose) or lymecycline for papulopustular; isotretinoin in refractory cases. Beware long-term topical steroids on the face (perioral dermatitis, steroid rosacea).

### 5.3 Urticaria and angioedema

- **Urticaria**: transient (< 24 h), itchy wheals; acute (< 6 weeks) from infection, drugs, foods, contact; chronic (≥ 6 weeks, most commonly spontaneous with autoimmune basis). Treat with second-generation non-sedating antihistamines, up-dosing to four times the licensed dose if needed, then omalizumab for refractory disease. Avoid NSAIDs and opioids if they aggravate. Individual wheals that last more than 24 h, leave bruising, or burn rather than itch suggest **urticarial vasculitis**: biopsy.
- **Angioedema**: swelling of deeper tissues (lips, eyelids, tongue, larynx). If with urticaria, mast cell-mediated; if **without** wheals, think **ACE-inhibitor** angioedema (can occur years after starting) or **hereditary angioedema** (C1 inhibitor deficiency: check C4, C1-inhibitor level and function; unresponsive to antihistamines, adrenaline and steroids; treat with C1-inhibitor concentrate, icatibant or ecallantide).
- **Anaphylaxis** (urticaria/angioedema plus respiratory or circulatory compromise): intramuscular adrenaline 0.5 mg (adult; 0.01 mg/kg in children), see chapter 37 and 40.

### 5.4 Other common conditions

- **Pityriasis rosea**: herald patch followed by oval scaly pink patches in a "Christmas tree" pattern along skin cleavage lines; self-limiting over 6–8 weeks; exclude secondary syphilis in sexually active patients (serology), and drug reactions.
- **Lichen planus**: itchy, flat-topped, violaceous polygonal papules (the "6 Ps": purple, polygonal, pruritic, planar, papules, plaques), Wickham striae, wrist and ankle, oral lacy white lesions, nail ridging; associated with hepatitis C and drugs (thiazides, beta-blockers, gold, antimalarials). Potent steroids; hypertrophic lesions on shins; follow oral/vulvar disease for malignant change.
- **Vitiligo**: depigmented patches, symmetrical, autoimmune; associated thyroid disease, type 1 diabetes, pernicious anaemia, Addison disease. Sun protection, topical steroids or calcineurin inhibitors, narrowband UVB, JAK inhibitor cream; camouflage and psychological support matter.
- **Alopecia areata**: round non-scarring patches with "exclamation mark" hairs; can progress to total scalp or body loss; topical or intralesional steroids, topical immunotherapy, JAK inhibitors (baricitinib, ritlecitinib).
- **Telogen effluvium**: diffuse shedding 2–3 months after a trigger (fever, surgery, childbirth, crash diet, iron deficiency, thyroid disease, drugs); reversible.
- **Androgenetic alopecia**: patterned thinning; minoxidil, finasteride (men), anti-androgens (women).
- **Scarring alopecia** (lichen planopilaris, discoid lupus, frontal fibrosing alopecia, folliculitis decalvans): loss of follicular openings, erythema, scale; needs biopsy and early treatment to preserve hair.
- **Hidradenitis suppurativa**: recurrent painful nodules, abscesses and sinus tracts in axillae, groin, inframammary and perianal areas; strongly associated with smoking and obesity; stage-based treatment (topical clindamycin, tetracycline courses, clindamycin–rifampicin, adalimumab, secukinumab, bimekizumab, surgery). Commonly diagnosed late (average delay 7–10 years): ask about recurrent "boils" in the flexures.
- **Pyoderma gangrenosum**: rapidly expanding painful ulcer with violaceous undermined border; pathergy; associated inflammatory bowel disease, rheumatoid arthritis, haematological disease. **Do not debride** (worsens). Diagnosis of exclusion (biopsy to exclude infection, vasculitis, malignancy); steroids, ciclosporin, biologics.
- **Erythema nodosum**: tender red nodules on shins; causes: streptococcal infection, sarcoidosis, TB, inflammatory bowel disease, drugs (oral contraceptive, sulfonamides), pregnancy, lymphoma, Behçet. Evaluate for the cause; self-resolves over 6 weeks.

## 6. Cutaneous drug reactions

Drug reactions are among the most frequent causes of rash in hospital patients. A rash appearing 4–14 days after a new drug (or within 1–3 days after re-exposure) is a drug eruption until proven otherwise.

### 6.1 Common, usually benign

- **Morbilliform (exanthematous) eruption**: symmetric pink macules and papules starting on trunk, spreading to limbs, itchy, with little systemic disturbance; days 4–14 of exposure; antibiotics (penicillins, sulfonamides), allopurinol, anticonvulsants, NSAIDs. Stop the drug if essential alternatives exist; supportive care with antihistamines and topical steroids; resolves in 1–2 weeks. **Aminopenicillin rash in infectious mononucleosis** is not true allergy and need not preclude future use, but record and consider delabelling after assessment.
- **Urticaria/angioedema**: immediate (minutes to hours); antibiotics, NSAIDs, opioids, contrast. Stop drug; treat; assess for anaphylaxis.
- **Fixed drug eruption**: well-defined round dusky red or violaceous plaque (sometimes a bulla) that recurs at the same site each time the drug is taken; lips and genitalia common; post-inflammatory pigment; culprits: paracetamol, NSAIDs, co-trimoxazole, tetracyclines.
- **Photosensitivity**: exaggerated sunburn on photo-exposed skin; amiodarone, tetracyclines, thiazides, NSAIDs, fluoroquinolones, voriconazole, St John's wort.
- **Lichenoid, bullous pemphigoid-like (DPP-4 inhibitors, loop diuretics), acneiform (steroids, EGFR inhibitors), erythema multiforme, and others.**

### 6.2 Severe cutaneous adverse reactions (SCAR)

These are **emergencies**. Red flags: **mucosal involvement** (mouth, eyes, genitals), **skin pain or tenderness**, **blisters or skin detachment**, **facial oedema**, **fever**, **lymphadenopathy**, **organ involvement**, **purpura**, **marked eosinophilia or abnormal LFTs**.

| Condition | Typical features | Latency | Common culprits |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Stevens–Johnson syndrome / toxic epidermal necrolysis (SJS/TEN)** | Prodrome of fever and malaise; painful dusky or purpuric macules, targetoid lesions, flaccid blisters and sheet-like detachment (Nikolsky sign); erosive mucositis of at least two sites; SJS < 10% BSA detachment, SJS/TEN overlap 10–30%, TEN > 30% | 4–28 days (usually 1–3 weeks) | Allopurinol, carbamazepine, lamotrigine, phenytoin, phenobarbital, sulfonamides (incl. co-trimoxazole), nevirapine, oxicam NSAIDs, aminopenicillins; *Mycoplasma pneumoniae* in children |
| **DRESS (drug reaction with eosinophilia and systemic symptoms)** | Fever, widespread morbilliform rash, facial oedema, lymphadenopathy, eosinophilia (> 1.5 × 10⁹/L) or atypical lymphocytes, hepatitis, nephritis, pneumonitis, myocarditis; HHV-6/7 reactivation | 2–8 weeks | Allopurinol, aromatic anticonvulsants, sulfonamides, vancomycin, minocycline, abacavir |
| **AGEP (acute generalised exanthematous pustulosis)** | Fever, hundreds of tiny non-follicular sterile pustules on erythema, neutrophilia | Hours to 2 days | Aminopenicillins, macrolides, diltiazem, terbinafine |
| **Anticoagulant (warfarin) necrosis, vasculitis, serum sickness-like reaction** | Painful necrotic skin, palpable purpura, arthralgia | Days to weeks | Warfarin (day 3–5), many drugs |

**Management of SJS/TEN.**

1. Stop the culprit immediately (and all non-essential drugs; check the 8 weeks of exposure).
2. Transfer to a burns unit or ICU with experience; scoring with SCORTEN (age > 40, malignancy, heart rate > 120, initial BSA > 10%, urea > 10 mmol/L, glucose > 14 mmol/L, bicarbonate < 20 mmol/L).
3. Supportive care: fluids (less than burns), analgesia, non-adherent dressings, temperature control, nutritional support, ophthalmology and gynaecology/urology review for mucosal involvement, infection surveillance, avoidance of unnecessary antibiotic prophylaxis.
4. Adjunctive immunomodulation (ciclosporin, etanercept, short high-dose steroids, IVIG) is used in some centres; evidence is limited; specialist decision.
5. Counsel on lifelong avoidance and cross-reactions; carry an allergy card; consider HLA testing for relatives (e.g., HLA-B*15:02 in people of Han Chinese, Thai and some Southeast Asian ancestry before carbamazepine; HLA-B*58:01 before allopurinol in Han Chinese, Thai, Korean; HLA-B*57:01 before abacavir).

**Management of DRESS.** Withdraw the drug; admit if organ involvement; systemic corticosteroids (prednisolone 0.5–1 mg/kg/day) tapered slowly over 6–12 weeks as relapses occur with rapid taper; monitor LFTs, creatinine, troponin, thyroid function (autoimmune thyroiditis can follow); watch for flares and autoimmune sequelae.

## 7. Blistering diseases

Distinguish **tense** blisters (subepidermal; bullous pemphigoid, dermatitis herpetiformis, epidermolysis bullosa acquisita) from **flaccid** blisters that rupture easily to leave erosions (intraepidermal; pemphigus). The Nikolsky sign (lateral pressure produces blister extension) is positive in pemphigus, SJS/TEN and staphylococcal scalded skin syndrome.

- **Bullous pemphigoid**: the most common autoimmune blistering disease, in people over 70. Itchy urticarial prodrome for weeks followed by tense bullae on erythematous bases (flexural limbs, trunk). Mucosal involvement is mild or absent. Diagnosis: skin biopsy from edge of lesion for histology plus perilesional biopsy for direct immunofluorescence (linear IgG/C3 at basement membrane), and serum anti-BP180/BP230. Treatment: potent topical steroids (clobetasol) over the whole body or oral prednisolone 0.3–0.5 mg/kg, doxycycline as steroid-sparing, methotrexate, dupilumab in refractory cases. Associated with DPP-4 inhibitors, loop diuretics, neurological disease (dementia, Parkinson, stroke).
- **Pemphigus vulgaris**: flaccid blisters and erosions, almost always with **painful oral erosions** (often first sign); desmoglein 3 ± 1 antibodies. Treatment: high-dose prednisolone plus **rituximab** (first-line), with steroid taper; historically fatal, now mostly controlled.
- **Dermatitis herpetiformis**: intensely itchy grouped vesicles on elbows, knees, buttocks, scalp; gluten-sensitive enteropathy; granular IgA in papillary dermis. Gluten-free diet and dapsone (check G6PD first).
- **Others**: linear IgA disease, erythema multiforme (target lesions on acral sites; recurrent herpes simplex is the commonest trigger), porphyria cutanea tarda (fragile blistering of the backs of hands; hepatitis C, alcohol, iron overload, oestrogen).

## 8. Skin cancer and pigmented lesions

### 8.1 Risk factors and prevention

Ultraviolet exposure (cumulative for SCC and BCC; intermittent intense exposure and sunburn in childhood for melanoma), fair skin (Fitzpatrick I–II), many naevi (> 100) or atypical naevi, family history, immunosuppression (organ transplant recipients have a 65–250-fold increased risk of SCC), prior skin cancer, arsenic, radiotherapy, chronic inflammation or scarring, HPV. Advise sun-protective behaviour: shade, clothing, hats, broad-spectrum sunscreen SPF 30 or above, avoid tanning beds.

### 8.2 Basal cell carcinoma (BCC)

The most common cancer in humans. Slow-growing, locally invasive, rarely metastasises. Pearly, translucent nodule with telangiectasia, rolled edge, central ulceration ("rodent ulcer"); superficial BCC resembles an eczematous or psoriasiform pink scaly patch that does not heal; morpheaform BCC is a scar-like plaque. Mostly on the head and neck. Diagnosis by biopsy. Treatment: surgical excision (4 mm margin for well-defined low-risk lesions), Mohs micrographic surgery (high-risk sites such as eyelids, nose, ears, lips; recurrent or morpheaform subtypes), topical imiquimod or 5-fluorouracil, cryotherapy, photodynamic therapy or radiotherapy for selected low-risk lesions. Hedgehog pathway inhibitors (vismodegib, sonidegib) for locally advanced or metastatic disease.

### 8.3 Actinic keratosis and squamous cell carcinoma (SCC)

**Actinic keratoses**: rough, scaly, erythematous macules or papules on sun-damaged skin (face, scalp, backs of hands). A small proportion progress to SCC. Treat with cryotherapy, topical 5-fluorouracil (with or without calcipotriol), imiquimod, diclofenac gel, or photodynamic therapy; field-directed therapy for widespread disease.

**Squamous cell carcinoma.** Firm, scaly or crusted nodule or ulcer, or a lesion that does not heal, often on lips, ears, scalp, dorsal hands and lower legs; rapid growth, tenderness, bleeding. Higher-risk features: diameter > 2 cm, thickness > 4 mm, perineural invasion, poor differentiation, immunosuppression, ear or lip site, arising in scar or chronic ulcer. Metastasises in 2–5% (higher with high-risk features) to regional nodes. Treatment: surgical excision with 4–6 mm margins, Mohs surgery for high-risk lesions, adjuvant radiotherapy for high-risk, PD-1 inhibitor cemiplimab for advanced disease. Perform a lymph-node examination.

**Bowen disease** (SCC in situ): well-defined erythematous scaly patch, slowly enlarging; treat with topical therapy, curettage, cryotherapy or excision. **Keratoacanthoma**: rapidly growing dome-shaped nodule with central keratin plug on sun-damaged skin; indistinguishable from SCC; treat as SCC.

### 8.4 Melanoma and pigmented lesions

Melanoma accounts for a small proportion of skin cancers but the majority of skin cancer deaths. Early detection and excision are curative in most cases; thickness (Breslow depth) is the dominant prognostic factor.

**Subtypes.** Superficial spreading (most common), nodular (rapidly growing, often amelanotic, pink or red; can lack ABCDE features: follow "EFG": Elevated, Firm, Growing), lentigo maligna (slowly enlarging irregular brown macule on chronically sun-damaged face of older people), acral lentiginous (palms, soles, nail unit; the most common subtype in people with darker skin), mucosal.

**Detection tools.**

- **ABCDE**: Asymmetry, Border irregularity, Colour variegation, Diameter > 6 mm, Evolution (change in size, shape, colour, symptoms such as itch or bleeding). Evolution is the most sensitive criterion.
- **Ugly duckling sign**: a lesion that looks different from the patient's other naevi.
- **7-point checklist** (NICE/UK): major features (change in size, irregular shape, irregular colour) score 2 points each, minor features (diameter ≥ 7 mm, inflammation, oozing/crusting, change in sensation) 1 point each; a total of 3 or more warrants referral.
- **Dermoscopy** improves diagnostic accuracy in trained hands (pigment network, globules, streaks, blue-white veil, atypical vessels); poor in untrained users.
- **Nail melanoma**: a longitudinal pigmented band (melanonychia) that is wide (> 3 mm), irregular, darkening, with pigment extension onto the nail fold (Hutchinson sign), in a single digit, especially thumb or great toe, in an adult. Biopsy the nail matrix.

**Management.** Complete excision biopsy with a 2–5 mm margin (never shave biopsy or curettage a suspected melanoma, as depth is lost); histology determines Breslow thickness, ulceration, mitotic rate. Wide local excision margins based on thickness (1 cm for in-situ to ≤ 1 mm; 1–2 cm for 1–2 mm; 2 cm for > 2 mm). Sentinel lymph node biopsy for lesions > 0.8 mm or with adverse features. Adjuvant immunotherapy (anti-PD-1) or BRAF/MEK inhibitors for stage III disease; first-line immune checkpoint inhibitors and targeted therapies for metastatic disease have transformed survival. Follow-up with skin and lymph node examination at intervals depending on stage; teach self-examination.

**Benign mimics.** Seborrhoeic keratosis (stuck-on, waxy, greasy surface, keratin plugs), dermatofibroma (firm, dimples on pinching), haemangioma (cherry angioma), solar lentigo, blue naevus, haemangioma, thrombosed haemorrhoid-like subungual haematoma (grows out with the nail; if unsure, biopsy). **Dysplastic (atypical) naevi** are larger, irregular, with variable pigment; document and monitor.

### 8.5 Less common skin malignancies

- **Merkel cell carcinoma**: rapidly growing painless firm pink or violaceous nodule on sun-exposed skin of older or immunosuppressed patients; aggressive. AEIOU mnemonic: Asymptomatic, Expanding rapidly, Immune suppression, Older than 50, UV-exposed site.
- **Cutaneous T-cell lymphoma (mycosis fungoides)**: patches and plaques resembling eczema or psoriasis, unresponsive to treatment, often in non-sun-exposed sites; needs repeated biopsies.
- **Kaposi sarcoma**: violaceous plaques and nodules; HIV, HHV-8.
- **Dermatofibrosarcoma protuberans, angiosarcoma (scalp of older adults, bruise-like), sebaceous carcinoma (eyelid)**.

## 10. Pruritus without a primary rash

Generalised itch with no skin lesions (apart from excoriation) deserves a systemic work-up. Causes include dry skin (the commonest), scabies (early), drug-induced, cholestasis (primary biliary cholangitis, obstructive jaundice, pregnancy), chronic kidney disease, iron deficiency, thyroid disease, diabetes, polycythaemia vera (aquagenic itch), lymphoma (Hodgkin), solid malignancy, HIV, neuropathic and psychogenic causes.

Initial investigations: FBC, ferritin, ESR/CRP, urea and creatinine, LFTs (including ALP and bilirubin), TSH, glucose/HbA1c, calcium, and, according to history, HIV, hepatitis serology, chest X-ray, serum protein electrophoresis. Review all medications. Treat the cause; emollients, cooling agents, and sedating antihistamines at night have limited effect for non-histaminergic itch; consider gabapentinoids, mirtazapine, naltrexone, cholestyramine (cholestasis), difelikefalin (CKD-associated pruritus).

## 12. Using topical corticosteroids

### 12.1 Potency (UK-style classification)

| Class | Examples |
|---|---|
| Mild | Hydrocortisone 0.5–2.5% |
| Moderate | Clobetasone butyrate 0.05%, betamethasone valerate 0.025% |
| Potent | Betamethasone valerate 0.1%, mometasone 0.1%, fluocinonide 0.05% |
| Very potent | Clobetasol propionate 0.05%, diflucortolone valerate 0.3% |

(The US system uses classes I–VII, with I the most potent.)

### 12.2 Principles

- **Match potency to site, age and disease.** Face, eyelids, neck, flexures and genitalia: mild only (short courses of moderate potency under supervision). Trunk and limbs: moderate to potent. Palms and soles and thick plaques: potent to very potent. Children and older adults: lowest effective potency.
- **Quantity: the fingertip unit (FTU)**: the amount squeezed from a standard tube along an adult index finger from the distal skin crease to the tip (≈ 0.5 g) covers an area twice the size of an adult flat hand. Approximate FTUs per application for an adult: one hand (front and back) 1; one arm 3; one leg 6; trunk (front or back) 7; face and neck 2.5.
- **Duration**: potent or very potent steroids for no more than 2–4 weeks continuously at one site, followed by a break or step-down.
- **Apply once or twice daily**, with emollients applied at a separate time (30 minutes apart).
- **Avoid** on untreated infection, rosacea, perioral dermatitis, acne, undiagnosed lesions (tinea incognito), and leg ulcers.
- **Side effects**: skin atrophy, striae, telangiectasia, purpura, perioral dermatitis, acne, rosacea-like eruptions, hypertrichosis, delayed wound healing, adrenal suppression (large quantities of potent steroids in children), glaucoma and cataract (periocular use), withdrawal flares and rebound ("red skin syndrome").
- **Steroid phobia** is common and leads to under-treatment: explain safe use clearly.

## 13. Dermatological emergencies

| Emergency | Key sign | Immediate action |
|---|---|---|
| SJS/TEN | Painful skin, mucosal erosions, blistering, new drug | Stop drug, transfer to burns/ICU |
| DRESS | Fever, facial oedema, rash, eosinophilia, organ dysfunction | Stop drug, admit, steroids |
| Erythroderma (> 90% BSA) | Fever, fluid loss, cardiac failure | Admit; identify cause (psoriasis, eczema, drug, lymphoma/Sézary); emollients, fluid care |
| Necrotising fasciitis | Pain out of proportion, bullae, dusky skin, toxicity | Surgery and IV antibiotics |
| Eczema herpeticum | Punched-out vesicles on eczema | IV or oral aciclovir at once |
| Toxic shock syndrome | Fever, hypotension, diffuse macular erythroderma, desquamation later | Remove foreign body/tampon, IV antibiotics (incl. clindamycin), supportive care |
| Staphylococcal scalded skin syndrome | Febrile infant with tender peeling | IV antistaphylococcal antibiotics |
| Meningococcaemia | Fever, non-blanching petechial/purpuric rash | Immediate IV ceftriaxone (see chapters 39 and 41) |
| Pemphigus | Mucosal erosions, flaccid blisters | Steroids, specialist care |
| Acute angioedema with airway involvement | Tongue or throat swelling | Adrenaline (mast cell mediated); airway management (see chapter 40) |
| Acute limb ischaemia, calciphylaxis, purpura fulminans | Dusky, painful, necrotic skin | Vascular/ICU/haematology review |
| Orbital cellulitis | Periorbital swelling, proptosis, painful eye movements, fever | Admit, CT, IV antibiotics, ophthalmology |

## Worked cases

### Case 1: A rash after antibiotics

**Presentation.** Aisha, 19, has a sore throat and fatigue for a week. Her GP prescribes amoxicillin. On day 4 she develops a widespread pink maculopapular rash on the trunk and limbs, not itchy, without fever worsening or mucosal involvement. Examination: tonsillar exudates, cervical and axillary lymphadenopathy, palpable spleen tip.

**Reasoning.** Rash after an aminopenicillin in a patient with exudative pharyngitis, generalised lymphadenopathy and splenomegaly strongly suggests **infectious mononucleosis** with an aminopenicillin-associated rash rather than IgE-mediated allergy. No red flags for SCAR (no mucosal erosions, blisters, facial oedema, pain, or organ involvement).

**Action.** Stop amoxicillin; confirm EBV (monospot/heterophile antibody, EBV VCA IgM; atypical lymphocytes). Reassure and document carefully ("rash during EBV infection"), as most patients later tolerate penicillins; consider formal drug challenge after recovery in a specialist allergy service before labelling as penicillin-allergic. Warn about contact sports avoidance for several weeks (splenic rupture risk).

**Lessons.** A rash after a drug does not equal allergy. Check the context, and never keep a lifelong "allergy" label that was not earned (it increases the use of broader antibiotics).

### Case 2: A child with worsening eczema

**Presentation.** Leo, 4, known atopic eczema, flares for three days with crusted, punched-out erosions and clusters of monomorphic vesicles on his cheeks and neck, fever 38.9 °C, irritability, and poor oral intake. His father has had cold sores.

**Reasoning.** **Eczema herpeticum** (disseminated HSV infection on eczematous skin). Can be mistaken for bacterial superinfection. It can involve the eye and cause viraemia and meningitis.

**Action.** Admit. IV aciclovir (10 mg/kg every 8 h, dose adjusted for renal function and hydration), hydrate, treat pain. Swab vesicle base for HSV PCR (but do not wait for results). Add flucloxacillin for secondary staphylococcal infection if suspected. Ophthalmology if periocular involvement. Continue topical emollients; avoid potent steroids on affected sites during acute phase. Counsel the parents: avoid contact with people with cold sores; early presentation with new vesicles on eczema in future.

**Lessons.** In eczema with fever and vesicles or punched-out erosions, treat as HSV immediately.

### Case 3: The "cellulitis" that was bilateral

**Presentation.** Mr D., 74, is sent to the emergency department for "bilateral lower leg cellulitis": both shins red, warm, itchy, scaly, with pitting oedema and haemosiderin staining. Temperature 36.9 °C, CRP 8 mg/L, WBC normal. He has varicose veins, heart failure and takes amlodipine.

**Reasoning.** Bilateral involvement, itch and scale, normal inflammatory markers, venous changes and oedema indicate **venous stasis dermatitis** with oedema (possibly worsened by amlodipine and heart failure) rather than cellulitis. Cellulitis is nearly always unilateral and painful rather than itchy.

**Action.** Emollient, moderate-potency topical steroid for 1–2 weeks, graded compression after excluding arterial disease (ankle–brachial pressure index), leg elevation, review of diuretic dosing and heart failure status, and avoidance of antibiotics. Check for contact dermatitis from topical agents. Arrange vascular referral for venous insufficiency.

**Lessons.** Inappropriate antibiotics for pseudocellulitis are common and harmful. Look at laterality, symptoms (itch vs pain), systemic signs and the context.

### Case 4: A new mole

**Presentation.** Tom, 42, fair-skinned, notices a pigmented lesion on his upper back that his wife says has been "growing for a year". It is 9 mm, asymmetrical, with variegated brown and black colour and an irregular border; it itches occasionally. He has had several sunburns as a teenager.

**Reasoning.** ABCDE positive on A, B, C, D, E. High suspicion of melanoma.

**Action.** Urgent referral (two-week-wait pathway) or primary excision biopsy with 2 mm margin including a cuff of normal skin and a layer of subcutaneous fat, no shave. Histology: superficial spreading melanoma, Breslow 1.4 mm, no ulceration, 2 mitoses/mm². Wide local excision with 1–2 cm margins; sentinel lymph node biopsy discussed (negative). Stage IB. Follow-up every 3–6 months for 3 years then yearly, whole-body skin check and nodal palpation, self-examination teaching, family screening, sun protection.

**Lessons.** Evolution is the most sensitive feature; excise, don't shave; ask the family.

### Case 5: Mouth sores and blisters

**Presentation.** A 58-year-old woman has had painful mouth erosions for 3 months, treated unsuccessfully as aphthous ulcers and candida. Now flaccid blisters appear on the chest and back; some rupture to leave raw erosions. Nikolsky sign positive. She is on no new drugs.

**Reasoning.** Painful oral erosions followed by flaccid cutaneous blisters indicate **pemphigus vulgaris**.

**Action.** Biopsy of lesional skin for histology (acantholysis) and perilesional skin for direct immunofluorescence (intercellular IgG, "chicken wire"), serum anti-desmoglein 1 and 3 antibodies. Start prednisolone 1 mg/kg/day with rituximab (two 1 g doses 2 weeks apart or alternative regimen), with bone protection, PPI, glucose monitoring, and screening for TB and hepatitis B before rituximab. Taper over months. Care for oral hygiene, analgesic mouthwashes.

**Lessons.** Persistent oral erosions beyond 2–3 weeks need biopsy. A labelled "recurrent aphthae" can hide pemphigus, lichen planus, or squamous cell carcinoma.

### Case 6: A rash with fever and facial swelling

**Presentation.** Mr G., 51, started allopurinol 5 weeks ago for gout. He now has fever 38.8 °C, a generalised morbilliform rash covering 60% of body surface, facial oedema, and enlarged cervical lymph nodes. Labs: eosinophils 2.4 × 10⁹/L, ALT 320 U/L, creatinine 168 µmol/L (previously 90).

**Reasoning.** Latency 2–8 weeks, facial oedema, lymphadenopathy, eosinophilia, hepatic and renal involvement: **DRESS** due to allopurinol (risk is raised in renal impairment and with HLA-B*58:01).

**Action.** Stop allopurinol. Admit, supportive care, topical potent steroids, systemic prednisolone 1 mg/kg/day with slow taper over 8–12 weeks. Monitor liver and kidney, ECG and troponin (myocarditis), TSH (thyroiditis at weeks to months), and viral reactivation if relapsing (HHV-6, CMV, EBV). Record the allergy. Gout alternatives later: febuxostat is not a safe substitute in many cases of allopurinol hypersensitivity and cross-reactivity must be considered; specialist rheumatology input needed.

**Lessons.** Rash plus fever plus organ involvement after 2–8 weeks of a high-risk drug: DRESS. Rapid steroid taper leads to relapse.

## Common pitfalls

1. **Naming before describing**: misclassifying lesions without careful description, distribution, and palpation.
2. **Treating undiagnosed rash with topical steroids**, causing tinea incognito, perioral dermatitis, and masking morphology; take scrapings first for scaly annular rashes.
3. **Missing eczema herpeticum** in a child or adult with atopic eczema, fever and vesicles.
4. **Overdiagnosing cellulitis**: bilateral, itchy, scaly, or afebrile rashes are rarely cellulitis; a missed alternative (stasis dermatitis, DVT, gout) is the commonest error.
5. **Underestimating necrotising fasciitis** when pain exceeds appearance.
6. **Not asking about drugs** started in the last 8 weeks (and over-the-counter, herbal products).
7. **Missing mucosal involvement** in a patient with new rash and fever (SJS/TEN).
8. **Giving systemic steroids for psoriasis**, risking erythrodermic or pustular flares.
9. **Failing to treat scabies contacts** simultaneously; mistaking post-scabetic itch for treatment failure.
10. **Biopsy or shave of suspected melanoma** with inadequate margin or depth.
11. **Neglecting darker skin**: expecting red, missing violaceous change; missing acral and nail melanoma.
12. **Neglecting the psychological impact** of acne, vitiligo, alopecia, psoriasis, hidradenitis.
13. **Not checking the whole skin**, scalp, nails and mucosae.
14. **Ignoring itch as a systemic symptom**, delaying diagnosis of lymphoma, cholestasis, renal disease or iron deficiency.
15. **Labelling every post-drug rash as allergy** and creating lifelong penicillin labels.

---

## Key points

- Describe first (distribution, primary lesion, secondary changes, colour, configuration), then diagnose. Examine all the skin.
- Atopic eczema: emollients, topical steroids by potency and site, treat infection, and know eczema herpeticum.
- Psoriasis is systemic: screen for arthritis and cardiometabolic disease; beware erythrodermic and pustular forms; avoid systemic steroids.
- Cellulitis is unilateral, painful, with systemic features; bilateral itchy leg "cellulitis" is usually stasis dermatitis. Necrotising fasciitis is a surgical emergency.
- Any rash with fever after a new drug, especially with mucosal involvement, skin pain, blistering, facial oedema or organ involvement, is an emergency: SJS/TEN or DRESS.
- Use ABCDE and the ugly-duckling sign; excise suspected melanoma with a narrow margin and send for histology.
- Skin is a window on systemic disease; persistent itch deserves a work-up.
- Use topical steroids by potency and fingertip units; avoid them on undiagnosed facial rashes.

## Self-assessment

**Questions**

1. A 5-year-old has itchy, red, scaly patches in the elbow and knee flexures, dry skin, and sleeps badly. What are the first-line treatments, and how much emollient might be needed per week?
2. Which three features distinguish cellulitis from venous stasis dermatitis?
3. A 30-year-old man has an annular scaly plaque on his thigh with a raised edge. Why should you avoid hydrocortisone cream before confirming the diagnosis?
4. List four clinical red flags that make a drug rash a possible severe cutaneous adverse reaction.
5. A woman of reproductive age with severe nodulocystic acne is starting isotretinoin. What must you arrange before and during treatment?
6. A 65-year-old man has a rapidly growing 1.5 cm firm pink nodule on his scalp, with a keratin core, that appeared 4 weeks ago. Differential diagnosis and next step?
7. A patient has pain out of proportion to skin findings, with dusky skin and early bullae on the thigh following a minor injury. What is the diagnosis to exclude and the first actions?
8. Describe the Fingertip Unit and how many are needed to treat one adult arm once.
9. A teenager has a sudden eruption of small, scaly, "raindrop" plaques on the trunk two weeks after a sore throat. Diagnosis and likely course?
10. A man of Han Chinese ancestry is about to start carbamazepine. What genetic test is recommended and why?

**Answers**

1. Atopic eczema. Emollients used generously (often 250–500 g per week for a child), soap substitutes, and topical corticosteroid of mild-to-moderate potency for flares, applied once or twice daily until clear; treat infection and consider nail trimming and sleep strategies. Calcineurin inhibitors for sensitive sites.
2. Cellulitis is typically unilateral, painful (not itchy), with warmth, spreading erythema, fever or malaise and raised inflammatory markers; stasis dermatitis is often bilateral, itchy and scaly with haemosiderin staining, varicosities and oedema, without systemic features.
3. Topical steroids alter the appearance (reduced scale, lost raised border, diffuse spread: tinea incognito) and worsen the fungal infection. Take skin scrapings for microscopy and culture first.
4. Mucosal involvement, skin pain, blistering or skin detachment (Nikolsky sign), facial oedema, fever, lymphadenopathy, eosinophilia or abnormal LFTs, purpura, and rapid spread. (Any four.)
5. Pregnancy prevention programme: confirm negative pregnancy test before starting, monthly during treatment and 5 weeks after; effective contraception (usually two methods or one highly effective) from one month before until one month after; baseline lipids and liver function (repeat at 1–2 months); mood review at each visit; counsel on dryness, photosensitivity, avoidance of tetracyclines and vitamin A supplements.
6. Squamous cell carcinoma or keratoacanthoma (indistinguishable clinically), also BCC, Merkel cell carcinoma, amelanotic melanoma. Urgent biopsy or excision with histology.
7. Necrotising fasciitis. Immediate senior surgical review for urgent exploration and debridement, broad-spectrum IV antibiotics with anaerobic cover and clindamycin, resuscitation, lactate and bloods; do not delay for imaging.
8. One FTU is the amount of cream squeezed from a tube along the length of an adult index finger from the distal crease to the tip (about 0.5 g); one adult arm requires about 3 FTUs per application.
9. Guttate psoriasis, triggered by streptococcal infection. Often resolves over weeks to months; emollients, mild to moderate topical steroids or vitamin D analogue, narrowband UVB for persistent disease; treat any ongoing streptococcal infection; some develop chronic plaque psoriasis later.
10. HLA-B*15:02 testing before carbamazepine in people of Han Chinese, Thai and some other Southeast Asian ancestries, because carriers have a markedly increased risk of SJS/TEN; avoid the drug if positive.
