## Chapter 2. Cognitive Biases and Diagnostic Error

### Learning objectives

After studying this chapter you should be able to:

1. Define diagnostic error and distinguish cognitive, system-related and no-fault causes.
2. Recognise the most common cognitive biases and name them in real cases.
3. Explain why experienced clinicians are *not* immune to bias.
4. Apply practical debiasing strategies: the diagnostic time-out, "what else?", cognitive forcing strategies and structured second looks.
5. Use handover, safety-netting and feedback to reduce error at the level of the system.

---

### 2.1 Diagnostic error: what it is and why it matters

A **diagnostic error** is a diagnosis that is missed, wrong or delayed, as judged against information that was available (or could reasonably have been obtained) at the time. It is distinct from a diagnosis that was *uncertain but reasonable*: the patient whose rare disease declares itself only after months of evolving symptoms has not necessarily been the victim of an error.

Studies using autopsies, chart reviews, malpractice claims and patient reports agree on several points:

- Diagnostic errors are common across all specialties and settings, and a meaningful share cause serious harm.
- Errors are usually **not** caused by a lack of knowledge. More often the clinician *knew* the disease but did not think of it, or thought of it and abandoned it too early.
- Most errors involve **several contributing factors** at once: a cognitive slip, a rushed environment, incomplete information, and a communication gap.

It helps to sort causes into three groups:

| Category | Examples |
|---|---|
| **Cognitive** (inside the clinician's head) | Anchoring, premature closure, failure to consider alternatives, faulty interpretation of a test |
| **System-related** (inside the organisation) | Lost results, overcrowded wards, poor handover, no access to records, interruptions, lack of senior review |
| **No-fault** (nobody did anything wrong) | Atypical presentation, patient misleading or unable to give a history, rare disease, evolving illness |

This chapter concentrates on the cognitive causes, because they are the ones that each of us can train against, but please keep the others in mind: the best-disciplined thinker will still fail in a system that loses the blood results.

---

### 2.2 Why smart, experienced clinicians err

Human cognition relies on **heuristics**: mental shortcuts that are quick and usually good enough. They are indispensable; without them nobody could manage a clinic of 30 patients. But the same shortcuts produce systematic errors, called **cognitive biases**, under predictable conditions:

- **time pressure** and interruptions;
- **fatigue** and sleep loss;
- **high cognitive load** (many patients, many tasks);
- **emotion** (anger, fear, sympathy, dislike);
- **ambiguous or incomplete information**;
- **overconfidence**, which grows with experience unless it is corrected by feedback.

Three uncomfortable truths follow:

1. **Experience does not remove bias.** It tends to make intuition faster, and sometimes more confidently wrong.
2. **You cannot feel a bias while it is acting.** Anchoring feels like clarity. Premature closure feels like having solved the problem.
3. **Knowing the list of biases is not enough.** Studies of teaching about bias show only modest benefit. What works better is changing the *process* (checklists, pauses, structured second looks) so that the safeguard does not depend on you feeling doubtful.

---

### 2.3 A catalogue of common biases

#### Anchoring

**Definition.** Locking onto an early piece of information and failing to adjust sufficiently as new data arrive.

**Example.** A triage note reads "migraine". Every clinician who follows reads the patient's story through that word.

#### Premature closure

**Definition.** Accepting a diagnosis before it has been fully verified, and stopping the search. It is often called the most common cognitive cause of diagnostic error.

**Example.** A woman with fatigue and a low haemoglobin is labelled "heavy periods". The cause is accepted; nobody asks whether the *type* of anaemia (a low MCV with low ferritin) could be caused by something else, such as bleeding from the bowel.

#### Confirmation bias

**Definition.** Seeking, noticing and remembering information that supports your hypothesis, and discounting what contradicts it.

**Example.** You suspect pneumonia, so you listen for crackles and find them. A normal chest X-ray is dismissed as "early".

#### Availability bias

**Definition.** Overestimating the probability of diagnoses that come easily to mind, because they are recent, dramatic or memorable.

**Example.** After diagnosing a pulmonary embolus last week, every breathless patient this week seems to have one. Conversely, after a quiet month of benign chest pain, one becomes less alert to a real ACS.

#### Representativeness (and the "typical" trap)

**Definition.** Judging probability by how closely the patient resembles a textbook picture, ignoring base rates and atypical presentations.

**Example.** "She is young and has no risk factors, so it can't be an MI." Women, older adults and diabetic patients frequently have atypical symptoms.

#### Base-rate neglect

**Definition.** Ignoring how common or rare a disease is in the population.

**Example.** Ordering a test for a rare disease in a patient with a low pre-test probability, then believing the (probably false) positive.

#### Framing effect

**Definition.** Being swayed by how the problem is presented.

**Example.** "A 40-year-old intoxicated man with abdominal pain" frames the case; the intoxication gets the blame, and a subdural haematoma or pancreatitis goes unconsidered.

#### Diagnostic momentum

**Definition.** A tentative label gets passed from person to person and gradually hardens into fact.

**Example.** "Probable UTI" in a nursing-home resident becomes "UTI" in the transfer letter, then "known UTI" in the ED, then the reason the confusion is not investigated further.

#### Triage cueing

**Definition.** The patient is sent to a specific team or location based on an early impression, and that team's lens determines what is looked for.

**Example.** A patient with weakness and back pain is routed to orthopaedics; a spinal cord compression from metastasis is found later than it should be.

#### Search satisficing ("the second fracture")

**Definition.** Stopping the search once you have found *something*.

**Example.** After a radiograph shows a fractured wrist, the associated scaphoid fracture or a second injury is missed. In another version: a patient's anaemia is attributed to the first abnormality found (e.g. low ferritin), while the accompanying haematological or malignant cause is not pursued.

#### Posterior probability error

**Definition.** Assuming that because a patient has had a given problem before, the current symptom must be from the same cause.

**Example.** "He is a frequent attender with anxiety." The chest pain this time is attributed to anxiety without full assessment.

#### Overconfidence

**Definition.** Believing you know more than you do; acting on incomplete information with excessive certainty.

**Example.** "I've seen hundreds of these, I don't need to examine the abdomen."

#### Affective bias

**Definition.** Emotion distorts judgement, in either direction. Sympathy for a patient may lower your threshold for admitting, but irritation with a "difficult" patient may raise your threshold for investigating.

**Example.** A patient with a history of substance use who presents with severe abdominal pain is dismissed as "drug-seeking"; the pain turns out to be a perforated viscus.

#### Sutton's slip (and its counterpart)

**Definition.** Going straight for the obvious diagnosis ("Willie Sutton robbed banks because that's where the money is"), without considering the unexpected one.

*Counterpart: Zebra retreat.* Not pursuing a rare diagnosis that is suspected, because it is rare, even when the evidence suggests it.

#### Psych-out error

**Definition.** Attributing a patient's physical symptoms to a psychiatric illness, or missing an organic cause in a patient with a mental disorder.

**Example.** An agitated patient with a known history of schizophrenia is thought to be "decompensating". The true cause is hypoglycaemia, hypoxia or delirium from infection.

### 2.4 Worked cases

#### Case 1: Anchoring on "migraine"

A 34-year-old woman with a history of migraine attends the emergency department with a severe headache. She is triaged as "migraine, similar to previous". The doctor treats with an antiemetic and an analgesic; the pain eases slightly and she is discharged.

Four days later she returns, collapsed. A CT shows subarachnoid haemorrhage.

*What was missed.* In the notes, but not in the thinking: the headache was **sudden in onset ("like a thunderclap", peaking within a minute)** and **"the worst of my life"**, features that differ from her usual migraine. She also had neck stiffness on direct questioning that was never asked about.

*Biases.* Anchoring (the triage label), framing (history of migraine), posterior probability error (previous headaches were migraines), premature closure (partial response to analgesia taken as confirming benign cause; note that response to analgesia does *not* exclude serious causes).

*Debiasing.* Ask a standard question: "Is this headache *different* from the previous ones? When did it reach maximum intensity?" Use red-flag checklists (Chapter 24). The diagnostic time-out: "If this was *not* migraine, what would it be, and what would I do to exclude it?"

#### Case 2: Premature closure and the incomplete anaemia work-up

A 52-year-old woman presents with fatigue and pallor. Haemoglobin is 8.9 g/dL (89 g/L), MCV is 72 fL, ferritin is low. She mentions that her periods are "heavy". The doctor writes "iron-deficiency anaemia secondary to menorrhagia", starts oral iron, and arranges follow-up.

At review in two months, her haemoglobin has only risen slightly and she now reports mild weight loss. A colonoscopy finds a caecal tumour.

*Biases.* Premature closure (the explanation for the iron deficiency was accepted when it appeared), search satisficing, availability (menorrhagia is a common cause in women), representativeness (a woman in her fifties with heavy periods "fits").

*Lesson.* In an adult, iron-deficiency anaemia is a **sign**, not a diagnosis. In men and in postmenopausal women, gastrointestinal blood loss must be excluded; in premenopausal women, the threshold is based on age, symptoms and response to treatment. A perimenopausal woman with weight loss deserves more than iron.

*Debiasing.* Ask "Does this explanation account for *all* of the findings, including the *degree* of anaemia? What is the single most dangerous cause I could be missing here?"

#### Case 3: Diagnostic momentum

An 81-year-old nursing-home resident becomes more drowsy and confused over 24 hours. A urine dipstick is positive for leucocytes and nitrites. The nurse's handover says "UTI, started antibiotics". The next doctor accepts this and does not examine further. Two days later the patient is hypotensive, with a respiratory rate of 30 and a right-sided consolidation on chest X-ray; the urine culture is negative.

*Biases.* Diagnostic momentum, anchoring, confirmation bias (a dipstick result in an older adult is very often positive without infection, since asymptomatic bacteriuria is common in this age group).

*Lesson.* Confusion in an older adult is a *syndrome* (delirium), with many potential causes: infection at any site, drugs, urinary retention, constipation, metabolic derangement, hypoxia, myocardial infarction, stroke. Positive dipstick results alone, in the absence of urinary symptoms, rarely justify attributing delirium to a urinary infection.

*Debiasing.* At handover, say "Working diagnosis: possible UTI. Not yet confirmed. Other sources not yet excluded. Please recheck chest, abdomen, drugs, and hydration."

#### Case 4: Search satisficing in trauma

A 27-year-old man falls from a bike, with obvious deformity of his left forearm. Radiographs confirm a displaced mid-shaft fracture of the radius and ulna. The team focuses on the fracture and arranges surgery. Overnight, he complains of left-sided pain on breathing and shows falling oxygen saturation. A chest X-ray shows a pneumothorax and rib fractures that were not looked for on arrival.

*Bias.* Search satisficing: the "distracting injury" captured attention.

*Lesson.* In trauma, follow a systematic primary survey (ABCDE, Chapter 37) *before* focusing on the injury that is most visible; a dramatic injury is not necessarily the most dangerous.

### 2.5 Debiasing: what works

Warnings alone do not reduce bias much. Strategies that *build structure into the process* do better.

#### 1. The diagnostic time-out

Pause before finalising a diagnosis and ask yourself:

- What else could this be?
- What is the most dangerous thing it could be? Have I excluded it?
- What doesn't fit?
- What would I expect to see if my leading diagnosis were wrong?
- Have I examined the patient properly, or am I relying on someone else's examination?

A diagnostic time-out takes thirty seconds. The cost is trivial and the yield, especially for high-risk presentations, is substantial.

#### 2. Consider the opposite

Deliberately write down reasons why your leading diagnosis might be wrong. This directly counters confirmation bias.

#### 3. Cognitive forcing strategies

These are fixed, rule-based habits that override the intuitive pathway at key points. Examples:

- "In any patient over 50 with new headache, always consider giant cell arteritis."
- "In any woman of childbearing age with abdominal pain, do a pregnancy test."
- "Every patient with iron-deficiency anaemia: ask why."
- "Every patient with unexplained syncope: examine for postural hypotension, check an ECG."
- "Always check the blood glucose in any altered mental state."

#### 4. Checklists and structured tools

Checklists compensate for fatigue and memory failure. Examples include red-flag lists, systematic review of systems for the unwell patient, and structured ECG interpretation. A checklist for a "can't-miss" differential (Chapter 3) ensures that each dangerous cause is deliberately considered.

#### 5. Get a second opinion, or a fresh look

Another person is not subject to your anchor. Ask a colleague to review the case *without* telling them your diagnosis first, then compare. Structured handover (Section 2.6) makes this more productive.

#### 6. Slow down when you feel certain early

If you are sure after thirty seconds, ask what is making you so. Quick certainty should raise, not lower, your vigilance for the specific unexpected.

#### 7. Seek feedback

Without feedback, intuition is not calibrated. Systematically follow up your patients, particularly the difficult ones: What was the final diagnosis? Was I right? What did I miss? Make a habit of checking outcomes for patients you sent home. This is the single most effective long-term protection against overconfidence.

#### 8. Manage your own state

Fatigue, hunger, anger and distraction raise error rates. When possible: take breaks, avoid making complex diagnostic decisions during interruptions, and be honest with yourself about your state at the end of a long shift.

#### 9. Pre-mortem thinking

Imagine that, in a week, the patient has returned with a catastrophic outcome. Ask: "What is the most likely explanation of why I failed?" The answer often points to the unexamined assumption.

### 2.6 System-level safeguards

Individual vigilance is necessary but insufficient. Good systems make errors harder to commit and easier to catch.

- **Structured handover.** Use a tool such as *I-PASS* or *SBAR* (Chapter 9). Include the *level of diagnostic certainty*, not just a label.
- **Test-result tracking.** Every test ordered has an owner who will see the result and act on it, including results that return after discharge.
- **Safety-netting.** Give patients clear written and verbal instructions about what to watch for and when to return (Chapter 73).
- **Early senior review** for high-risk presentations.
- **Second-reader processes** for imaging and ECGs in settings where they are available.
- **Listening to patients and families.** "She's not herself" and "he never complained like this before" have a high predictive value. A family's concern is a data point, not a nuisance.
- **A culture of speaking up.** A junior colleague or nurse who says "I'm worried about this patient" should be taken seriously.
- **Learning from near-misses.** Review cases without blame, focusing on how the process allowed the error.

---

### 2.7 When the patient is part of the problem, and part of the solution

Patients can contribute to diagnostic error without any fault of their own: language barriers, difficulty expressing symptoms, cognitive impairment, mistrust, and stigma can all distort the history. Conversely, patients are your best safeguard.

- Invite the patient to participate: "What do *you* think is going on?" and "What worries you most?" frequently uncovers the missing piece.
- Ask: "Is there anything else?" at least once before ending the history.
- Close the loop: "If anything changes, or you don't feel right, come back, even if you think it's minor."

---

### 2.8 Common pitfalls

- **Believing that bias is something that happens to others.** Everyone has it; it is part of normal cognition.
- **Relying on willpower.** Process changes (pause, checklist) outperform good intentions.
- **Over-correction.** Chasing every rare diagnosis causes over-investigation and harm. The aim is *calibrated* thinking, not universal suspicion.
- **Blame culture.** If the response to an error is blame, people hide errors, and learning stops.
- **Treating the label as final in a handover.**

---

### 2.9 Key points

- Diagnostic errors are common, and most arise from reasoning failures combined with system weaknesses, not from lack of knowledge.
- The most frequent biases are anchoring, premature closure, confirmation bias, availability, and diagnostic momentum.
- You cannot rely on feeling doubtful; put safeguards into the process.
- The diagnostic time-out ("What else? What doesn't fit? What can't I miss?") is simple, quick and effective.
- Seek feedback on outcomes, because intuition without feedback becomes overconfidence.
- Hand over *uncertainty* as clearly as you hand over diagnoses.

### 2.10 Self-assessment

1. Name the bias: "He's a known alcoholic, so the confusion must be intoxication."
   *Answer: Framing effect / affective bias, with possible premature closure. The organic causes of confusion (head injury, hypoglycaemia, Wernicke's encephalopathy, infection) must still be excluded.*

2. A radiologist reports a rib fracture on a trauma X-ray and stops looking. Which bias is this?
   *Answer: Search satisficing.*

3. Why is it important to include your level of diagnostic certainty in a handover?
   *Answer: A tentative label that is handed over without its uncertainty can harden into fact (diagnostic momentum), and later clinicians will stop considering alternatives.*

4. List three cognitive forcing strategies you could adopt in your own practice.
   *Answer: Open-ended; e.g. pregnancy test in every woman of childbearing age with abdominal pain; check glucose in all altered mental states; always ask "why?" about iron-deficiency anaemia.*

5. Why is knowing the list of biases not enough to prevent them?
   *Answer: Biases are not perceived while operating; structural safeguards (pause, checklist, second opinion, feedback) work better than awareness alone.*
