## Chapter 16. Musculoskeletal Examination

### Learning objectives

After studying this chapter you should be able to:

1. Perform a GALS (gait, arms, legs, spine) screening examination and decide when a regional examination is needed.
2. Apply the "look, feel, move" framework, plus function and special tests, to each major joint.
3. Distinguish inflammatory from mechanical (degenerative) joint disease, and monoarticular from polyarticular presentations.
4. Recognise the musculoskeletal emergencies: the hot swollen joint (septic arthritis), cauda equina syndrome, compartment syndrome, and fracture with neurovascular compromise.
5. Perform and interpret common special tests for the shoulder, hand and wrist, spine, hip, knee and ankle, and know their limitations.
6. Use clinical decision rules (for example the Ottawa ankle rules) to decide on imaging.
7. Recognise the key paediatric musculoskeletal presentations: the limping child and the irritable hip.

---

### 16.1 The musculoskeletal presentation

Musculoskeletal complaints account for a large share of primary care consultations, and most are benign and self-limiting. The diagnostic task is to separate three groups quickly:

1. **The urgent and dangerous:** septic arthritis, fracture or dislocation with neurovascular injury, compartment syndrome, cauda equina syndrome, spinal infection or malignancy, vasculitis (giant cell arteritis), non-accidental injury.
2. **The inflammatory** (rheumatoid arthritis, spondyloarthritis, crystal arthritis, connective tissue disease, polymyalgia rheumatica), which need specific treatment and earlier specialist review.
3. **The mechanical, degenerative or soft-tissue** (osteoarthritis, tendinopathy, bursitis, ligament sprain, non-specific back pain), which usually need reassurance, activity advice, analgesia and physiotherapy.

History does much of the sorting (Chapter 6). Ask about **onset, site, number of joints, pattern (additive, migratory, flitting), morning stiffness and its duration, effect of rest and activity, night pain, swelling, locking or giving way, trauma, fever, systemic features** (rash, eye redness, mouth ulcers, diarrhoea, urethritis, psoriasis, weight loss), **function** (dressing, stairs, work), and **family history.**

| Feature | Inflammatory | Mechanical / degenerative |
|---|---|---|
| Morning stiffness | Prolonged (>30–60 min) | Brief (<30 min) |
| Effect of activity | Improves with movement | Worsens with use, improves with rest |
| Night pain | Common, wakes in the second half of the night | Occurs with position or movement |
| Swelling | Soft tissue and effusion, warmth | Bony, firm; effusions less warm |
| Systemic features | Fatigue, fever, weight loss, raised CRP/ESR | Absent |
| Pattern | Symmetrical small joints (RA); axial and entheses (spondyloarthritis) | Weight-bearing joints, DIPs, first CMC |

### 16.2 General principles of examination

#### Look, feel, move

For every joint, follow the same triad, then add function and special tests:

- **Look:** at rest, compare sides. Scars, skin colour (erythema), swelling, deformity, muscle wasting, posture, alignment, sinus tracts, nail and skin changes (psoriasis).
- **Feel:** temperature (back of the hand, compared with the opposite side), tenderness (joint line vs. tendon vs. bone vs. soft tissue), effusion, synovial thickening ("boggy"), crepitus, nodules, bony swelling, pulses and sensation distal to the joint.
- **Move:** **active** (patient-controlled) first, then **passive** (examiner-controlled), and **resisted** (power). Measure range in degrees against the normal range, noting pain, crepitus and end-feel (hard, springy, empty).
- **Function:** the activities that matter to the patient (gripping, reaching the back, climbing stairs, squatting).
- **Special tests:** after the screening examination.

Always **compare with the contralateral side**, examine the joint **above and below** (hip pathology presents as knee pain, cervical spine as shoulder pain), and note whether pain is **reproduced**.

#### Terminology

- **Arthritis:** inflammation of a joint (swelling, warmth, restricted painful movement).
- **Arthralgia:** joint pain without signs of inflammation.
- **Synovitis:** inflamed synovium, with swelling that is soft or boggy, warm, and often tender along the joint margins.
- **Enthesitis:** inflammation at tendon or ligament insertion (heel, Achilles, plantar fascia).
- **Dactylitis:** "sausage digit" (spondyloarthritis, psoriatic arthritis, sarcoidosis, sickle cell disease).
- **Effusion:** fluid within the joint.

### 16.3 The GALS screen

GALS (Gait, Arms, Legs, Spine) is a rapid screening examination (a few minutes) used to detect musculoskeletal abnormality. It begins with three screening questions:

1. Do you have any pain or stiffness in your muscles, joints or back?
2. Can you dress yourself completely without difficulty?
3. Can you walk up and down stairs without difficulty?

If all three answers are "no" and the screening examination is normal, significant musculoskeletal disease is unlikely. Any "yes" or abnormality leads to a detailed regional examination.

#### Steps (patient in underwear, barefoot)

| Component | What to do | What to look for |
|---|---|---|
| **Gait** | Watch the patient walk, turn and return | Smooth, symmetrical, heel-strike and toe-off, arm swing; antalgic limp, Trendelenburg, high-stepping, shuffling |
| **Spine, standing** (from behind, side and front) | Inspect posture; palpate; lateral flexion | Scoliosis, kyphosis, loss of lordosis, pelvic tilt, muscle spasm, leg length discrepancy, muscle bulk |
| **Spine, bending** | Forward flexion touching toes (normal: lumbar spine flexes and the gap widens by >5 cm on Schober's test) | Reduced lumbar flexion, pain |
| **Cervical spine** | Ear to shoulder; chin to chest; rotation | Reduced range |
| **Arms** | Hands behind the head (shoulder abduction and external rotation); arms stretched forward, palms down then up (elbow, forearm); make a fist, finger-to-thumb (pincer) grip, squeeze the MCP joints (compression test); inspect hands | Swelling, deformity, nodules, muscle wasting, tenderness |
| **Legs, supine** | Passive hip and knee flexion, internal rotation of the hip; knee effusion (patellar tap); squeeze the metatarsal heads; inspect feet and soles | Reduced hip rotation (earliest sign of hip arthritis), crepitus, effusion, foot deformity, callosities |

Findings of significance are then examined in detail, using the regional steps below.

### 16.4 The hand and wrist

#### Look

Dorsum and palm, in good light, resting on a pillow. Note:

- **Rheumatoid arthritis:** symmetrical swelling of the MCP and PIP joints, ulnar deviation, swan-neck and boutonnière deformities, Z-thumb, subcutaneous (rheumatoid) nodules over extensor surfaces, dorsal wrist swelling, wasting of the dorsal interossei, extensor tendon rupture. **Distal interphalangeal joints are typically spared.**
- **Osteoarthritis:** bony nodes at the DIP (Heberden) and PIP (Bouchard) joints, squaring of the first carpometacarpal joint ("squared hand"), with relatively preserved MCPs (except in haemochromatosis arthropathy, which affects the 2nd and 3rd MCP joints).
- **Psoriatic arthritis:** nail pitting, onycholysis, dactylitis, skin plaques, DIP involvement, asymmetrical oligoarthritis, "arthritis mutilans."
- **Gout:** tophi (chalky nodules) on fingers, ears, elbows; acute episodes in the first MTP joint.
- **Systemic sclerosis:** tight shiny skin, sclerodactyly, telangiectasia, calcinosis, digital ulcers, Raynaud phenomenon.
- **Other signs:** palmar erythema, Dupuytren contracture, thenar wasting (carpal tunnel), clubbing, nail-fold infarcts (vasculitis).

#### Feel

Warmth, synovial swelling, joint-line tenderness, nodules. **Squeeze test:** compress across the MCPs; tenderness suggests synovitis. Check the radial and ulnar pulses, capillary refill and sensation.

#### Move

Flexion and extension at each joint; opposition of the thumb; Grip strength. Function: pick up a coin, undo a button, hold a pen.

#### Special tests

| Test | Method | Suggests |
|---|---|---|
| **Phalen** | Flex both wrists fully, backs of hands together, hold 60 seconds | Carpal tunnel syndrome (reproduces tingling in median nerve distribution) |
| **Tinel** | Tap over the carpal tunnel | Carpal tunnel syndrome |
| **Durkan (carpal compression)** | Press over the carpal tunnel for 30 seconds | Carpal tunnel syndrome (often more reliable than Phalen/Tinel) |
| **Finkelstein** | Fist around the thumb, ulnar deviation of the wrist | De Quervain tenosynovitis (pain over the radial styloid) |
| **Scaphoid tenderness** | Pain in the anatomical snuffbox, over the scaphoid tubercle, or with axial thumb compression | Scaphoid fracture (may be occult on early X-ray) |
| **Froment sign** | Hold paper between thumb and index finger; flexion of the thumb interphalangeal joint during pull | Ulnar nerve palsy |

**Carpal tunnel syndrome:** nocturnal paraesthesia in the thumb, index, middle and radial half of the ring finger, relieved by shaking the hand ("flick sign"), with weakness of thumb abduction and thenar wasting in advanced cases. Associations: pregnancy, hypothyroidism, diabetes, rheumatoid arthritis, acromegaly, repetitive use.

### 16.5 The elbow and shoulder

#### Elbow

- **Look:** swelling (olecranon bursitis, effusion: bulge each side of the olecranon), carrying angle (cubitus valgus/varus), rheumatoid nodules, psoriatic plaques, tophi.
- **Feel:** medial and lateral epicondyles, the olecranon, the radial head, the ulnar nerve in the groove.
- **Move:** flexion (0–150°), extension (0°), pronation and supination (about 80–90° each).
- **Tests:** **Lateral epicondylitis (tennis elbow)**: tenderness at the lateral epicondyle, pain on resisted wrist extension with the elbow extended. **Medial epicondylitis (golfer's elbow)**: pain on resisted wrist flexion.

#### Shoulder

Shoulder pain arises from the **glenohumeral joint, the rotator cuff and subacromial space, the acromioclavicular joint, the cervical spine, or referred sources** (diaphragm, heart, apex of the lung). Examine neck movements and consider a cardiac or pulmonary cause.

- **Look:** wasting (deltoid, supraspinatus, infraspinatus), deformity, asymmetry, scapular winging, "squaring" of the shoulder (dislocation).
- **Feel:** sternoclavicular joint, clavicle, AC joint, acromion, greater tuberosity, bicipital groove, scapula.
- **Move (active then passive):** flexion (to 180°), extension (about 60°), abduction (to 180°; the first 15° is by supraspinatus, then deltoid; the scapulohumeral rhythm), adduction, internal rotation (hand up the back to the level of the vertebra), external rotation (elbow at the side).
  - **Painful arc** (60–120° abduction): subacromial impingement.
  - **Pain with both active and passive limitation in all planes**, especially external rotation: **adhesive capsulitis (frozen shoulder)**.
- **Rotator cuff tests:**
  - **Supraspinatus:** "empty can" (Jobe) test: abduct to 90° in the scapular plane, thumbs down, resist downward pressure. Pain or weakness.
  - **Infraspinatus and teres minor:** resisted external rotation with the elbow at the side.
  - **Subscapularis:** resisted internal rotation; "lift-off" test (hand behind the back, lift away).
  - **Impingement:** Neer (passive forward flexion with arm internally rotated) and Hawkins–Kennedy (shoulder flexed to 90° and internally rotated) tests.
  - **Drop arm test:** the patient cannot hold the arm at 90° abduction or lowers it uncontrolled, indicating a large rotator cuff tear.
- **Biceps:** Speed's test (resisted forward flexion with the forearm supinated and elbow extended).
- **Acromioclavicular joint:** tenderness; pain on cross-body adduction.
- **Instability:** apprehension test (abduction and external rotation; apprehension or fear of dislocation).
- **Neurovascular:** axillary nerve (sensation over the deltoid "regimental badge" area, deltoid contraction) after dislocation.

**Do not forget:** shoulder pain in an older patient with fever and elevated markers could be septic arthritis; in a patient with an axial pain, cervical radiculopathy. **Left shoulder tip pain** after trauma suggests splenic injury (Kehr sign). Bilateral shoulder and hip girdle pain and stiffness in a person over 50 suggests **polymyalgia rheumatica** (with raised ESR/CRP, rapid steroid response, and a link to giant cell arteritis).

### 16.6 The spine

#### History

Location, radiation, duration, mechanism, red flags (see below), neurological symptoms (weakness, numbness, bladder, bowel and sexual dysfunction), effect of coughing, sneezing and posture, night pain, morning stiffness, general health.

#### Red flags in back pain (Chapter 72)

- Age <20 or >55 at first onset.
- Significant trauma, or minor trauma in an older or osteoporotic patient.
- **Constant, progressive, non-mechanical pain; thoracic pain; night pain that is unrelieved by rest.**
- **History of cancer; unexplained weight loss.**
- Fever, immunosuppression, injecting drug use, recent infection or procedure.
- Long-term corticosteroid use.
- **Neurological deficit: progressive weakness, saddle (perineal) numbness, urinary retention or incontinence, faecal incontinence, bilateral leg symptoms (cauda equina syndrome).**

#### Examination

- **Look:** posture, scoliosis (ask the patient to bend forward; a rib hump), kyphosis, loss of lumbar lordosis, skin (café-au-lait spots, hairy patch, dimple), muscle spasm, gait.
- **Feel:** midline spinous processes (percussion tenderness suggests infection or fracture), paraspinal muscles, sacroiliac joints, sciatic notch.
- **Move:** flexion, extension, lateral flexion, rotation.
  - **Schober test:** mark the lumbosacral junction (line between the dimples of Venus) and points 10 cm above and 5 cm below; on maximal flexion, the distance between the upper and lower marks should increase from 15 cm to at least 20 cm (an increase of 5 cm). Reduced in ankylosing spondylitis (axial spondyloarthritis).
  - **Chest expansion:** normally ≥5 cm at the 4th intercostal space; reduced in ankylosing spondylitis.
  - **Occiput-to-wall distance** (normally zero).
- **Neural tension tests:**
  - **Straight leg raise (SLR):** passive raising of the extended leg in the supine patient. Positive when radicular pain below the knee is reproduced at 30–70° of elevation (sciatic nerve root irritation, L5/S1; sensitive but not specific). **Crossed SLR** (raising the unaffected leg reproduces pain in the affected leg) is highly specific for disc herniation.
  - **Femoral stretch test:** prone, knee flexion with hip extension; reproduces anterior thigh pain in L2–L4 radiculopathy.
- **Neurological examination of the lower limbs (Chapter 15):** power by myotome (L2 hip flexion; L3 knee extension; L4 ankle dorsiflexion; L5 great toe extension; S1 plantarflexion), sensation by dermatome, reflexes (knee L3/L4; ankle S1), perianal sensation, anal tone (DRE if cauda equina is suspected).
- **Hip and sacroiliac joints:** hip pathology may cause groin, buttock or knee pain; SI joint tests (FABER, pelvic compression).
- **Abdominal and vascular examination:** pulses, abdominal aortic aneurysm, renal angle tenderness.

#### Common spinal syndromes

| Syndrome | Features |
|---|---|
| **Non-specific (mechanical) low back pain** | Pain varying with movement and posture; no red flags; no neurological deficit; good prognosis, most improve in weeks |
| **Lumbar radiculopathy (sciatica)** | Pain radiating below the knee, paraesthesia in dermatomal distribution, positive SLR, motor and reflex changes; L5 (foot drop, great toe extension weakness) and S1 (reduced ankle jerk, weak plantarflexion) are the commonest |
| **Lumbar spinal stenosis** | Neurogenic claudication: bilateral leg pain and heaviness on walking or standing, relieved by sitting or flexing forward; pulses are normal |
| **Cauda equina syndrome** | Bilateral sciatica or leg weakness, saddle anaesthesia, urinary retention or overflow, faecal incontinence, loss of anal tone: **surgical emergency** requiring urgent MRI |
| **Axial spondyloarthritis (e.g. ankylosing spondylitis)** | Young adult (<40), insidious back pain >3 months, morning stiffness, improves with exercise not rest, nocturnal pain in the second half of the night, reduced Schober, sacroiliitis, enthesitis, uveitis, psoriasis, IBD |
| **Vertebral fracture (osteoporotic)** | Sudden pain after minimal trauma; focal tenderness; height loss; kyphosis |
| **Spinal infection (discitis, osteomyelitis, epidural abscess)** | Fever, severe focal back pain, risk factors (injecting drug use, diabetes, immunosuppression, recent procedure), progressive weakness; raised CRP/ESR |
| **Malignancy (metastasis, myeloma)** | Constant, progressive, night pain, history of cancer, weight loss; consider **metastatic spinal cord compression** if neurological symptoms |
| **Cervical radiculopathy / myelopathy** | Neck pain with arm pain/paraesthesia in a dermatomal distribution; myelopathy: clumsy hands, gait disturbance, upper motor neuron signs in the legs, positive Hoffmann reflex |

### 16.7 The hip

#### Look

Gait (antalgic, Trendelenburg), leg length (apparent vs. true), muscle wasting (quadriceps, gluteals), deformity (external rotation and shortening in a fractured neck of femur), scars, skin changes.

#### Feel

Greater trochanter (trochanteric bursitis; tenderness lateral), groin (joint line), inguinal region, adductors.

#### Move

- **Flexion** (~120°), **extension** (~15–20°), **abduction** (~45°), **adduction** (~25–30°), **internal rotation** (~40°, tested with the hip flexed to 90°), **external rotation** (~45°).
- **Reduced internal rotation is the earliest sign of hip arthritis;** pain on internal rotation also suggests irritable hip, slipped capital femoral epiphysis, or hip effusion.

#### Special tests

| Test | Purpose |
|---|---|
| **Thomas test** | Fixed flexion deformity: flex the opposite hip fully to flatten the lumbar lordosis; the tested thigh rises if there is a flexion contracture |
| **Trendelenburg test** | Single-leg stand; the pelvis drops on the unsupported side if the abductors (gluteus medius, L5 nerve, or hip pathology) of the standing leg are weak |
| **Leg length** | True length: ASIS to the medial malleolus; apparent length: umbilicus (or xiphisternum) to the medial malleolus |
| **FABER (Patrick)** | Flexion, abduction, external rotation: groin pain indicates hip pathology; posterior pain suggests sacroiliac joint |
| **Log roll** | Rolling the leg in extension reproduces pain in an intra-articular hip lesion |

**Fractured neck of femur:** the leg is shortened, externally rotated and adducted, with severe pain and inability to bear weight, typically after a fall in an older person. Always consider hip fracture in an elderly person with new hip or groin pain, even if the X-ray is initially normal (occult fracture: MRI).

### 16.8 The knee

#### Look

Standing and lying: swelling (effusion fills the hollows beside the patella), varus ("bow-legged") or valgus ("knock-kneed") deformity, wasting of the quadriceps (vastus medialis wastes early), scars, erythema, popliteal swelling (Baker cyst), prepatellar or infrapatellar bursitis.

#### Feel

Temperature, joint-line tenderness (medial/lateral meniscus), patella, tibial tuberosity (Osgood–Schlatter in adolescents), tendon insertions, popliteal fossa.

#### Effusion tests

- **Patellar tap** (for moderate-to-large effusions): compress the suprapatellar pouch with one hand, tap the patella with the other. A "tap" on the femoral groove indicates fluid.
- **Bulge (sweep or stroke) test** (small effusions): milk fluid medially, then stroke the lateral side and look for a medial bulge.

#### Move

Flexion (~135°), extension (0° to 5° hyperextension). Assess for crepitus (OA: patellofemoral crepitus on movement), locking (mechanical block: bucket-handle meniscal tear, loose body), giving way (instability).

#### Ligament and meniscal tests

| Structure | Test | Positive finding |
|---|---|---|
| **ACL** | **Lachman** (knee flexed 20–30°, anterior pull on the tibia, thigh stabilised) | Increased anterior translation with a soft end-point (most sensitive and specific for ACL rupture); **anterior drawer** (90° flexion) is less sensitive; **pivot shift** |
| **PCL** | **Posterior drawer**; posterior sag sign | Posterior tibial translation |
| **MCL** | **Valgus stress** at 0° and 30° | Medial opening, pain |
| **LCL** | **Varus stress** at 0° and 30° | Lateral opening |
| **Menisci** | **McMurray test** (flex knee fully, rotate the tibia, extend with varus/valgus stress); **Thessaly test**; joint-line tenderness | Painful click or catching; joint-line tenderness is also a useful sign |
| **Patellofemoral** | **Patellar apprehension test**; patellar grind | Fear of subluxation; anterior knee pain |

A **haemarthrosis** (rapid swelling within hours of injury) suggests ACL rupture, patellar dislocation, or intra-articular fracture. **Locked knee** with effusion after a twisting injury suggests a displaced meniscal tear.

**The Ottawa knee rules** (patient aged ≥55, isolated tenderness of the patella, tenderness at the head of the fibula, inability to flex to 90°, inability to bear weight for four steps) help decide when an X-ray is needed.

### 16.9 The ankle and foot

- **Look:** swelling, bruising, deformity, alignment (pes planus, pes cavus, hallux valgus), callosities, skin (ulcers, diabetic foot), nail changes, footwear wear patterns.
- **Feel:** malleoli (lateral, medial), base of the 5th metatarsal, navicular, Achilles tendon (palpable gap in rupture), plantar fascia (heel), metatarsal heads, the first MTP joint, pulses (dorsalis pedis, posterior tibial), sensation.
- **Move:** dorsiflexion (~20°), plantarflexion (~45°), inversion and eversion (subtalar joint), toe movements.
- **Tests:**
  - **Anterior drawer test** (ankle, lateral ligament laxity), **talar tilt.**
  - **Squeeze test** (compress the tibia and fibula above the ankle; pain suggests syndesmotic injury).
  - **Thompson (calf squeeze) test:** with the patient prone and the foot hanging over the edge, squeeze the calf; absent plantarflexion indicates **Achilles tendon rupture.**
- **Ottawa ankle rules:** an ankle X-ray series is required only if there is pain in the malleolar zone **and** any of: bone tenderness at the posterior edge or tip of either malleolus (distal 6 cm), or inability to bear weight (four steps) both immediately and in the emergency department. A foot X-ray series is required if there is pain in the midfoot zone **and** bone tenderness at the base of the 5th metatarsal or the navicular, or inability to bear weight. The rules have very high sensitivity for clinically significant fractures and reduce radiography substantially.
- **Acute gout:** the first MTP joint is red, hot, swollen and exquisitely tender (podagra).
- **Diabetic foot:** examine every patient with diabetes for sensation (10 g monofilament, vibration), pulses, deformity, ulcers, and skin.

### 16.10 The hot, swollen joint: a diagnostic emergency

An acutely hot, swollen, painful joint must be treated as **septic arthritis until proven otherwise,** since delay can destroy cartilage within days and carries a mortality of roughly 10% in adults.

**Features:** severe pain with refusal to move the joint, warmth, effusion, fever (not always present, especially in older or immunosuppressed patients), raised inflammatory markers. Risk factors: prosthetic joint, pre-existing joint disease (RA), diabetes, immunosuppression, injecting drug use, recent joint surgery or injection, skin infection, bacteraemia, age >80. Gonococcal arthritis occurs in young sexually active adults (migratory polyarthralgia, tenosynovitis, pustular skin lesions).

**Action:** **joint aspiration** (before antibiotics where it will not delay treatment) for white cell count, Gram stain, culture, and crystals; blood cultures; urgent orthopaedic input; IV antibiotics; joint washout where indicated.

#### Differential of the acute monoarthritis

| Diagnosis | Clues |
|---|---|
| **Septic arthritis** | Fever, systemic upset, risk factors; synovial fluid with high leukocyte count (often >50,000/µL, but lower values do not exclude infection), organisms |
| **Gout** | Middle-aged or older man (or postmenopausal woman), first MTP, alcohol, diuretics, renal impairment; **needle-shaped, negatively birefringent monosodium urate crystals** |
| **Pseudogout (CPPD)** | Older patient, knee or wrist; chondrocalcinosis on X-ray; **rhomboid, weakly positively birefringent crystals** |
| **Haemarthrosis** | Trauma, anticoagulants, haemophilia |
| **Reactive arthritis** | Following urethritis or gastroenteritis; asymmetrical lower limb oligoarthritis, conjunctivitis, urethritis |
| **Early inflammatory arthritis** | RA, psoriatic arthritis, spondyloarthritis |
| **Trauma/fracture, haemarthrosis** | History |
| **Lyme arthritis** | Endemic region, tick exposure |

**Note:** Gout and infection can coexist; crystals in the fluid do not exclude infection.

### 16.11 Polyarticular patterns

| Pattern | Likely diagnoses |
|---|---|
| **Symmetrical small-joint polyarthritis (MCP, PIP, wrists), morning stiffness >1 hour** | Rheumatoid arthritis (RF, anti-CCP positive in many), SLE, viral arthritis (parvovirus B19, hepatitis B, rubella) |
| **Asymmetrical oligoarthritis, DIP involvement, dactylitis, nail changes, psoriasis** | Psoriatic arthritis |
| **Lower-limb asymmetrical oligoarthritis after infection with eye and urethral symptoms** | Reactive arthritis |
| **Bony enlargement of DIPs, PIPs, first CMC, knees, hips; "wear and tear"** | Osteoarthritis |
| **Axial pain and stiffness, young adult, sacroiliitis** | Axial spondyloarthritis |
| **Proximal pain and stiffness in age >50 with raised ESR** | Polymyalgia rheumatica (ask about headache, scalp tenderness, jaw claudication: giant cell arteritis) |
| **Widespread pain, fatigue, poor sleep, tender points, normal investigations** | Fibromyalgia (diagnosis of exclusion with positive features) |
| **Joint pain with rash, photosensitivity, mouth ulcers, serositis, renal disease** | SLE |
| **Joint pain with fever, rash, lymphadenopathy** | Infections, adult-onset Still's disease, drug reactions |
| **Hypermobile joints with pain** | Joint hypermobility spectrum, Ehlers–Danlos syndrome (Beighton score) |

### 16.12 The musculoskeletal system in children

- **The limping child:** broad differential depending on age.
  - **Toddlers (1–3 years):** septic arthritis, transient synovitis, osteomyelitis, fracture ("toddler's fracture" of the tibia), developmental dysplasia (DDH), neoplasia, non-accidental injury.
  - **Children (4–10):** **transient synovitis (irritable hip)** commonest, Perthes disease, septic arthritis, juvenile idiopathic arthritis, leukaemia, trauma.
  - **Adolescents (10–16):** **slipped capital femoral epiphysis (SCFE),** typically in an overweight boy with hip or referred knee pain, with the leg externally rotated and reduced internal rotation; sports injuries (Osgood–Schlatter, Sever disease), tumours.
- **Kocher criteria** for septic arthritis in a child with an irritable hip: non-weight-bearing, fever >38.5 °C, ESR >40 mm/h, WBC >12,000/µL. With 3–4 criteria the probability is high.
- **Developmental dysplasia of the hip:** screening in neonates (Barlow and Ortolani manoeuvres), asymmetric skin folds, limited abduction, apparent leg-length difference; ultrasound.
- **Non-accidental injury:** fractures inconsistent with the history or developmental stage, multiple fractures of different ages, rib fractures in infants, spiral fractures of long bones in non-ambulant children, bruising in unusual sites, delay in presenting. Take a careful history, document precisely, and follow safeguarding procedures (Chapter 59).
- **Never dismiss "growing pains" without a careful examination** (night pain, limp, systemic features, or unilateral pain suggest another cause).

### 16.13 Emergencies and neurovascular compromise

- **Compartment syndrome:** pain out of proportion to the injury, **pain on passive stretching of the muscles in the compartment,** tense swollen compartment, paraesthesia; **pulselessness is a very late sign.** Follows fractures (tibia, forearm), crush injury, tight casts, reperfusion. Treat by releasing constrictive dressings and urgent fasciotomy.
- **Acute limb ischaemia:** pain, pallor, pulselessness, paraesthesia, paralysis, perishing cold (Chapter 12).
- **Open fracture:** requires urgent antibiotics, irrigation and debridement; tetanus prophylaxis.
- **Dislocations (shoulder, hip, knee, patella):** neurovascular check before and after reduction; knee dislocation has a high risk of popliteal artery injury.
- **Cauda equina syndrome and spinal cord compression:** see 16.6.
- **Necrotising fasciitis:** pain out of proportion to skin changes, rapid progression, systemic toxicity, crepitus; a surgical emergency (Chapter 18).
- **Osteomyelitis:** bone pain, fever, tenderness, raised markers; children (metaphysis), diabetics (foot), injecting drug users (vertebrae).

### 16.14 Worked cases

#### Case 1: Hot swollen knee

*A 67-year-old woman with rheumatoid arthritis on methotrexate and low-dose prednisolone has had 2 days of a swollen, hot, intensely painful right knee. She cannot bear weight. Temperature 38.1 °C, pulse 104. The knee has a large effusion, is erythematous and warm, and any movement is very painful.*

**Reasoning.** An acute monoarthritis in an immunosuppressed patient with a pre-existing joint disease is **septic arthritis until proven otherwise**; fever may be blunted by steroids. Differential: crystal arthritis, an RA flare, haemarthrosis.

**Next steps.** Urgent joint aspiration (cell count, Gram stain, culture, crystals), blood cultures, full blood count, CRP, urgent orthopaedic referral, IV antibiotics after aspiration (do not delay if the patient is septic), joint washout. The immunosuppressed patient needs particular care.

#### Case 2: Back pain with red flags

*A 58-year-old man with prostate cancer treated five years ago has had 3 weeks of progressive thoracic back pain, worse at night, and 2 days of leg weakness and unsteadiness. Examination shows tenderness over T9, spastic weakness of both legs, brisk reflexes and extensor plantar responses, and a sensory level at T10. He has urinary hesitancy.*

**Reasoning.** Progressive night pain, history of malignancy, and new upper motor neuron signs with a sensory level and sphincter symptoms: **metastatic spinal cord compression** until proven otherwise.

**Action.** Same-day MRI of the whole spine, high-dose dexamethasone as per local protocol, urgent oncology/spinal surgery liaison, flat bed rest with log-roll precautions if there is spinal instability.

#### Case 3: Sciatica

*A 40-year-old warehouse worker has had 10 days of sharp low back pain radiating down the posterolateral right leg to the foot, worse on coughing. Straight leg raise reproduces his leg pain at 40°; he has mild weakness of right great toe extension and reduced sensation on the dorsum of the foot; the ankle jerk is normal. No bladder or bowel symptoms, no saddle numbness.*

**Reasoning.** L5 radiculopathy, probably from a lumbar disc herniation (L4/5). No cauda equina features. Most patients improve over 6–12 weeks.

**Management.** Reassurance, analgesia (neuropathic agents if needed), staying active, physiotherapy; safety-net advice for cauda equina symptoms (saddle numbness, bladder or bowel change, bilateral symptoms) to return urgently; MRI and spinal referral if severe, progressive or persistent deficit, or if pain does not improve.

#### Case 4: Painful swollen big toe

*A 56-year-old man wakes at 3 a.m. with an intensely painful, red, swollen right first MTP joint. He drinks beer regularly, takes a thiazide for hypertension, and had a large meal the previous evening. Temperature 37.8 °C. The joint is too tender to touch, with overlying shiny skin.*

**Reasoning.** Classic **acute gout** (podagra). Risk factors: alcohol, diuretics, hypertension, male sex, renal impairment, obesity. Septic arthritis must be considered if there is fever and systemic upset, particularly with a prosthesis or risk factors.

**Next steps.** If the diagnosis is uncertain, aspirate for crystals (negatively birefringent needles) and culture. Treat with an NSAID (if no contraindication), colchicine, or a short course of corticosteroids; defer urate-lowering therapy until the flare has settled, with prophylaxis when starting it. Review contributing factors (thiazide, alcohol, diet), check renal function and serum urate.

#### Case 5: Twisting knee injury

*A 24-year-old footballer pivoted and heard a "pop" in the left knee. Rapid swelling within an hour, unable to continue playing, the knee feels unstable. On examination: a large effusion, positive Lachman test with a soft end-point.*

**Reasoning.** An immediate haemarthrosis after a twisting injury with a "pop" and instability: **ACL rupture** (often with a meniscal or collateral ligament injury). Lachman is more sensitive than the anterior drawer.

**Next steps.** Rest, ice, compression, elevation, analgesia, crutches; Ottawa knee rules or X-ray for fracture, MRI, orthopaedic or sports medicine referral for reconstruction or rehabilitation decisions.

#### Case 6: The limping adolescent

*A 13-year-old overweight boy has had 3 weeks of vague left knee pain and a limp. The knee is normal on examination. The left leg lies in external rotation; hip flexion causes the thigh to rotate externally; internal rotation is painful and markedly limited.*

**Reasoning.** Knee pain referred from the hip in an obese adolescent with external rotation and limited internal rotation: **slipped capital femoral epiphysis** until proven otherwise. A missed diagnosis may result in avascular necrosis.

**Action.** Stop weight bearing (crutches), urgent orthopaedic referral, AP and frog-leg lateral hip X-rays (or lateral view). Examine both hips (bilateral in up to a quarter).

### 16.15 Common pitfalls

- **Examining only the painful joint.** Check the joint above and below (hip for knee pain; cervical spine for shoulder pain).
- **Missing septic arthritis** in the immunosuppressed, elderly or those who are afebrile.
- **Relying on imaging** to explain pain: degenerative changes on X-ray or MRI are common in asymptomatic people.
- **Missing the red flags in back pain.**
- **Not testing power, sensation and reflexes** in patients with back or neck pain.
- **Relying on a normal early X-ray** to exclude a scaphoid, hip or stress fracture.
- **Treating gout without considering infection,** or vice versa.
- **Failing to recognise referred pain** (cardiac pain in the arm and jaw, diaphragmatic pain in the shoulder, hip pain in the knee).
- **Mistaking "growing pains" in children** without careful examination.
- **Missing non-accidental injury.**
- **Waiting for pulselessness** to diagnose compartment syndrome.

---

### 16.16 Key points

- GALS is a rapid screen; any abnormality prompts a regional examination.
- Use look, feel, move, function and special tests; compare sides and examine the joint above and below.
- Distinguish inflammatory from mechanical disease by morning stiffness, effect of activity, night pain and systemic features.
- A hot swollen joint is septic arthritis until proved otherwise: aspirate, culture, treat.
- Know the red flags in back pain; cauda equina syndrome and cord compression need emergency MRI.
- Use clinical rules (Ottawa) to avoid unnecessary imaging; know that early X-rays may miss scaphoid and femoral neck fractures.
- In children, consider SCFE, septic arthritis, Perthes disease and non-accidental injury in a limping child.
- Compartment syndrome is clinical: pain on passive stretch, out-of-proportion pain; pulselessness is late.

### 16.17 Self-assessment

1. What does GALS stand for, and what are the three screening questions?
   *Answer: Gait, Arms, Legs, Spine. (1) Any pain or stiffness in muscles, joints or back? (2) Can you dress without difficulty? (3) Can you manage stairs without difficulty?*

2. Describe the Lachman test and what it detects.
   *Answer: With the knee flexed 20–30° and the thigh stabilised, pull the tibia forward; increased anterior translation with a soft end-point indicates ACL rupture.*

3. Which crystal and which birefringence characterise gout and pseudogout?
   *Answer: Gout: needle-shaped, negatively birefringent monosodium urate; pseudogout: rhomboid, weakly positively birefringent calcium pyrophosphate.*

4. List four red flags for serious spinal pathology.
   *Answer (any four): age <20 or >55, history of cancer, weight loss, fever or immunosuppression, night or constant pain, trauma, neurological deficit, saddle anaesthesia, bladder/bowel dysfunction, injecting drug use.*

5. According to the Ottawa ankle rules, when is an ankle X-ray indicated?
   *Answer: Malleolar-zone pain plus bone tenderness at the posterior edge or tip of either malleolus (distal 6 cm) or inability to bear weight for four steps.*

6. A 12-year-old overweight boy has knee pain and a limp. Which joint do you examine, and which diagnosis must you exclude?
   *Answer: The hip (referred pain); slipped capital femoral epiphysis.*
