# Chapter 51. Neurology

## Learning objectives

After studying this chapter you should be able to:

1. Localise a neurological problem (where is the lesion?) and then name its likely cause (what is the lesion?) using tempo of onset and pattern of deficit.
2. Diagnose ischaemic and haemorrhagic stroke and TIA, apply acute stroke time windows, and plan secondary prevention.
3. Classify seizures, distinguish epileptic seizures from mimics, and choose first-line treatment with attention to women of childbearing potential.
4. Recognise the main peripheral neuropathy patterns and run a proportionate work-up, including the emergencies (Guillain–Barré syndrome, cord and cauda equina compression).
5. Diagnose dementia and its main subtypes, separate it from delirium and depression, and exclude reversible causes.
6. Diagnose Parkinson disease and recognise parkinsonism that is not Parkinson disease.
7. Manage migraine, and recognise other neurological conditions encountered in general practice: multiple sclerosis, myasthenia gravis, motor neuron disease, normal pressure hydrocephalus, and functional neurological disorder.

> **Note on thresholds and doses.** Time windows, doses and drug choices follow commonly used guidelines (AHA/ASA, ESO, NICE, ILAE, AAN, MDS) at the time of writing. They change often in neurology. Check your national stroke and epilepsy protocols before acting on any number printed here.

## 1. The neurological method: where, then what

### 1.1 Localise first

Every neurological presentation is two questions. **Where** is the lesion? **What** is the pathology? The first is answered by the examination (chapter 15), the second by the tempo and the context.

| Level | Typical features |
|---|---|
| Cortex | Aphasia, neglect, visual field defect, apraxia, seizures, cortical sensory loss |
| Subcortical / internal capsule | Pure motor or pure sensory hemiparesis affecting face, arm and leg together |
| Brainstem | Crossed signs (cranial nerve on one side, long tract on the other), diplopia, vertigo, dysarthria, dysphagia, altered consciousness |
| Cerebellum | Ataxia, intention tremor, nystagmus, dysarthria, dysmetria |
| Basal ganglia | Bradykinesia, rigidity, rest tremor, chorea, dystonia |
| Spinal cord | Sensory level, spastic paraparesis, sphincter disturbance, brisk reflexes below the lesion |
| Cauda equina / root | Lower motor neuron signs in a root distribution, saddle numbness, retention, reduced reflexes |
| Peripheral nerve | Glove-and-stocking or nerve-territory deficit, reduced reflexes |
| Neuromuscular junction | Fatigable weakness, ocular and bulbar involvement, normal sensation and reflexes |
| Muscle | Proximal symmetrical weakness, normal sensation, reflexes preserved until late |

Useful rules:

- **Upper vs lower motor neuron.** Spasticity, hyperreflexia, extensor plantar and little wasting: upper. Flaccidity, wasting, fasciculations and absent reflexes: lower.
- **A single lesion** explains one syndrome. When the findings need two lesions, think multifocal disease (multiple sclerosis, vasculitis, embolic stroke, metastases, mononeuritis multiplex) or a diffuse process (metabolic, toxic, neuromuscular).
- **Face, arm and leg all weak on one side**: think contralateral hemisphere or capsule. **Face on one side, limbs on the other**: brainstem.
- **Bilateral leg weakness with a sensory level and sphincter loss**: the spinal cord, until proven otherwise.

### 1.2 Then use tempo

| Tempo | Typical causes |
|---|---|
| Seconds to minutes | Vascular (stroke, TIA), seizure, syncope, migraine aura (builds over minutes, marches) |
| Hours to days | Infection, inflammation (Guillain–Barré, optic neuritis, transverse myelitis), toxic/metabolic, haemorrhage |
| Weeks to months | Tumour, subacute infection (TB, fungal), autoimmune, some demyelination |
| Months to years | Neurodegeneration (dementia, Parkinson, motor neuron disease), slowly growing tumours, hereditary |
| Relapsing and remitting | Multiple sclerosis, migraine, epilepsy, myasthenia gravis, periodic paralysis |

A deficit that is **maximal at onset** points to a vascular event. One that **spreads over minutes** suggests migraine or a focal seizure. One that **evolves over days** suggests inflammation or infection.

### 1.3 Red flags that change urgency

- Sudden focal deficit, speech disturbance, facial droop, visual loss.
- Thunderclap headache.
- Headache with fever, neck stiffness, rash or reduced consciousness.
- New neurological deficit with papilloedema or progressive headache.
- Back pain with leg weakness, saddle numbness or sphincter disturbance.
- Rapidly ascending weakness, breathlessness, or bulbar involvement.
- Status epilepticus or a first seizure with persisting abnormality.
- Acute confusion.

These cases are covered in chapters 24, 33 and 41. This chapter focuses on diagnosis and management of the major disease groups.

## 2. Cerebrovascular disease

### 2.1 Stroke and TIA: definitions

- **Stroke**: sudden focal (rarely global) neurological dysfunction of presumed vascular origin lasting more than 24 hours, or with imaging evidence of infarction or haemorrhage, regardless of duration.
- **TIA**: transient episode of neurological dysfunction caused by focal ischaemia without acute infarction on imaging (tissue-based definition). Most last minutes; a TIA that lasts longer than 1 hour is rare and often has infarction on MRI.

Around 85% of strokes are ischaemic, 10–13% intracerebral haemorrhage, and about 3% subarachnoid haemorrhage.

### 2.2 Ischaemic stroke syndromes

| Territory | Features |
|---|---|
| Anterior circulation (MCA, ACA, ICA) | Contralateral hemiparesis (face and arm greater than leg in MCA), hemisensory loss, homonymous hemianopia, dominant hemisphere aphasia, non-dominant neglect, gaze deviation toward the lesion; ACA: leg weakness greater than arm, abulia, incontinence |
| Posterior circulation (vertebrobasilar, PCA) | Vertigo, diplopia, ataxia, crossed signs, dysphagia, bilateral or alternating weakness, homonymous hemianopia (PCA), reduced consciousness (basilar) |
| Lacunar | Pure motor, pure sensory, sensorimotor, ataxic hemiparesis, dysarthria–clumsy hand; no cortical signs |
| Retinal | Transient monocular visual loss (amaurosis fugax: a "curtain") |

Mechanisms (TOAST): large artery atherosclerosis, cardioembolism (atrial fibrillation, recent MI, valve disease, endocarditis, patent foramen ovale in the young), small vessel disease, other determined cause (dissection, vasculitis, hypercoagulable state, moyamoya, CADASIL), and undetermined. In people under 50, think dissection, PFO with paradoxical embolism, cardiac sources, drugs (cocaine, amphetamines), thrombophilia, migraine, pregnancy and puerperium, and sickle cell disease.

### 2.3 Recognising stroke quickly

Use a validated tool: **FAST** (face, arms, speech, time), **BE-FAST** (adds balance and eyes), or a prehospital scale such as the Cincinnati scale. Posterior stroke is missed more often. Then the NIHSS to quantify deficit (0–42; higher means more severe).

**Mimics** are 20–30% of suspected strokes: hypoglycaemia (always check capillary glucose first), seizure with post-ictal Todd paralysis, migraine with aura, functional disorder, sepsis with recrudescence of old deficits, brain tumour, subdural haematoma, peripheral vestibulopathy, hypotension, and metabolic encephalopathy. A clinician who is unsure should treat as stroke and let imaging sort it out, but never skip the glucose.

### 2.4 Acute imaging and reperfusion

Immediate brain imaging is mandatory. **Non-contrast CT** excludes haemorrhage; **CT angiography** shows large vessel occlusion; **CT perfusion or MRI (DWI-FLAIR mismatch)** selects patients in later time windows. MRI with DWI is far more sensitive than CT for early infarction, especially in the posterior fossa.

**Intravenous thrombolysis** (alteplase 0.9 mg/kg, maximum 90 mg, 10% as bolus and the rest over 60 minutes, or tenecteplase 0.25 mg/kg, maximum 25 mg, single bolus, where approved):

- Standard window: within 4.5 hours of symptom onset (or last known well).
- Extended window: selected patients with perfusion or DWI-FLAIR mismatch beyond 4.5 hours or with wake-up stroke.
- Absolute contraindications include intracranial haemorrhage on imaging, recent major surgery or trauma, active internal bleeding, and uncontrolled blood pressure (above 185/110 mmHg before treatment; lower it first). Therapeutic anticoagulation with an elevated INR or recent DOAC are major concerns; follow local protocol. Mild or rapidly improving deficits that are not disabling usually do not warrant lysis; disabling deficits do, however small the NIHSS.
- Benefit is time-dependent: **time is brain**. About 1.9 million neurons are lost per minute in a large vessel stroke. Aim for door-to-needle under 60 minutes (best centres under 30).

**Mechanical thrombectomy** for anterior circulation large-vessel occlusion (ICA, M1, proximal M2) within 6 hours, and up to 24 hours in selected patients on perfusion imaging; evidence is also strong for large core infarcts and expanding for basilar occlusion. Give thrombolysis first if eligible; do not delay thrombectomy to wait for lysis response. Transfer rapidly to a thrombectomy-capable centre.

### 2.5 Acute supportive care

- **Admit to a stroke unit.** Organised stroke unit care reduces death and dependency more than any single drug.
- Blood pressure: do not lower routinely in the first 24–48 hours unless extremely high (above 220/120 mmHg) or if lysis is planned; hypotension is harmful.
- Glucose 7.8–10 mmol/L (140–180 mg/dL); treat hypoglycaemia and marked hyperglycaemia.
- Oxygen only if saturation is below about 94%.
- Temperature: treat fever and look for infection.
- **Swallow screen before any oral intake**; aspiration pneumonia is the commonest cause of early death.
- Early mobilisation after 24 hours (avoid very early high-dose mobilisation), DVT prevention with intermittent pneumatic compression, nutrition, bladder care, mood and cognition screening, rehabilitation from day 1.
- Antiplatelet therapy: aspirin 300 mg within 24–48 hours (after 24 hours if lysis given and after repeat imaging). In minor stroke or high-risk TIA (ABCD2 ≥ 4 or NIHSS ≤ 3), short-term **dual antiplatelet therapy** (aspirin plus clopidogrel, for 21 days, or ticagrelor plus aspirin for 30 days) started within 24 hours reduces recurrence.
- Decompressive hemicraniectomy for malignant MCA infarction in selected patients under 60 (and carefully selected older patients).
- Look for complications: haemorrhagic transformation, brain oedema, seizures, depression, pressure sores, shoulder pain.

### 2.6 TIA: urgent evaluation

A TIA is a stroke warning. The 90-day stroke risk is roughly 10% and about half occurs in the first 48 hours. Treat as an emergency: same-day specialist assessment (or at least within 24 hours). Workup:

- Brain imaging (MRI with DWI preferred), vascular imaging of head and neck (carotid duplex, CTA or MRA), ECG, prolonged rhythm monitoring (the yield rises with duration; consider implantable loop recorders in cryptogenic stroke), echocardiography when indicated, blood tests (glucose, lipids, FBC, ESR/CRP in older patients, thrombophilia screening in selected young patients).
- **Carotid stenosis** 70–99% (NASCET) symptomatic: carotid endarterectomy within 2 weeks of the event. 50–69% in men with a recent event: benefit, with individual assessment.
- Start antiplatelet therapy immediately after exclusion of haemorrhage.

### 2.7 Secondary prevention

- **Blood pressure**: target below 130/80 mmHg for most; begin after the acute phase.
- **Antithrombotic**: single antiplatelet long term (clopidogrel or aspirin; or aspirin plus dipyridamole); anticoagulation for atrial fibrillation (DOAC preferred; start from about 2–14 days depending on infarct size, with early start supported by recent trials).
- **Statin** (high intensity; LDL target below 1.8 mmol/L, or 1.4 mmol/L in very high risk patients; add ezetimibe if needed).
- Lifestyle: smoking cessation, Mediterranean diet, exercise, moderate alcohol, weight control.
- Diabetes control, treatment of sleep apnoea, PFO closure in carefully selected patients 18–60 with cryptogenic stroke and high-risk PFO features.

### 2.8 Intracerebral haemorrhage (ICH)

Sudden headache, vomiting, rapidly progressive deficit, decreased consciousness, markedly raised blood pressure; non-contrast CT shows hyperdense blood. Causes: hypertension (basal ganglia, thalamus, pons, cerebellum), cerebral amyloid angiopathy (lobar, elderly, recurrent), anticoagulants, AVM or aneurysm, tumour, venous thrombosis, drugs.

Management: reverse anticoagulation immediately (prothrombin complex concentrate and vitamin K for warfarin; idarucizumab for dabigatran; andexanet alfa or PCC for factor Xa inhibitors), lower systolic blood pressure promptly (target about 140 mmHg, avoiding large swings), treat in a stroke or neurocritical unit, neurosurgical consult for cerebellar haemorrhage with brainstem compression or hydrocephalus (often an emergency), and consider evacuation in selected superficial lobar haematomas. Avoid starting antithrombotics for some weeks; individualise.

### 2.9 Subarachnoid haemorrhage (SAH)

Thunderclap headache ("worst of my life", peak within seconds to a minute), often with vomiting, neck stiffness, photophobia, brief loss of consciousness, focal signs (third nerve palsy from posterior communicating aneurysm). Non-contrast CT within 6 hours of onset has sensitivity close to 100% when read by a trained radiologist on a modern scanner and obtained early; beyond 6 hours, sensitivity falls and **lumbar puncture** (xanthochromia, at least 12 hours after onset) is required if CT is negative and suspicion remains. CT angiography locates the aneurysm. Treatment: secure the aneurysm (coiling or clipping) in a neurosurgical centre, nimodipine 60 mg every 4 hours for 21 days, manage hydrocephalus, avoid hypotension. Complications: rebleeding, vasospasm and delayed ischaemia, hyponatraemia, hydrocephalus.

### 2.10 Cerebral venous thrombosis

Consider in young women (pregnancy, puerperium, oral contraceptives), prothrombotic states, infection, or dehydration, presenting with headache (often progressive), seizures, focal deficits, papilloedema, and encephalopathy. CT venography or MR venography. Treat with anticoagulation (LMWH then warfarin or DOAC), even with haemorrhagic venous infarction.

## 3. Epilepsy and seizures

### 3.1 Definitions

- **Seizure**: transient occurrence of signs or symptoms due to abnormal excessive or synchronous neuronal activity.
- **Epilepsy**: at least two unprovoked seizures more than 24 hours apart; or one unprovoked seizure with a recurrence risk of at least 60% over 10 years (e.g., MRI lesion, epileptiform EEG, remote structural cause); or an epilepsy syndrome diagnosis (ILAE 2014).
- **Acute symptomatic (provoked) seizures**: occur in close relation to an acute insult (stroke, trauma, infection, metabolic disturbance, drug or alcohol withdrawal, hypoglycaemia). These do not equal epilepsy.

### 3.2 Classification

**Focal onset** (awareness preserved or impaired; motor or non-motor onset; may evolve to bilateral tonic–clonic). **Generalised onset** (tonic–clonic, absence, myoclonic, atonic). **Unknown onset.** Describe the first sign: it localises. An aura (déjà vu, rising epigastric sensation, olfactory hallucination) suggests temporal lobe onset; jerking of one hand, Jacksonian march, suggests motor cortex.

### 3.3 Taking a history of a "funny turn"

A witness account is the single most valuable part. Ask:

- Circumstances: prior sleep deprivation, alcohol, standing up, pain, emotional stress, flashing lights.
- Prodrome: aura, palpitations, lightheadedness, pallor.
- Events: onset, eye deviation, automatisms, stiffening, rhythmic jerking, duration, cyanosis, tongue biting (lateral), incontinence (not specific), recovery time and confusion.

| Feature | Favours epileptic seizure | Favours syncope | Favours functional (psychogenic nonepileptic) seizure |
|---|---|---|---|
| Trigger | Sleep deprivation, flashing lights, none | Standing, heat, pain, micturition, cough | Stress, emotional triggers, being observed |
| Onset | Aura, abrupt | Gradual lightheadedness, vision dimming | Gradual, often long |
| Duration of unresponsiveness | 1–3 minutes, post-ictal confusion for minutes–hours | Seconds; rapid recovery | Often over 2–5 minutes, fluctuating |
| Movement | Stiffening then rhythmic jerking, in-phase | Brief, irregular myoclonic jerks after the faint | Asynchronous, side-to-side head movement, pelvic thrusting, eyes tightly closed, ictal crying |
| Tongue bite | Lateral | Tip, rare | Tip, rare |
| Post-ictal | Confusion, drowsiness | Quick recovery, pallor | Often rapid recovery, possibly emotional |

Convulsive syncope (brief jerks after loss of consciousness from cerebral hypoperfusion) is a classic mimic. Always check ECG for long QT, Brugada, pre-excitation and conduction disease.

### 3.4 Investigations after a first seizure

- Bedside: glucose, sodium, calcium, magnesium, creatinine, liver function, toxicology if indicated, pregnancy test, ECG.
- **EEG**: ideally within 24–72 hours after a first seizure (higher yield when early; sleep-deprived recordings improve it); a normal routine EEG does not exclude epilepsy (sensitivity about 50% for a single recording), and an abnormal EEG in the right context supports it. Do not use EEG to diagnose epilepsy in a patient with a non-epileptic history.
- **MRI brain** (epilepsy protocol) in all adults with a first unprovoked seizure and focal onset, and in all with epilepsy unless clearly idiopathic generalised.
- Lumbar puncture if infection or autoimmune encephalitis suspected.
- Driving advice: patients must not drive and should follow national regulations (in many European countries, 6–12 months seizure-free after a first unprovoked seizure; longer for epilepsy; commercial licences more restrictive).

### 3.5 Treatment

Start antiseizure medication (ASM) after a second seizure, or after a first seizure when the risk of recurrence is high or the consequences of a second seizure are serious (patient's choice after counselling).

| Seizure type | First-line options | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Focal | Lamotrigine, levetiracetam, carbamazepine, oxcarbazepine, lacosamide | Lamotrigine and levetiracetam are well tolerated; carbamazepine is effective but interacts widely |
| Generalised tonic–clonic | Lamotrigine, levetiracetam, valproate | **Valproate must be avoided in women and girls of childbearing potential** unless no alternative and a pregnancy prevention programme is in place (teratogenicity, neurodevelopmental delay) |
| Absence | Ethosuximide, valproate (men), lamotrigine | Ethosuximide is superior to lamotrigine in childhood absence |
| Myoclonic (JME) | Levetiracetam, valproate (men), lamotrigine (may worsen myoclonus in some) | Avoid carbamazepine, oxcarbazepine, phenytoin (worsen generalised seizures) |

Principles: monotherapy first, titrate slowly, aim for seizure freedom with tolerable side effects; about 60–70% become seizure-free with the first or second drug; after two failed drugs, refer for specialist evaluation including consideration of surgery (temporal lobe resection, laser ablation, vagus nerve stimulation). Teach: adherence, trigger avoidance, sleep, alcohol limits, drug interactions (enzyme-inducing ASMs reduce the efficacy of combined oral contraceptives and some DOACs), and folic acid for women planning pregnancy (higher dose recommended on enzyme-inducing drugs per local guidance). Counsel on safety (showers, swimming alone, heights), SUDEP (risk reduced by seizure control and night-time supervision).

**Status epilepticus** is covered in chapter 41. Remember the sequence: benzodiazepine within 5 minutes, second-line IV levetiracetam, valproate or fosphenytoin, and ICU for refractory cases.

## 4. Peripheral neuropathies and related conditions

### 4.1 Pattern recognition

| Pattern | Examples |
|---|---|
| Symmetrical distal sensory-predominant polyneuropathy | Diabetes, alcohol, B12 deficiency, hypothyroidism, uraemia, chemotherapy (platinum, taxanes, vincristine), drugs (isoniazid, metronidazole, amiodarone, nitrofurantoin), monoclonal gammopathy, HIV, hereditary (CMT) |
| Motor-predominant | Guillain–Barré, CIDP, multifocal motor neuropathy, CMT, lead, porphyria, diphtheria |
| Small fibre | Burning pain, autonomic symptoms, normal nerve conduction: diabetes, impaired glucose tolerance, amyloid, Fabry disease, Sjögren |
| Mononeuropathy | Carpal tunnel, ulnar neuropathy at the elbow, radial palsy ("Saturday night"), common peroneal palsy, Bell palsy |
| Mononeuritis multiplex | Vasculitis (ANCA, polyarteritis nodosa), diabetes, leprosy, sarcoid, cryoglobulinaemia, lymphoma |
| Radiculopathy | Disc herniation, spondylosis, herpes zoster, diabetes (diabetic amyotrophy), Lyme |
| Plexopathy | Brachial neuritis (Parsonage–Turner), tumour (Pancoast), radiation, diabetes |

### 4.2 Work-up of a symmetrical polyneuropathy

Take a thorough history: onset and tempo, family history, alcohol, drugs, toxins, diet (vegan, bariatric surgery), systemic disease, travel. Examine for foot deformity (pes cavus), reflexes, vibration and proprioception, autonomic signs, skin changes, and thickened nerves.

First-line tests (a pragmatic set resolves most cases): glucose/HbA1c (and oral glucose tolerance test in idiopathic cases), vitamin B12 (with methylmalonic acid if borderline), folate, TSH, renal and liver function, FBC, ESR/CRP, serum protein electrophoresis and free light chains, HIV and hepatitis serology when risk factors exist. Nerve conduction studies and EMG distinguish axonal from demyelinating, motor from sensory, and focal from generalised; no cause is found in up to 20–30%.

**Management**: treat the cause, optimise glycaemic control, protect insensate feet (diabetic foot care), stop offending drugs, replace deficiencies, physiotherapy and falls prevention. Neuropathic pain: first-line options are amitriptyline (10–75 mg at night), duloxetine (60 mg), gabapentin or pregabalin (renally adjust); topical capsaicin or lidocaine for localised pain; avoid opioids; tramadol only as short-term rescue.

### 4.3 Focal entrapment neuropathies

**Carpal tunnel syndrome**: nocturnal paraesthesia in the thumb, index and middle fingers, often waking the patient, relieved by shaking the hand ("flick sign"). Thenar wasting and weakness of abductor pollicis brevis in advanced cases. Phalen and Tinel tests have modest accuracy; nerve conduction studies confirm. Causes: pregnancy, hypothyroidism, diabetes, rheumatoid arthritis, acromegaly, amyloid. Treatment: night wrist splints, steroid injection, surgical release for persistent or severe disease.

**Ulnar neuropathy at the elbow**: numbness of the little and ring finger, weak grip and finger abduction, positive Froment sign. **Common peroneal neuropathy at the fibular head**: foot drop with sensory loss over the dorsum of the foot; look for leg crossing, weight loss, plaster casts. Distinguish from L5 radiculopathy (weak inversion and back pain) and from a central cause.

### 4.4 Guillain–Barré syndrome (GBS)

An acute immune-mediated polyradiculoneuropathy, often 1–3 weeks after infection (*Campylobacter jejuni*, cytomegalovirus, Epstein–Barr, Zika, COVID-19 in some series, vaccines in very rare cases).

- **Presentation**: symmetrical ascending weakness over hours to days (maximal at 4 weeks), distal paraesthesia, areflexia, back or limb pain. Cranial nerves (especially facial weakness), autonomic instability (arrhythmia, labile blood pressure, ileus, urinary retention), and respiratory failure (about 25%).
- **Variants**: Miller Fisher syndrome (ophthalmoplegia, ataxia, areflexia; anti-GQ1b antibodies), pharyngeal–cervical–brachial variant, acute motor axonal neuropathy.
- **Diagnosis**: clinical, supported by cerebrospinal fluid with raised protein and a normal cell count ("albuminocytological dissociation"; may be normal in the first week) and nerve conduction studies.
- **Red flags against GBS**: sensory level, marked asymmetry, early sphincter loss, high fever, CSF pleocytosis (consider HIV, Lyme, polio, lymphoma).
- **Management**: admit and monitor. Check vital capacity (forced vital capacity below 20 mL/kg, rapid decline, or bulbar weakness is a trigger for ICU), single breath count, heart rhythm, blood pressure, bladder and bowel, DVT prophylaxis, pain control. **IV immunoglobulin 0.4 g/kg/day for 5 days or plasma exchange** (equally effective; do not use both in sequence; steroids are not helpful). Start within 2–4 weeks of onset in patients who cannot walk unaided. About 5% die; 20% remain disabled.

### 4.5 Chronic inflammatory demyelinating polyneuropathy (CIDP)

Progressive or relapsing symmetrical proximal and distal weakness over more than 8 weeks, with sensory symptoms and reduced reflexes. Treat with steroids, IVIG or plasma exchange, followed by maintenance. Refer.

### 4.6 Bell palsy and facial weakness

Acute unilateral lower motor neuron facial palsy (forehead involved: cannot wrinkle brow) reaching maximum within 72 hours. **Forehead sparing** means upper motor neuron origin; treat as stroke. Exclude otitis media, herpes zoster oticus (Ramsay Hunt: vesicles in the ear canal), Lyme disease, sarcoid, parotid tumour, and HIV in endemic areas. Treatment: oral prednisolone 50–60 mg daily for 7–10 days started within 72 hours improves recovery; antivirals add little (offer in severe palsy); eye protection (lubricants, taping at night) is essential. About 70–85% recover fully. Refer if no sign of recovery at 3 weeks (some guidelines say 3 months), recurrent, or if atypical features.

## 5. Spinal cord and cauda equina emergencies

**Acute cord compression** (metastases, disc, abscess, haematoma, trauma): back pain (often thoracic, worse lying flat, at night), progressive weakness, sensory level, sphincter dysfunction. Urgent MRI of the entire spine, high-dose dexamethasone for malignant compression, and neurosurgical/oncological review. See chapter 50.

**Cauda equina syndrome**: severe low back pain with bilateral sciatica, saddle anaesthesia, bladder and bowel disturbance (retention, loss of anal tone), sexual dysfunction, leg weakness. Urgent MRI and decompression within 24–48 hours; **do not wait for retention to develop**. A normal bladder scan does not exclude early cauda equina syndrome with incomplete lesion.

**Transverse myelitis**: acute bilateral motor, sensory and autonomic dysfunction with a sensory level, evolving over hours to days. MRI with gadolinium, lumbar puncture, AQP4 and MOG antibodies, and early treatment with IV methylprednisolone 1 g daily for 3–5 days; consider plasma exchange for severe cases.

## 6. Dementia and cognitive impairment

### 6.1 Distinguish delirium, depression and dementia

| | Delirium | Dementia | Depression |
|---|---|---|---|
| Onset | Hours to days | Months to years | Weeks to months |
| Course | Fluctuating, worse at night | Progressive, steady | Variable |
| Attention | Impaired | Preserved until late | Variable, effortful |
| Consciousness | Altered | Clear | Clear |
| Mood | Labile | Often apathetic | Low, with insight and complaints about memory |

Delirium is a medical emergency (see chapters 33 and 56) and is common in dementia. Screen with the 4AT, CAM or equivalent.

### 6.2 Presenting complaints and first assessment

Memory complaints from the patient alone, without informant concern, are often benign or due to depression or anxiety. A **collateral history** from a relative is essential: repeated questions, missed bills, getting lost, personality change, new difficulty with tasks, safety issues (driving, cooking).

Cognitive screening: MoCA (score below 26 abnormal; education adjusted), MMSE (below 24; culture and education matter), Addenbrooke's Cognitive Examination III, Mini-Cog (rapid; clock drawing plus 3-word recall), and in primary care the GPCOG or 6CIT. Brief tests are screening tools, not diagnoses.

### 6.3 Reversible and contributing causes: the dementia screen

- Bloods: FBC, ESR/CRP, U&E, calcium, glucose/HbA1c, TSH, B12 and folate, liver function. Add HIV, syphilis, and Lyme serology if risk factors exist.
- Drugs: anticholinergics, benzodiazepines, opioids, sedative antihistamines, antiepileptics. Use a tool such as the Anticholinergic Cognitive Burden scale and deprescribe.
- Depression, sleep apnoea, alcohol, hearing and vision impairment.
- Structural: **neuroimaging (MRI or CT)** for all patients with suspected dementia, to look for vascular disease, tumour, subdural haematoma, normal pressure hydrocephalus, and pattern of atrophy.
- Others where indicated: EEG, CSF biomarkers (amyloid-β42, tau, phospho-tau), amyloid and tau PET, plasma phospho-tau, FDG-PET, DaTscan, genetic testing.

### 6.4 Subtypes

**Alzheimer disease (about 60–70% of dementia).** Insidious onset, progressive loss of episodic memory (recent events, repeated questions), then language, visuospatial and executive function. Early insight loss. Imaging: medial temporal and hippocampal atrophy. CSF or PET biomarkers confirm amyloid and tau pathology. Typical course 8–10 years from diagnosis.

**Vascular dementia.** Stepwise decline, vascular risk factors, focal signs, gait and executive dysfunction, often with apathy and urinary urgency. MRI shows infarcts or extensive white matter lesions. Often mixed with Alzheimer disease.

**Dementia with Lewy bodies.** Fluctuating cognition, recurrent well-formed visual hallucinations, parkinsonism (after or within 1 year of dementia onset), REM sleep behaviour disorder, severe neuroleptic sensitivity (**avoid typical antipsychotics and use cautiously atypicals; quetiapine or clozapine at low dose when needed**). Distinguished from Parkinson disease dementia by timing (dementia before or within a year of motor signs).

**Frontotemporal dementia.** Onset often 45–65. Behavioural variant: disinhibition, apathy, loss of empathy, compulsions, hyperorality, with relatively preserved memory early. Language variants: progressive non-fluent aphasia, semantic dementia. MRI/FDG-PET: frontal and temporal atrophy/hypometabolism. May overlap with motor neuron disease.

**Others**: normal pressure hydrocephalus, Creutzfeldt–Jakob disease (rapidly progressive dementia over weeks to months with myoclonus; MRI DWI cortical ribboning, RT-QuIC in CSF), alcohol-related dementia, HIV-associated neurocognitive disorder, autoimmune encephalitis (subacute memory loss, seizures, psychiatric features), neurosyphilis, chronic subdural haematoma, Huntington disease.

### 6.5 Management

- Disclose the diagnosis sensitively; involve family; assess capacity and decision-making; plan ahead (power of attorney, advance care planning, driving, finances).
- Non-drug care first: structured routine, stimulation, carer support, treatment of hearing and vision loss, safety.
- **Cholinesterase inhibitors** (donepezil, rivastigmine, galantamine) for mild to moderate Alzheimer disease and for Lewy body and Parkinson dementia; **memantine** for moderate to severe disease. Effects are modest; reassess periodically; watch bradycardia and syncope.
- **Anti-amyloid monoclonal antibodies** (lecanemab, donanemab) are approved in some jurisdictions for early Alzheimer disease with confirmed amyloid pathology, with modest benefit and risk of amyloid-related imaging abnormalities (ARIA) needing MRI monitoring and APOE ε4 genotyping; access and eligibility are restricted.
- Behavioural and psychological symptoms: look for pain, infection, constipation, retention, boredom, drugs; use non-pharmacological measures; antipsychotics only for severe distress or risk, at the lowest dose for the shortest time (risk of stroke and death).
- Control vascular risk factors; treat hearing loss, depression, and sleep disorders.

### 6.6 Normal pressure hydrocephalus (NPH)

Triad: **gait disturbance (magnetic, wide-based, shuffling gait), urinary urgency/incontinence, and cognitive decline**; the gait comes first and is the most responsive. MRI: enlarged ventricles out of proportion to sulcal atrophy, with disproportionately enlarged subarachnoid space hydrocephalus (DESH). A high-volume lumbar puncture trial (30–50 mL) or lumbar drain with gait testing before and after predicts shunt response. Consider in anyone with "dementia plus gait problems plus incontinence".

## 7. Parkinson disease and parkinsonism

### 7.1 Diagnosis

Parkinson disease is a clinical diagnosis. **Bradykinesia** (slowness with decrement in amplitude or speed of repetitive movement) plus at least one of **rest tremor** (4–6 Hz "pill-rolling") or **rigidity** (lead pipe, with cogwheeling if tremor is present). Postural instability appears later. Supportive features: asymmetric onset, good response to levodopa, levodopa-induced dyskinesias, olfactory loss, constipation, REM sleep behaviour disorder, depression, anxiety, orthostatic hypotension, and urinary dysfunction.

Examination: facial hypomimia, reduced blink rate, soft monotonous voice (hypophonia), micrographia, reduced arm swing, stooped posture, shuffling gait with festination, difficulty turning, and positive pull test late.

**Imaging** is not required for typical disease. DaTscan (dopamine transporter SPECT) distinguishes degenerative parkinsonism from essential tremor, drug-induced parkinsonism and vascular parkinsonism, but not between Parkinson disease and atypical parkinsonian syndromes. MRI helps with atypical features (vascular lesions, NPH, structural lesion).

### 7.2 Red flags for atypical parkinsonism

- Early postural instability and falls (progressive supranuclear palsy).
- Vertical gaze palsy (PSP).
- Early severe autonomic failure (multiple system atrophy, MSA).
- Cerebellar signs (MSA-C).
- Poor or absent levodopa response.
- Symmetrical onset, lack of rest tremor.
- Early dementia or hallucinations (dementia with Lewy bodies).
- Alien limb, cortical sensory loss, apraxia (corticobasal syndrome).
- Rapid progression.

### 7.3 Secondary causes

Drug-induced parkinsonism (antipsychotics, metoclopramide, prochlorperazine, valproate, lithium, antiemetics) is the commonest reversible cause and may take weeks to months to resolve. Vascular parkinsonism (lower body, gait predominant), NPH, toxins (manganese, MPTP, carbon monoxide), Wilson disease (young onset, consider if under 40), brain tumour and encephalitis.

### 7.4 Management

- Offer treatment when symptoms impair function; early education, exercise (aerobic, balance, and physiotherapy have proven benefit), speech therapy, occupational therapy.
- **Levodopa** (with carbidopa or benserazide) is the most effective drug; start low, go slow; take 30 minutes before meals; protein can reduce absorption. Motor complications (wearing-off, dyskinesia) arise in about half after 5 years. Longer-acting formulations, COMT inhibitors (entacapone, opicapone), MAO-B inhibitors (rasagiline, safinamide), and dopamine agonists (pramipexole, ropinirole, rotigotine: impulse control disorders, sleep attacks, hallucinations; use with caution in older adults) are alternatives or add-ons. Amantadine for dyskinesias.
- **Device-aided therapies**: deep brain stimulation (subthalamic nucleus or globus pallidus interna) for selected patients with motor fluctuations and good cognition; levodopa–carbidopa intestinal gel; apomorphine pump.
- **Non-motor symptoms** need active management: constipation (fluids, fibre, macrogol), orthostatic hypotension (stop causative drugs, fludrocortisone, midodrine, compression), depression/anxiety (SSRIs, CBT), psychosis (**quetiapine or clozapine only**, pimavanserin where available; never haloperidol), sleep, drooling, pain.
- **Never withhold or delay scheduled levodopa in hospital**: sudden withdrawal can cause neuroleptic malignant-like syndrome (parkinsonism–hyperpyrexia). Avoid dopamine antagonists (metoclopramide, haloperidol, prochlorperazine); use domperidone or ondansetron for nausea.
- Regular review for falls, dysphagia, cognition, and driving.

### 7.5 Essential tremor vs Parkinson tremor

Essential tremor: postural and action tremor of hands (8–12 Hz), improves with alcohol, symmetrical, family history in about half, head or voice tremor common; no bradykinesia. First-line propranolol 40–240 mg/day or primidone; botulinum toxin and focused ultrasound or DBS in refractory cases. Parkinson tremor: rest tremor, asymmetric, suppressed by voluntary movement.

## 8. Headache and migraine

Chapter 24 covers the diagnostic approach to headache and the red flags. Here the focus is management of the commonest primary headache.

### 8.1 Migraine diagnosis

Recurrent headache attacks lasting 4–72 hours, typically unilateral, pulsating, moderate to severe, aggravated by routine activity, with nausea or photophobia and phonophobia. Aura (in about a third): fully reversible visual (scintillating scotoma, zigzag), sensory (spreading paraesthesia, usually hand to face), or speech symptoms developing over 5 or more minutes and lasting 5–60 minutes. Aura with sudden onset, motor weakness, or prolonged duration needs exclusion of TIA/stroke.

### 8.2 Acute treatment

- Start early, in the headache phase, and within an hour for best effect.
- Mild to moderate: aspirin 900 mg, ibuprofen 400–600 mg, naproxen 500–1000 mg, diclofenac, paracetamol 1 g (less effective); add an antiemetic (metoclopramide or domperidone 10 mg).
- Moderate to severe or no response: **triptans** (sumatriptan 50–100 mg oral, 6 mg subcutaneous, 20 mg nasal; rizatriptan, zolmitriptan, eletriptan, naratriptan). Contraindicated in ischaemic heart disease, uncontrolled hypertension, hemiplegic or brainstem aura, and stroke history. Take at headache onset (not during aura). Limit use.
- Newer options: gepants (rimegepant, ubrogepant, atogepant) and lasmiditan, without vasoconstriction.
- **Medication-overuse headache**: simple analgesics on 15 or more days a month, or triptans, opioids, combination analgesics on 10 or more days a month, for over 3 months. Withdrawal, education and prophylaxis are the treatment.
- Avoid opioids and butalbital-containing analgesics.

### 8.3 Prevention

Offer when two or more attacks a month are disabling, acute treatment is ineffective or contraindicated, or attacks are prolonged or associated with prolonged aura. Options: propranolol 80–240 mg/day or metoprolol; amitriptyline 10–75 mg at night; topiramate 50–100 mg/day (teratogenic; reduces efficacy of oral contraceptives at higher doses; cognitive side effects); candesartan 8–16 mg; valproate (not in women of childbearing potential); flunarizine; venlafaxine; botulinum toxin for chronic migraine; and **CGRP pathway monoclonal antibodies** (erenumab, fremanezumab, galcanezumab, eptinezumab). Assess efficacy at 8–12 weeks. Lifestyle: regular sleep, meals, hydration, exercise; keep a headache diary.

**Women**: combined hormonal contraception is contraindicated in migraine with aura (risk of ischaemic stroke); use progestogen-only or non-hormonal methods. Triptans and paracetamol can be used in pregnancy when needed; discuss alternatives.

### 8.4 Cluster headache

Severe unilateral orbital or periorbital pain lasting 15–180 minutes, with ipsilateral lacrimation, nasal congestion, ptosis or miosis, restlessness or agitation (patients pace, in contrast to migraine), occurring in clusters up to several times a day for weeks. Acute: 100% oxygen 12–15 L/min for 15 minutes via a non-rebreathing mask, or subcutaneous sumatriptan 6 mg. Preventive: verapamil (ECG monitoring), prednisolone bridge, galcanezumab. Do not miss secondary causes: MRI is recommended at first presentation.

## 9. Other conditions encountered often

### 9.1 Multiple sclerosis (MS)

Immune-mediated demyelination of the central nervous system, typically presenting at 20–40 (women more than men, about 2–3:1) with a first attack: optic neuritis (painful monocular visual loss with a relative afferent pupillary defect and dyschromatopsia), brainstem syndromes (internuclear ophthalmoplegia, diplopia, vertigo), partial myelitis (sensory level, Lhermitte sign), or sensory disturbance. Relapsing–remitting in 85%. Others: primary progressive, secondary progressive.

Diagnosis (2017 McDonald criteria): clinical attacks plus MRI evidence of dissemination in space and time, or CSF-specific oligoclonal bands. Exclude mimics: neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder (AQP4), MOG antibody disease, sarcoid, vasculitis, vitamin B12 deficiency, CNS infections, small vessel disease, and others.

Treatment: acute relapses with methylprednisolone 1 g IV or orally daily for 3–5 days; disease-modifying therapies (interferon beta, glatiramer, teriflunomide, dimethyl fumarate, fingolimod and siponimod, ozanimod, natalizumab, ocrelizumab, ofatumumab, cladribine, alemtuzumab, autologous stem cell transplantation in selected cases) started early and by a specialist. Symptomatic care: spasticity (baclofen, tizanidine), fatigue, bladder dysfunction, pain, depression, heat sensitivity (Uhthoff phenomenon). Counsel on pregnancy; relapse rate falls in pregnancy and rises postpartum.

### 9.2 Myasthenia gravis

Autoimmune disease of the neuromuscular junction (anti-AChR in ~85% of generalised cases, anti-MuSK, anti-LRP4). **Fatigable weakness**: ptosis, diplopia, bulbar symptoms (dysarthria, dysphagia, nasal speech), neck and proximal limb weakness, worse with sustained activity and at the end of the day, better with rest. Diagnosis: antibody testing, repetitive nerve stimulation and single-fibre EMG, ice-pack test for ptosis, CT chest for thymoma. Treatment: pyridostigmine, prednisolone, azathioprine and other immunosuppressants, thymectomy in selected patients, and newer complement and FcRn inhibitors. **Myasthenic crisis**: respiratory failure needing ventilation, treated with IVIG or plasma exchange. Drugs that exacerbate: aminoglycosides, fluoroquinolones, macrolides, magnesium, beta-blockers, and botulinum toxin; check before prescribing. Lambert–Eaton syndrome (proximal weakness with autonomic features, reduced reflexes improving after exercise, small-cell lung cancer in many) is the paraneoplastic mimic.

### 9.3 Motor neuron disease / amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS)

Progressive degeneration of upper and lower motor neurons: asymmetric limb weakness and wasting with fasciculations and brisk reflexes (mix of lower and upper motor neuron signs), bulbar onset (slurred speech, dysphagia, tongue fasciculation), no sensory loss, preserved eye movements and sphincter control. Median survival 2–4 years, with respiratory failure as the usual cause of death. Diagnosis by exclusion with EMG; exclude cervical myelopathy, multifocal motor neuropathy, and others. Care: multidisciplinary clinic, riluzole (modest survival benefit), edaravone, tofersen for SOD1 mutation, non-invasive ventilation (improves survival and quality of life), PEG feeding, communication aids, palliative care early. Breaking the news requires time and sensitivity.

### 9.4 Functional neurological disorder (FND)

Genuine neurological symptoms (weakness, tremor, gait disturbance, seizures, sensory loss, speech, movement disorders) arising from altered nervous system function, not damage, and **diagnosed on positive signs** rather than by exclusion: Hoover sign (hip extension weakness that normalises with contralateral hip flexion), entrainment of functional tremor, give-way weakness, drift without pronation, midline splitting of vibration, and a dragging monoplegic gait. Co-existing neurological disease occurs in a substantial minority. Communicate the diagnosis positively ("real, common, potentially reversible"); avoid "psychogenic" or "all in the mind"; refer to physiotherapy and specialist-led rehabilitation; treat comorbid depression, anxiety and pain.

### 9.5 Syncope and vertigo

Covered in chapters 24 and 28. In vertigo, use the HINTS examination (head impulse, nystagmus, test of skew) only in the acute vestibular syndrome with continuous vertigo and nystagmus, and by trained examiners; in intermittent vertigo (BPPV), use the Dix–Hallpike manoeuvre and the Epley manoeuvre.

## Worked cases

### Case 1: The woman who woke up with a weak arm

**Presentation.** Elena, 68, wakes at 06:30 with slurred speech and a weak right arm. Her husband last saw her normal at 23:00. She has hypertension and atrial fibrillation diagnosed last year; she stopped her anticoagulant after a nosebleed. BP 172/96, pulse 96 irregular, glucose 6.8 mmol/L. NIHSS 9: right facial droop, right arm drift, right leg mild weakness, expressive aphasia.

**Reasoning.** Left MCA syndrome. Cardioembolic stroke in untreated AF. The onset is unwitnessed (last known well 23:00; arrival 08:00 is 9 hours later), so the standard 4.5-hour window is closed. Non-contrast CT: no haemorrhage, early hypodensity in the left insula. CT angiography: left M1 occlusion. CT perfusion: small core and large penumbra.

**Action.** Direct transfer for thrombectomy: perfusion mismatch supports treatment up to 24 hours. Intravenous thrombolysis is considered if MRI shows DWI–FLAIR mismatch, but in this centre the thrombectomy pathway is prioritised. After successful recanalisation, blood pressure is controlled below 180/105 mmHg, swallow screen before diet, admission to the stroke unit. A DOAC is started at day 4 given moderate infarct size; plan includes a discussion on the nosebleed (modifiable cause, ENT review) and a clear explanation that stopping anticoagulation after a minor bleed carried a larger stroke risk.

**Lessons.** Wake-up stroke is not an exclusion for reperfusion: imaging selects patients. AF without anticoagulation is the leading preventable cause of severe stroke.

### Case 2: The "migraine" that was a carotid dissection

**Presentation.** Marco, 38, has right-sided neck and facial pain for 2 days after a cycling fall, with drooping of the right eyelid and a small pupil. Then 30 minutes of transient left hand weakness. No previous migraine.

**Reasoning.** Pain in the neck and face with ipsilateral partial Horner syndrome (ptosis and miosis, preserved sweating of the forehead) after minor trauma is **internal carotid artery dissection** until proven otherwise, with a TIA from artery-to-artery embolism.

**Action.** MRI with fat-suppressed sequences and CT or MR angiography show a long tapered occlusion of the right internal carotid artery with intramural haematoma. Start antithrombotic therapy (antiplatelet or anticoagulation are equivalent in trials; choose by local policy and patient factors), avoid neck manipulation and heavy lifting, and follow up with imaging. Thrombolysis is not contraindicated by dissection if a stroke occurs.

**Lessons.** New headache or neck pain with a Horner syndrome is a dissection. Young stroke: always think dissection, PFO, drugs, thrombophilia.

### Case 3: First seizure at a party

**Presentation.** Sara, 19, collapsed at a party after a night without sleep, stiffened, jerked for about 1 minute, was confused for 15 minutes. A friend reports a tongue bite on the side. She has had two early-morning myoclonic jerks of the arms in the past year, dropping her coffee. EEG: generalised 4–5 Hz polyspike-and-wave discharges. MRI normal.

**Reasoning.** Juvenile myoclonic epilepsy: myoclonic jerks on waking, generalised tonic–clonic seizure triggered by sleep deprivation and alcohol, typical EEG. The syndrome needs lifelong treatment in most.

**Action.** Valproate is the most effective drug but is avoided in women of childbearing potential. Start levetiracetam (or lamotrigine, noting that it may exacerbate myoclonus in some and interacts with oral contraceptive hormones). Counsel: regular sleep, limited alcohol, avoid flashing lights (photosensitivity occurs in about 30%), contraception and pregnancy planning, folic acid, driving rules, safety. Avoid carbamazepine and phenytoin.

**Lessons.** The syndrome diagnosis drives drug choice. Ask directly about morning myoclonic jerks.

### Case 4: Progressive foot weakness after a diarrhoeal illness

**Presentation.** Anil, 45, had diarrhoea two weeks ago. For 3 days he has had tingling in the feet, then difficulty climbing stairs; today he cannot walk without support and has an unsteady voice. Reflexes absent at the knees and ankles, symmetrical distal weakness 3/5, mild bilateral facial weakness. Sensation: reduced vibration in the toes. Single breath count 16 (reduced). Heart rate 110 and fluctuating BP.

**Reasoning.** Guillain–Barré syndrome (post-*Campylobacter*). Autonomic involvement and facial weakness raise concern for rapid evolution.

**Action.** Admit to a high-dependency area. Serial FVC (baseline 3.0 L, 38 mL/kg; falls to 1.6 L, 20 mL/kg overnight) triggers ICU transfer. Lumbar puncture: protein 1.2 g/L, 2 cells/µL. IVIG 0.4 g/kg/day for 5 days. DVT prophylaxis, continuous ECG, swallowing assessment, pain relief (gabapentin), early rehabilitation. He is intubated on day 2 for respiratory failure, extubated on day 9, and walks with a stick at 3 months.

**Lessons.** Respiratory monitoring (FVC, not oxygen saturation) guides care. Peripheral neuropathy plus ascending pattern plus areflexia equals GBS until disproven.

### Case 5: "Forgetful" and now falling

**Presentation.** Mr G., 76, retired engineer, has been forgetting appointments for 18 months. His wife reports that he sometimes sees children in the living room at night, shouts and acts out dreams, and was found staring blankly for 20 minutes. His handwriting has become smaller. Exam: reduced arm swing, mild rigidity, bradykinesia, MoCA 19 with prominent visuospatial and attention deficits. Orthostatic BP drop 30 mmHg.

**Reasoning.** Core features of dementia with Lewy bodies: fluctuating cognition, visual hallucinations, REM sleep behaviour disorder, parkinsonism, and autonomic dysfunction. MRI: mild global atrophy, relative preservation of the medial temporal lobes. DaTscan: reduced striatal uptake.

**Action.** Start rivastigmine (benefit for cognition and hallucinations), review medicines for anticholinergic burden, make a plan for orthostatic hypotension (hydration, compression, stop nocturnal antihypertensives), melatonin or low-dose clonazepam for REM sleep behaviour disorder with bedroom safety, and **flag clearly in the notes: avoid haloperidol and other typical antipsychotics** (risk of severe neuroleptic sensitivity). Support for his wife, driving discontinuation, advance care planning.

**Lessons.** Fluctuations and visual hallucinations are the key. The wrong sedative can be catastrophic.

### Case 6: Drooping eyelids by evening

**Presentation.** Lina, 31, has had double vision and drooping eyelids that worsen towards evening, difficulty chewing steak, and nasal speech after a long conversation. Sustained upgaze for 60 seconds causes ptosis. Reflexes normal, sensation intact.

**Reasoning.** Myasthenia gravis with ocular and bulbar features. Anti-AChR antibodies positive. CT chest: no thymoma (in young women hyperplasia is common). Single-fibre EMG shows increased jitter.

**Action.** Pyridostigmine 60 mg four times daily, then prednisolone started cautiously (steroid-induced transient worsening is possible); azathioprine as a steroid-sparing drug; thymectomy discussed for generalised, antibody-positive disease under 50–60 years. Educate about crisis symptoms (breathlessness, difficulty swallowing) and give a list of drugs to avoid (aminoglycosides, fluoroquinolones, macrolides, magnesium infusions). Contraception counselling before azathioprine and mycophenolate (mycophenolate is teratogenic).

**Lessons.** Fatigability is the hallmark. Antibiotics are the commonest iatrogenic precipitant.

## Common pitfalls

1. **Missing hypoglycaemia** as a stroke mimic: check the glucose first.
2. **Delaying imaging** in suspected stroke because the deficit is mild or improving.
3. **Dismissing posterior circulation stroke** as "just vertigo" because the CT scan is normal; early MRI with DWI is more sensitive.
4. **Treating a TIA as low urgency.** The risk is highest in the first 48 hours.
5. **Not anticoagulating atrial fibrillation** after TIA or stroke, or stopping anticoagulation after a minor bleed without reassessing risk.
6. **Reading a negative CT as excluding subarachnoid haemorrhage** beyond 6 hours or on poor-quality imaging.
7. **Starting antiseizure medication after a provoked seizure** without addressing the cause, or prescribing valproate to a woman of childbearing potential.
8. **Calling convulsive syncope epilepsy**, or vice versa, without a witness account and ECG.
9. **Over-investigating the symmetrical neuropathy** without a basic metabolic screen, or accepting "idiopathic" before checking glucose tolerance, B12 and paraprotein.
10. **Missing cauda equina syndrome** because the patient can still pass urine.
11. **Treating GBS with a steroid**, or relying on pulse oximetry rather than vital capacity.
12. **Diagnosing dementia in the presence of delirium or depression**, or without neuroimaging, drug review and blood tests.
13. **Giving haloperidol to patients with Parkinson disease or Lewy body dementia**, or withholding levodopa in hospital.
14. **Using triptans in patients with ischaemic heart disease or hemiplegic migraine**, or combined oral contraceptives in migraine with aura.
15. **Missing medication-overuse headache**, which maintains chronic headache.
16. **Diagnosing functional disorder without positive signs**, or missing an organic disease behind an apparent functional one.

## Key points

- Localise (where) then determine the cause (what), guided by tempo.
- Stroke is a time-critical emergency: image immediately, treat with thrombolysis or thrombectomy where eligible, admit to a stroke unit, and prevent recurrence with antithrombotics, BP, lipid control and anticoagulation for AF.
- TIA is a stroke until proven otherwise: assess within 24 hours.
- Thunderclap headache needs CT and, if negative beyond 6 hours, lumbar puncture.
- A witness account, ECG and EEG/MRI guide the diagnosis of a first seizure; choose drugs by seizure type and avoid valproate in women of childbearing potential.
- Peripheral neuropathy needs a pattern-based, proportionate work-up; GBS needs respiratory monitoring and IVIG or plasma exchange.
- Dementia is a syndrome with several causes; exclude delirium, depression, drugs and reversible disease; know the Lewy body hazards.
- Parkinson disease is clinical; recognise red flags for atypical parkinsonism, give levodopa on time, and avoid dopamine antagonists.
- Migraine: treat early, avoid overuse, offer prevention, and avoid oestrogen with aura.
- Functional neurological disorder is diagnosed on positive signs.

## Self-assessment

**Questions**

1. A man, 70, has right face, arm and leg weakness and aphasia 2 hours ago. CT shows no haemorrhage. BP 190/105. What is the first step regarding blood pressure, and what treatment options exist?
2. A 24-year-old has a transient episode of left hand weakness lasting 20 minutes and a right-sided neck pain after a chiropractic neck manipulation. What diagnosis must be excluded?
3. How would you distinguish convulsive syncope from an epileptic seizure?
4. Why is valproate avoided in women of childbearing potential, and what are alternatives for generalised epilepsy?
5. List four red flags that make Parkinson disease unlikely.
6. A patient with ascending weakness has FVC 1.2 L (weight 75 kg). What do you do?
7. Distinguish delirium from dementia by two features each.
8. An elderly woman with a brain MRI suggestive of normal pressure hydrocephalus: what is the classic triad and which feature responds best to shunting?
9. Which two classes of antibiotic worsen myasthenia gravis?
10. A woman with migraine with aura asks about contraception. What do you advise?

**Answers**

1. Blood pressure above 185/110 mmHg is a contraindication to thrombolysis until it is lowered; give IV labetalol or nicardipine to bring it below that threshold, then treat with thrombolysis if no other contraindication, within 4.5 hours of onset. CT angiography to identify large-vessel occlusion and arrange thrombectomy in parallel. Do not delay.
2. Cervical artery (carotid or vertebral) dissection. Perform CT or MR angiography; start antithrombotic therapy.
3. Convulsive syncope: trigger and prodrome (standing, heat, pain), pallor, loss of consciousness for seconds, brief irregular jerks after falling, rapid recovery. Epileptic seizure: aura, longer unresponsiveness (minutes), rhythmic jerks, lateral tongue bite, cyanosis, prolonged post-ictal confusion. An ECG is mandatory.
4. Valproate has a high risk of major malformations (neural tube defects) and neurodevelopmental disorders (autism, reduced IQ) in children exposed in utero. Alternatives: lamotrigine and levetiracetam (also considered in pregnancy with lower risk than valproate).
5. Any four of: early falls and postural instability, symmetrical onset, absent rest tremor, poor levodopa response, early dementia or hallucinations, early severe autonomic failure, cerebellar signs, vertical gaze palsy, rapid progression, pyramidal signs, antipsychotic exposure.
6. This is an emergency: FVC below 20 mL/kg (here 16 mL/kg) predicts respiratory failure. Transfer to ICU or a high-dependency unit for close monitoring and early ventilation; start IVIG or plasma exchange; check bulbar function and autonomic stability.
7. Delirium: acute onset (hours to days), fluctuating course, inattention, altered consciousness. Dementia: insidious onset, steady progression, preserved attention early, clear consciousness.
8. Gait disturbance, urinary incontinence and cognitive decline; gait improves most.
9. Aminoglycosides, fluoroquinolones (macrolides also).
10. Avoid combined oestrogen-containing contraceptives (stroke risk). Offer progestogen-only methods (pill, implant), a levonorgestrel intrauterine system, copper intrauterine device, or barrier methods.
