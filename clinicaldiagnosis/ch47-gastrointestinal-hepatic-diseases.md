## Chapter 47. Gastrointestinal and Hepatic Diseases

### Learning objectives

After studying this chapter you should be able to:

1. Evaluate dyspepsia, reflux, dysphagia and peptic ulcer disease, including alarm features, *H. pylori* testing and eradication, and the recognition of Barrett's oesophagus, achalasia and eosinophilic oesophagitis.
2. Interpret viral hepatitis serology (A, B, C, D, E), and outline indications and regimens for treatment, vaccination and screening.
3. Diagnose and manage alcohol-related liver disease, metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), autoimmune and metabolic liver diseases, and drug-induced liver injury.
4. Stage cirrhosis (Child–Pugh, MELD) and manage its complications: ascites, spontaneous bacterial peritonitis, varices, hepatic encephalopathy, hepatorenal syndrome and hepatocellular carcinoma.
5. Diagnose and manage acute and chronic pancreatitis, and recognise pancreatic cancer.
6. Differentiate ulcerative colitis from Crohn's disease, grade severity, and apply stepwise therapy, including acute severe colitis.
7. Describe coeliac disease, diverticular disease, colorectal cancer screening and surveillance, and gallstone and biliary disease.

---

### 47.1 Approach to gastrointestinal and hepatic disease

A structured approach (Chapters 6, 14, 25–27) combines:

1. **Symptom pattern:** dysphagia, heartburn, dyspepsia, nausea and vomiting, abdominal pain, change in bowel habit, bleeding, weight loss, jaundice, itch.
2. **Alarm (red-flag) features:** **age >55 with new symptoms, progressive dysphagia or odynophagia, persistent vomiting, GI bleeding or iron-deficiency anaemia, unexplained weight loss, palpable mass, jaundice, family history of GI cancer.** These mandate endoscopy or imaging and not empirical therapy alone.
3. **Examination** (Chapter 14), including stigmata of chronic liver disease and rectal examination.
4. **Tests:** FBC, ferritin, CRP, U&E, LFTs, coagulation, albumin, coeliac serology, faecal calprotectin/FIT, viral serology, ultrasound, CT/MRI/MRCP, **upper and lower endoscopy,** elastography, and biopsy where needed.
5. **Treat and review:** many conditions are chronic; adherence, surveillance and lifestyle counselling matter.

### 47.2 Oesophageal and gastric disease

#### Gastro-oesophageal reflux disease (GORD)

- **Symptoms:** heartburn (retrosternal burning after meals, bending, lying), regurgitation, water brash; extra-oesophageal (chronic cough, hoarseness, asthma, dental erosion, globus). **Risk factors:** obesity, hiatus hernia, pregnancy, smoking, alcohol, large meals, drugs (calcium channel blockers, nitrates, anticholinergics, bisphosphonates), scleroderma.
- **Alarm features** (dysphagia, odynophagia, weight loss, anaemia/bleeding, persistent vomiting, age >55 with new onset, family history of upper GI cancer) require **upper endoscopy.**
- **Management:** lifestyle (weight loss, avoid late meals, raise the head of the bed, avoid triggers), **PPI for 4–8 weeks** (omeprazole 20 mg, esomeprazole, lansoprazole), then lowest effective dose or on demand; H₂ blockers/alginates as adjuncts; **endoscopy** if alarm features, refractory symptoms (despite 8 weeks of twice-daily PPI), or long-standing disease in high-risk men >50 (Barrett's screening); **anti-reflux surgery** (laparoscopic fundoplication) or **magnetic sphincter augmentation** for severe regurgitation or PPI intolerance after confirming reflux (24-hour pH-impedance monitoring, manometry).
- **Complications:** **erosive oesophagitis** (Los Angeles grades A–D), **peptic stricture,** **Barrett's oesophagus** (columnar/intestinal metaplasia of the distal oesophagus; risk of adenocarcinoma ≈0.2–0.5%/year; **endoscopic surveillance** at 3–5 year intervals for short segments, more often for long segments or dysplasia; **endoscopic eradication therapy** (radiofrequency ablation, endoscopic mucosal resection) for dysplasia/early cancer; lifelong PPI), **oesophageal adenocarcinoma.**
- **Long-term PPI safety:** low risk overall (rare: hypomagnesaemia, B12 deficiency, fractures, *C. difficile*, pneumonia, kidney disease associations); **deprescribe when no indication.**

#### Dyspepsia and functional dyspepsia

- **Dyspepsia:** epigastric pain/burning, postprandial fullness, early satiety, bloating, nausea. **Organic causes:** peptic ulcer, GORD, gastric/oesophageal cancer, gastroparesis, biliary or pancreatic disease, medications (NSAIDs, bisphosphonates, iron), coeliac disease, *H. pylori.*
- **Approach:** under 45–55 and no alarm features: **"test and treat" for *H. pylori*** (stool antigen or urea breath test) and review drugs, then **PPI trial.** Age >55 or alarm features: **endoscopy.**
- **Functional dyspepsia (Rome IV):** bothersome epigastric pain/burning, postprandial fullness or early satiety for ≥3 months (onset ≥6 months) with no structural cause: **postprandial distress syndrome** vs **epigastric pain syndrome;** treatment: reassurance, small frequent meals, PPI, *H. pylori* eradication, prokinetics, low-dose tricyclic antidepressants, psychological therapy.
- **Gastroparesis:** delayed gastric emptying (diabetes, post-surgical, idiopathic, drugs); nausea, vomiting, early satiety; **scintigraphic gastric emptying study;** diet modification, prokinetics (metoclopramide short term; domperidone; erythromycin), glycaemic control, gastric pacing.

#### Peptic ulcer disease (PUD) and *H. pylori*

- **Causes:** ***H. pylori*** (the commonest, in ≈90% of duodenal and ≈70% of gastric ulcers), **NSAIDs/aspirin** (including low-dose), corticosteroids with NSAIDs, smoking, Zollinger–Ellison syndrome (gastrinoma: multiple/refractory ulcers, diarrhoea, MEN1), stress ulcers (ICU), malignancy (gastric ulcer), Crohn's, CMV/HSV, drugs (bisphosphonates, potassium, mycophenolate).
- **Features:** epigastric pain (duodenal: nocturnal, relieved by food or antacids, hunger pain; gastric: worsened by food, with weight loss/anorexia); **complications:** bleeding (Chapter 27), perforation (Chapter 42), gastric outlet obstruction, penetration.
- **Diagnosis:** **upper endoscopy** (biopsy gastric ulcers for malignancy and *H. pylori;* repeat endoscopy at 6–8 weeks to confirm healing of gastric ulcers); ***H. pylori* testing:** **urea breath test** or **stool antigen** (stop PPIs 2 weeks and antibiotics 4 weeks before), serology (only if no previous treatment and untreated), histology/rapid urease test at endoscopy.
- **Treatment:**
  - **Eradicate *H. pylori*** if positive: **bismuth quadruple therapy** (PPI + bismuth + tetracycline + metronidazole for 10–14 days) or **clarithromycin-based triple therapy** (PPI + clarithromycin + amoxicillin/metronidazole for 14 days) **only where clarithromycin resistance is low (<15%);** vonoprazan-based regimens in some regions; **confirm eradication ≥4 weeks after treatment** (breath/stool test) in ulcer disease.
  - **PPI** for 4–8 weeks (ulcer healing); **stop NSAIDs** where possible; if NSAID/aspirin is necessary: **co-prescribe a PPI,** use COX-2 selective inhibitor with PPI in high-risk, and aspirin for secondary cardiovascular prevention should usually continue with a PPI.
  - **Test for Zollinger–Ellison** (fasting gastrin off PPI, secretin test) if refractory/multiple/atypical ulcers.
- **Gastric cancer:** risk factors: ***H. pylori,* smoking,** salted/smoked foods, **atrophic gastritis/pernicious anaemia,** intestinal metaplasia, family history (CDH1), blood group A, previous gastric surgery, Epstein–Barr virus; presentation: early satiety, weight loss, epigastric pain, dysphagia, anaemia, vomiting, Virchow node, Sister Mary Joseph nodule; **endoscopy with biopsy,** CT/EUS staging, laparoscopy; treatment: **endoscopic resection** for early cancer, **gastrectomy with D2 lymphadenectomy,** perioperative chemotherapy (FLOT), targeted therapy (trastuzumab for HER2, immunotherapy); *H. pylori* eradication reduces risk.

#### Dysphagia

- **Oropharyngeal dysphagia** (difficulty initiating swallow, coughing/choking, nasal regurgitation): stroke, Parkinson disease, MND, myasthenia, head and neck cancer, Zenker diverticulum, post-radiotherapy: speech and language therapy assessment, videofluoroscopy.
- **Oesophageal dysphagia:** **mechanical** (solids first, progressive: **cancer, peptic stricture, Schatzki ring, web,** extrinsic compression, **eosinophilic oesophagitis**) vs **motility** (solids and liquids from the start: **achalasia,** diffuse spasm, scleroderma).
- **Investigations:** **upper endoscopy first** (biopsies), then barium swallow and **high-resolution manometry** (if endoscopy normal).
- **Achalasia:** failure of lower oesophageal sphincter relaxation (loss of inhibitory neurons; secondary: Chagas disease, pseudo-achalasia from cancer); dysphagia for solids and liquids, regurgitation, chest pain, weight loss, nocturnal cough; **barium swallow "bird's beak,"** manometry diagnostic; **treatment:** **pneumatic dilation, laparoscopic Heller myotomy, or peroral endoscopic myotomy (POEM);** botulinum toxin for the frail; increased oesophageal cancer risk.
- **Eosinophilic oesophagitis (EoE):** young atopic men; dysphagia, **food bolus impaction,** heartburn unresponsive to PPI; endoscopy (rings, furrows, white exudates, strictures) and **biopsies (≥15 eosinophils/high-power field);** treatment: **PPI trial, swallowed topical steroids (budesonide, fluticasone), dietary elimination, dupilumab,** dilation for strictures.
- **Oesophageal cancer:** **adenocarcinoma** (Barrett's, obesity, GORD) and **squamous cell carcinoma** (smoking, alcohol, hot drinks, achalasia, caustic injury, tylosis); progressive dysphagia and weight loss; endoscopy, CT, EUS, PET; treatment: endoscopic therapy for early disease, **neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy ± surgery (oesophagectomy),** definitive chemoradiotherapy, immunotherapy; palliation (stents, brachytherapy).

### 47.3 Viral hepatitis

| Virus | Transmission | Acute illness | Chronic infection | Diagnosis | Treatment and prevention |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Hepatitis A (HAV)** | Faeco-oral (contaminated food/water, travel, MSM) | Self-limited (rarely acute liver failure, esp. older adults/chronic liver disease) | No | **Anti-HAV IgM** | Supportive; **vaccine** (pre-exposure; post-exposure vaccine ± immunoglobulin) |
| **Hepatitis B (HBV)** | Blood, sexual, **vertical (mother-to-child)** | Acute (adults: ≈95% clear; neonates/infants: >90% become chronic) | **Chronic if HBsAg persists >6 months;** risk of cirrhosis and HCC | **HBsAg, anti-HBc IgM/IgG, HBeAg/anti-HBe, HBV DNA, anti-HBs** | **Tenofovir (TDF/TAF) or entecavir** (long-term suppression); peg-interferon (selected); **vaccine** (3 doses), birth dose + HBIG for infants of HBsAg-positive mothers; screening of at-risk |
| **Hepatitis C (HCV)** | Blood (IV drug use, transfusion pre-1992, tattoos, healthcare, vertical, sexual in MSM with HIV) | Often asymptomatic | **≈70–80% chronic;** cirrhosis in 15–20% over 20–30 years; HCC | **Anti-HCV, then HCV RNA** | **Direct-acting antivirals (DAAs):** **sofosbuvir/velpatasvir** (pangenotypic) or glecaprevir/pibrentasvir for 8–12 weeks: **cure (SVR) >95%;** no vaccine; **universal one-time screening of adults** (many guidelines) |
| **Hepatitis D (HDV)** | Requires HBV (co-infection or superinfection) | Severe acute hepatitis; rapid progression | Chronic HDV: accelerated cirrhosis | **Anti-HDV, HDV RNA** in HBsAg-positive | **Bulevirtide** (entry inhibitor), peg-interferon; HBV vaccination prevents |
| **Hepatitis E (HEV)** | Faeco-oral (genotypes 1–2: water, epidemics, **severe in pregnancy**); **genotype 3: zoonotic (pork, game; Europe)** | Self-limited; high mortality in pregnancy and chronic liver disease | **Chronic in immunosuppressed (transplant)** | **Anti-HEV IgM, HEV RNA** | Supportive; ribavirin for chronic infection; reduce immunosuppression; vaccine (China) |

#### Interpreting hepatitis B serology

| HBsAg | Anti-HBc | Anti-HBs | HBV DNA | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **+** | **IgM +** | – | + | **Acute HBV** |
| **+** | **IgG +** | – | variable | **Chronic HBV** (HBeAg positive/negative; immune-tolerant, immune-active, inactive carrier, HBeAg-negative hepatitis) |
| – | + | **+** | – | **Resolved (past) infection;** immunity; **risk of reactivation under immunosuppression (rituximab, chemotherapy, steroids, anti-TNF, DAAs)** |
| – | – | **+** | – | **Vaccinated** (immune) |
| – | + | – | – | Isolated anti-HBc: window period, occult HBV, past infection with waned anti-HBs, false positive |
| – | – | – | – | Susceptible |

- **Indications to treat chronic HBV:** HBV DNA >2,000 IU/mL with raised ALT (or fibrosis/cirrhosis, any DNA level in cirrhosis), family history of HCC/cirrhosis, extrahepatic manifestations; pregnancy (high viral load: tenofovir from the third trimester); **prophylaxis** in HBsAg-positive (and often anti-HBc-positive) patients starting immunosuppression.
- **HCC surveillance** (ultrasound ± AFP every 6 months) in cirrhosis, and in selected non-cirrhotic HBV carriers (Asian men >40, women >50, African >20, family history).
- **Acute hepatitis:** symptoms (anorexia, nausea, malaise, right upper quadrant pain, jaundice, dark urine), ALT/AST in the 1,000s; check **INR** (acute liver failure), glucose, mental state (Chapter 27).
- **Prevention:** HBV vaccination (universal infant programmes, healthcare workers, people with diabetes/CKD, MSM, travellers), safer injecting and sexual practices, needle exchange, screening of blood products, **HCV treatment as prevention.**

### 47.4 Alcohol-related, metabolic and other liver disease

#### Alcohol-related liver disease (ALD)

- **Spectrum:** **steatosis** (reversible), **alcoholic hepatitis,** **fibrosis/cirrhosis,** HCC. Risk rises with intake (>30–40 g/day men, >20 g/day women long term; individual susceptibility; female sex, obesity, genetics).
- **Alcoholic hepatitis:** recent heavy drinking, **jaundice, fever, tender hepatomegaly, leucocytosis,** AST:ALT >2 (AST usually <300 U/L), raised bilirubin and INR; **severity:** **Maddrey discriminant function ≥32** or **MELD >20;** infection screen; **prednisolone 40 mg daily for 28 days** (if no infection or GI bleeding; Lille score at day 7 to stop if non-responder) modestly improves short-term survival; nutrition (35–40 kcal/kg/day, protein 1.2–1.5 g/kg), thiamine and vitamins, treat withdrawal, **abstinence** (essential), consider early transplantation in selected severe non-responders.
- **Management of ALD:** **alcohol cessation** (brief intervention, counselling, **acamprosate, naltrexone** (avoid naltrexone in decompensated liver disease), **baclofen**), **nutrition,** vaccinations, screen for HCC/varices when cirrhotic; **transplant** assessment after demonstrated abstinence/ support.

#### Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD; formerly NAFLD)

- **Definition:** **hepatic steatosis (>5% hepatocytes) plus at least one cardiometabolic risk factor** (obesity, type 2 diabetes, hypertension, dyslipidaemia), with no or modest alcohol intake; **MASH** (steatohepatitis) with inflammation/ballooning can progress to fibrosis, cirrhosis and HCC. The most common cause of chronic liver disease worldwide.
- **Assessment:** ALT often normal/mildly raised; **non-invasive fibrosis assessment:** **FIB-4 index** = (age × AST) / (platelets × √ALT) (**<1.3 low risk;** 1.3–2.67 indeterminate → **elastography (VCTE ≥8–10 kPa) or ELF test;** **>2.67 high risk**), biopsy for uncertain cases; exclude other causes (viral hepatitis, alcohol, haemochromatosis, autoimmune, Wilson).
- **Management:** **weight loss ≥7–10%** (lifestyle: Mediterranean diet, exercise ≥150 minutes/week; bariatric surgery in appropriate patients), **manage diabetes** (GLP-1 receptor agonists such as semaglutide improve steatohepatitis; SGLT2 inhibitors; pioglitazone), **statins are safe,** control hypertension, **cardiovascular risk reduction (the commonest cause of death),** **resmetirom** (approved for MASH with F2–F3 fibrosis), vitamin E in selected non-diabetic biopsy-proven MASH; **HCC and variceal surveillance in cirrhosis.**

#### Autoimmune and cholestatic liver disease

| Disease | Features | Diagnosis | Treatment |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Autoimmune hepatitis** | Women, any age; fatigue, jaundice, high ALT, **high IgG;** associated autoimmune disease | **ANA, anti-smooth muscle antibody, anti-LKM1;** biopsy (interface hepatitis, plasma cells); exclude viral, drug | **Prednisolone then azathioprine** (maintenance); budesonide in some; long-term |
| **Primary biliary cholangitis (PBC)** | Middle-aged women; fatigue, **itch,** raised ALP, xanthelasma, osteoporosis | **Anti-mitochondrial antibodies (AMA)** | **Ursodeoxycholic acid;** obeticholic acid or fibrates (bezafibrate), **elafibranor/seladelpar** for non-responders; itch: cholestyramine, rifampicin; transplant |
| **Primary sclerosing cholangitis (PSC)** | Men, **ulcerative colitis** (70–80%), cholestasis, cholangitis; **cholangiocarcinoma and colon cancer risk** | **MRCP** (beading, strictures) | No proven drug therapy; **colonoscopy surveillance,** dominant stricture management, **transplantation** |
| **Wilson disease** | Age <40 with hepatitis/cirrhosis, neuropsychiatric signs, **Kayser–Fleischer rings,** Coombs-negative haemolysis | **Low caeruloplasmin, raised 24-hour urinary copper,** liver copper, ATP7B genetics | **Chelation (penicillamine, trientine), zinc;** transplant for acute liver failure |
| **Haemochromatosis** | HFE C282Y homozygotes; fatigue, arthropathy (MCP), diabetes, hypogonadism, cirrhosis, cardiomyopathy, skin bronzing | **Ferritin >300 (men) /200 (women), transferrin saturation >45%,** HFE genotype; MRI liver iron | **Venesection** to ferritin 50–100 µg/L; screen first-degree relatives; HCC surveillance in cirrhosis |
| **Alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency** | Liver disease (children and adults) and early emphysema | AAT level, phenotype | Supportive; transplantation |
| **Drug-induced liver injury (DILI)** | Hepatocellular, cholestatic or mixed; timing; **paracetamol (dose-dependent),** idiosyncratic: amoxicillin-clavulanate, flucloxacillin, isoniazid, methotrexate, amiodarone, statins (rare), herbal supplements, anabolic steroids | **Exclusion diagnosis;** RUCAM score | **Stop the drug;** NAC for paracetamol; steroids in hypersensitivity/autoimmune-like DILI; avoid rechallenge |

### 47.5 Cirrhosis and its complications

#### Definition, causes and stages

**Cirrhosis** is advanced fibrosis with regenerative nodules and distorted vascular architecture, causing **portal hypertension** and hepatocellular dysfunction. **Causes:** alcohol, **MASLD,** viral hepatitis B/C, autoimmune, cholestatic (PBC, PSC), Wilson, haemochromatosis, AATD, Budd–Chiari, heart failure, cryptogenic. **Compensated** (no complications) vs **decompensated** (ascites, variceal bleeding, encephalopathy, jaundice): median survival drops from >10 years to about 2 years after decompensation.

- **Diagnosis:** clinical signs (Chapter 14), platelets low, INR high, albumin low, bilirubin high; **ultrasound** (nodular liver, splenomegaly, ascites), **elastography** (VCTE ≥15 kPa suggests cirrhosis), biopsy if needed; **endoscopy** for varices.
- **Child–Pugh score** (bilirubin, albumin, INR, ascites, encephalopathy: 1–3 points each): **A (5–6), B (7–9), C (10–15);** **MELD / MELD-Na** (bilirubin, INR, creatinine, sodium) for transplant allocation and prognosis.
- **General management:** treat the cause, abstain from alcohol, **nutrition (energy 35 kcal/kg/day, protein 1.2–1.5 g/kg/day, late-evening snack; avoid protein restriction),** vaccinations (HAV, HBV, influenza, pneumococcal, COVID-19), **avoid NSAIDs, aminoglycosides, benzodiazepines/sedatives, contrast without need, excessive paracetamol (≤2 g/day),** manage diabetes, screen for **osteoporosis, HCC (6-monthly ultrasound ± AFP), varices;** **refer for transplant assessment** at decompensation or MELD ≥15.

#### Ascites

- **Grades:** 1 (ultrasound only), 2 (moderate), 3 (tense). **Diagnostic paracentesis** in all new ascites and admissions: **cell count (neutrophils), albumin (SAAG ≥1.1 g/dL = portal hypertension), protein, culture (inoculate blood-culture bottles at bedside), cytology** as indicated.
- **Treatment:** **sodium restriction (about 2 g/day or 90 mmol sodium),** **diuretics:** **spironolactone 100–400 mg + furosemide 40–160 mg** (monitor K⁺, Na⁺, creatinine; reduce if hyponatraemia, AKI); **large-volume paracentesis (>5 L) with albumin (6–8 g/L removed)** for tense ascites; **refractory ascites:** serial paracentesis, **TIPS,** liver transplant, avoid ACE inhibitors/ARBs and NSAIDs; **midodrine** and long-term albumin in selected patients; **hepatic hydrothorax** (diuretics, TIPS; avoid chest tubes).
- **Hyponatraemia:** fluid restriction only if <125 mmol/L; vaptans controversial.

#### Spontaneous bacterial peritonitis (SBP)

- **Ascitic fluid neutrophil count ≥250/µL** without a surgical source. **Presentation:** fever, abdominal pain/tenderness, new or worsening ascites, encephalopathy, AKI, GI bleeding; **may be asymptomatic: tap every admitted cirrhotic patient with ascites.**
- **Treatment:** **IV ceftriaxone or cefotaxime** (empirical; broaden for nosocomial/MDR), **albumin 1.5 g/kg on day 1 and 1 g/kg on day 3** (reduces HRS-AKI and mortality); **secondary prophylaxis** (norfloxacin/ciprofloxacin or co-trimoxazole); primary prophylaxis if ascitic protein <15 g/L plus renal/hepatic dysfunction, or after GI bleeding.

#### Variceal bleeding and portal hypertension

- Chapter 27 (acute management). **Screening endoscopy** at diagnosis of cirrhosis (unless platelets >150 and elastography <20 kPa: Baveno criteria); **primary prophylaxis:** **non-selective beta-blocker (carvedilol preferred, or propranolol)** for medium–large varices (or endoscopic variceal ligation if intolerant); **secondary prophylaxis:** NSBB + EVL; **TIPS** for refractory bleeding.
- **Portal vein thrombosis:** anticoagulation in many; **Budd–Chiari syndrome:** anticoagulation, TIPS, treat the cause (myeloproliferative neoplasm, thrombophilia, PNH).

#### Hepatic encephalopathy (HE)

- West Haven grades 1–4; precipitants (infection, GI bleeding, constipation, dehydration, diuretics, electrolyte disturbance, sedatives, TIPS, non-adherence); **treatment:** treat precipitants, **lactulose** (titrate to 2–3 soft stools/day), **rifaximin 550 mg twice daily for recurrence prevention,** avoid sedatives, protein not restricted; **covert HE** impairs driving.

#### Hepatorenal syndrome (HRS-AKI)

- AKI in cirrhosis with ascites without other cause, after stopping diuretics/nephrotoxins and volume expansion with **albumin 1 g/kg (max 100 g) for 2 days.** **Treatment:** **terlipressin (or noradrenaline) plus albumin;** renal replacement therapy as bridge; **liver transplant** (± kidney).

#### Other complications

- **Hepatopulmonary syndrome** (platypnoea, hypoxia, intrapulmonary shunting; bubble echo; transplant), **portopulmonary hypertension,** **cirrhotic cardiomyopathy,** **coagulopathy** (balanced; do not routinely correct INR), **thrombocytopenia, anaemia, infections, osteoporosis, sarcopenia, hepatocellular carcinoma.**

#### Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC)

- **Risk:** cirrhosis (any cause), chronic HBV (even without cirrhosis), HCV, MASLD. **Surveillance:** **ultrasound ± AFP every 6 months** in cirrhosis. **Diagnosis:** **multiphase CT or MRI** (arterial hyperenhancement with washout; LI-RADS 5) without biopsy in cirrhosis; AFP.
- **Staging and treatment (BCLC):** **resection or ablation** (single small tumour with preserved liver function), **liver transplantation** (Milan criteria: single ≤5 cm or up to 3 ≤3 cm), **TACE** (intermediate), **systemic therapy** (atezolizumab + bevacizumab; durvalumab + tremelimumab; lenvatinib, sorafenib) for advanced disease; best supportive care.

#### Liver transplantation

- **Indications:** decompensated cirrhosis (MELD ≥15), HCC within criteria, acute liver failure meeting King's College criteria, selected metabolic/cholestatic diseases. **Contraindications:** active extrahepatic malignancy, uncontrolled sepsis, severe cardiopulmonary disease, ongoing substance use without engagement (assessed individually).

### 47.6 Pancreatic disease

#### Acute pancreatitis

(Diagnosis, severity, management: Chapter 42.) **Aetiology:** gallstones (≈40%), alcohol (≈30%), hypertriglyceridaemia (>11 mmol/L), hypercalcaemia, drugs (azathioprine, valproate, GLP-1 agonists rarely), post-ERCP, trauma, autoimmune (IgG4), infections, genetic, idiopathic (EUS to exclude microlithiasis). **Cholecystectomy** in the same admission for mild gallstone pancreatitis; abstinence and brief intervention for alcohol; triglyceride lowering (insulin infusion, plasmapheresis in severe).

#### Chronic pancreatitis

- **Causes:** **alcohol** (≈70%), smoking, genetic (PRSS1, SPINK1, CFTR), autoimmune, obstructive, hypertriglyceridaemia, idiopathic (**TIGAR-O**).
- **Features:** chronic or recurrent epigastric pain radiating to the back, **exocrine insufficiency** (steatorrhoea, weight loss, fat-soluble vitamin deficiency when >90% function lost), **endocrine insufficiency** (type 3c diabetes), calcifications, pseudocysts, biliary or duodenal obstruction, **pancreatic cancer risk.**
- **Diagnosis:** CT (calcifications, ductal dilatation), **MRCP/secretin MRCP,** EUS; **faecal elastase <200 µg/g** (exocrine insufficiency).
- **Treatment:** **abstinence from alcohol and smoking,** **pancreatic enzyme replacement (e.g. Creon)** with meals, analgesia (stepwise, avoid long-term opioids; neuropathic agents; coeliac plexus block), diabetes management, vitamin supplementation, endoscopic therapy (stones, strictures) or surgery (drainage, resection) for refractory pain and complications; screening for malnutrition and osteoporosis.

#### Pancreatic cancer

- **Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma:** risk factors: smoking, chronic pancreatitis, new-onset diabetes in older adults, family history/genetic syndromes (BRCA1/2, PALB2, Lynch, Peutz–Jeghers, CDKN2A), obesity. **Presentation:** painless obstructive jaundice (head), epigastric/back pain, weight loss, anorexia, new diabetes, steatorrhoea, migratory thrombophlebitis (Trousseau), Courvoisier sign.
- **Diagnosis:** **pancreatic-protocol CT,** EUS with biopsy, CA 19-9 (not for screening); staging for resectability (**resectable, borderline, locally advanced, metastatic**).
- **Treatment:** **surgery (Whipple/pancreaticoduodenectomy)** plus **adjuvant chemotherapy (modified FOLFIRINOX)** for resectable disease (5-year survival about 20–30% after resection); neoadjuvant therapy for borderline resectable; systemic chemotherapy (FOLFIRINOX, gemcitabine–nab-paclitaxel) for advanced disease; biliary stenting, pancreatic enzymes, palliative care early.
- **Neuroendocrine tumours** (insulinoma, gastrinoma, glucagonoma, VIPoma, non-functioning): better prognosis; surgery, somatostatin analogues, PRRT.

### 47.7 Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD)

| | **Ulcerative colitis (UC)** | **Crohn's disease (CD)** |
|---|---|---|
| **Extent** | **Rectum and extending proximally, continuous**; colon only | **Any part of the GI tract (terminal ileum and colon most common); skip lesions** |
| **Depth** | Mucosal/submucosal | **Transmural** |
| **Symptoms** | **Bloody diarrhoea, urgency, tenesmus,** mucus, abdominal cramps | Diarrhoea (often non-bloody), **abdominal pain (RIF),** weight loss, fever, **perianal disease, fistulae, strictures, oral ulcers** |
| **Endoscopy/histology** | Continuous erythema, friability, ulcers; crypt abscesses, no granulomas | Aphthous/linear ulcers, cobblestoning, skip lesions; **non-caseating granulomas** (≈30%) |
| **Complications** | Toxic megacolon, perforation, **colorectal cancer (risk after 8–10 years of extensive disease)**, PSC | Strictures/obstruction, fistulae (enteroenteric, enterovesical, perianal), abscess, malabsorption, B12 deficiency, **small bowel cancer** |
| **Smoking** | Protective (not advised) | **Worsens** |
| **Surgery** | **Curative (colectomy)** | Not curative; for complications |

- **Epidemiology:** peak onset 15–35 (second peak >50); genetics (NOD2), microbiome, environment.
- **Extra-intestinal manifestations:** **arthropathy** (peripheral, axial spondyloarthritis), **erythema nodosum, pyoderma gangrenosum,** uveitis/episcleritis, **PSC,** oral aphthae, osteoporosis, gallstones, kidney stones, VTE (high risk), anaemia, growth failure in children.
- **Diagnosis:** history, **FBC, CRP, ferritin, albumin, stool microbiology (including *C. difficile*), faecal calprotectin** (>150–250 µg/g suggests intestinal inflammation), **ileocolonoscopy with biopsies,** **MR/CT enterography** and capsule endoscopy for small bowel Crohn's, perianal MRI; **exclude infection** (including CMV in refractory colitis).
- **Severity of UC (Truelove and Witts):** **mild** (<4 stools/day, no systemic upset), **moderate** (4–6), **severe** (**≥6 bloody stools/day plus systemic toxicity:** heart rate >90, temperature >37.8 °C, Hb <105 g/L, ESR >30 or CRP >30).

#### Treatment of ulcerative colitis

- **Mild–moderate:** **5-ASA** (mesalazine) **oral plus rectal** (proctitis/left-sided: topical suppository/enema first), **budesonide MMX,** oral prednisolone 40 mg tapering over 8 weeks for flares; maintenance with 5-ASA.
- **Steroid-dependent or refractory:** **thiopurines** (azathioprine; check TPMT/NUDT15; lymphoma and skin cancer risk), **biologics** (**infliximab, adalimumab, golimumab, vedolizumab, ustekinumab, mirikizumab, risankizumab**), **small molecules** (**tofacitinib, upadacitinib, filgotinib, ozanimod, etrasimod:** JAK inhibitors have VTE/cardiovascular warnings); **colectomy** for failure, dysplasia/cancer.
- **Acute severe ulcerative colitis (ASUC): inpatient emergency:**
  1. **IV hydrocortisone 100 mg four times daily** (or methylprednisolone 60 mg daily), **VTE prophylaxis (LMWH),** IV fluids and electrolytes (correct potassium and magnesium), avoid opioids, anticholinergics, NSAIDs, antidiarrhoeals (precipitate megacolon), **stool cultures and *C. difficile* toxin,** CMV if refractory, **abdominal X-ray** (colonic dilatation >5.5–6 cm: toxic megacolon), **flexible sigmoidoscopy** (severity, CMV), nutritional support.
  2. **Reassess at day 3:** **Oxford criteria** (stool frequency >8/day, or 3–8 with CRP >45 mg/L, predicts failure): **rescue therapy with infliximab or ciclosporin** (day 3–5), or **colectomy** (joint gastroenterology–colorectal surgery decision from admission).
  3. **Indications for emergency colectomy:** toxic megacolon, perforation, massive bleeding, failure of rescue therapy at 4–7 days.
- **Surveillance colonoscopy** from 8–10 years after onset (extensive/left-sided colitis; annual in high-risk, e.g. PSC) with chromoendoscopy.

#### Treatment of Crohn's disease

- **Induction:** **corticosteroids** (prednisolone 40 mg; **budesonide** for ileocaecal disease), **exclusive enteral nutrition** (children), **biologics early** (anti-TNF ± immunomodulator, **ustekinumab, vedolizumab, risankizumab, upadacitinib**) in moderate-severe, stricturing or penetrating disease and perianal disease; **antibiotics/drainage** for abscesses, perianal fistulae (**MRI, examination under anaesthesia, seton drainage, then anti-TNF**).
- **Maintenance:** **thiopurines or methotrexate,** biologics; **stop smoking;** **surgery** (stricturoplasty, ileocaecal resection) for obstructing disease, fistulae, refractory disease; post-operative prophylaxis (thiopurine/anti-TNF, metronidazole) and endoscopic recurrence monitoring at 6–12 months.
- **Before biologics/immunosuppression:** screen for **TB (IGRA, chest X-ray), hepatitis B/C, HIV, VZV status,** vaccinations (influenza, pneumococcal, HPV, COVID-19; avoid live vaccines), skin checks, cervical screening; therapeutic drug monitoring; monitor for infections, malignancy, demyelination, drug-induced lupus, psoriasiform reactions.
- **Differential diagnosis:** infectious colitis (*Campylobacter, Salmonella, Shigella, C. difficile,* CMV, amoebiasis, TB), ischaemic colitis, **microscopic colitis,** diverticular colitis, NSAID enteropathy, radiation, coeliac, IBS (normal calprotectin), colorectal cancer, intestinal TB (in endemic areas, mimics Crohn's).
- **Special populations:** pregnancy (disease activity at conception predicts course; most drugs compatible: mesalazine, thiopurines, anti-TNF; avoid methotrexate, thalidomide), children (growth), older adults (cautious immunosuppression), pouchitis after ileal pouch–anal anastomosis.

### 47.8 Coeliac disease, malabsorption, and functional disorders

- **Coeliac disease** (Chapter 26): **tTG-IgA (with total IgA)** while eating gluten; **duodenal biopsies** in adults (≥4 from the distal duodenum and one from the bulb) (serology-only diagnosis acceptable in children with tTG >10× ULN and positive EMA); **lifelong gluten-free diet** with dietitian support; check iron, B12, folate, vitamin D, bone density, thyroid; screen first-degree relatives; **complications:** refractory disease, enteropathy-associated T-cell lymphoma, small bowel adenocarcinoma, hyposplenism (vaccinate), osteoporosis; associated type 1 diabetes, autoimmune thyroid disease, dermatitis herpetiformis, IgA deficiency.
- **Small intestinal bacterial overgrowth (SIBO):** bloating, diarrhoea, malabsorption in anatomical or motility disorders; breath tests, empirical rifaximin.
- **Other malabsorptive causes:** chronic pancreatitis, bile acid malabsorption, Crohn's, **tropical sprue, giardiasis, Whipple disease** (*Tropheryma whipplei*: arthralgia, diarrhoea, weight loss, neurological; PAS-positive macrophages), short bowel syndrome, **lactose intolerance** (hydrogen breath test; dietary lactose reduction).
- **Irritable bowel syndrome and functional disorders:** Chapter 25 (Rome IV); positive diagnosis without alarm features; first-line dietary advice (regular meals, low FODMAP under dietitian guidance), fibre adjustment, antispasmodics, loperamide/laxatives, **tricyclic antidepressants,** gut-directed hypnotherapy/CBT; linaclotide for IBS-C; rifaximin or eluxadoline for IBS-D.

### 47.9 Colorectal disease

#### Diverticular disease

- **Diverticulosis** (asymptomatic outpouchings; Western diets, ageing) rarely causes symptoms. **Acute diverticulitis:** left iliac fossa pain, fever, change in bowel habit, tender mass; **CT** to confirm and detect complications (abscess, perforation, fistula, obstruction); **uncomplicated** (immunocompetent): **observation/supportive care ± antibiotics** (antibiotics reserved for systemic features, immunosuppression, comorbidity); **complicated:** antibiotics (co-amoxiclav; or ciprofloxacin + metronidazole), **percutaneous drainage** of abscesses >4–5 cm, **surgery** (Hartmann's or resection with anastomosis) for peritonitis, obstruction, failed non-operative therapy. **Colonoscopy 6–8 weeks after** an episode (exclude cancer, especially with complicated disease). **Diverticular bleeding:** Chapter 27. **Recurrence prevention:** fibre, weight loss, stop smoking, avoid NSAIDs, mesalazine has limited evidence.

#### Colorectal cancer (CRC)

- **Epidemiology:** third most common cancer; risk rises after 50 (increasing in younger adults). **Risk factors:** age, family history, **IBD, adenomatous polyps,** diet (red/processed meat, low fibre), obesity, diabetes, smoking, alcohol, physical inactivity, inherited syndromes.
- **Inherited syndromes:** **Lynch syndrome** (hereditary nonpolyposis colorectal cancer: MLH1, MSH2, MSH6, PMS2 mutations; early-onset CRC, endometrial, ovarian, gastric, urinary tract cancers; **MMR immunohistochemistry/MSI testing of all CRCs,** germline testing, **colonoscopy every 1–2 years from age 20–25,** aspirin chemoprevention), **familial adenomatous polyposis** (APC; hundreds of polyps by teenage years; prophylactic colectomy), MUTYH-associated polyposis, Peutz–Jeghers, juvenile polyposis.
- **Presentation:** **rectal bleeding, change in bowel habit, iron-deficiency anaemia, abdominal pain, weight loss, palpable mass,** obstruction, perforation; right-sided (occult blood, anaemia, mass), left-sided/rectal (altered habit, bright red bleeding, tenesmus).
- **Diagnosis and staging:** **colonoscopy with biopsy** (or CT colonography), **CT chest/abdomen/pelvis,** **MRI pelvis** (rectal cancer), **CEA** (baseline, follow-up), molecular testing (**RAS, BRAF, MSI/dMMR, HER2**).
- **Treatment:** **surgery** (colectomy with lymph node dissection; total mesorectal excision for rectal), **adjuvant chemotherapy** (FOLFOX/CAPOX) for **stage III** and high-risk stage II colon cancer; **rectal cancer:** neoadjuvant (chemo)radiotherapy or total neoadjuvant therapy, organ preservation in selected responders; **metastatic:** liver/lung resection or ablation when feasible, systemic chemotherapy (FOLFOX/FOLFIRI) + biologics (anti-EGFR for RAS wild-type left-sided; bevacizumab), **immunotherapy for dMMR/MSI-high tumours,** targeted agents (BRAF, KRAS G12C, HER2).
- **Screening (reduces mortality):** **faecal immunochemical test (FIT)** every 1–2 years, **colonoscopy every 10 years,** flexible sigmoidoscopy, CT colonography, **age 45–50 to 74/75** (country-specific); high-risk (family history, IBD, Lynch) earlier and more intensive.
- **Polyp surveillance:** adenomas (size ≥10 mm, ≥3 adenomas, high-grade dysplasia, villous histology) have shorter surveillance intervals (3 years) than low-risk (5–10 years), per guidelines; **serrated polyps** ≥10 mm or with dysplasia increase risk.
- **Anal cancer:** HPV-related; chemoradiotherapy; screening in high-risk (HIV, MSM). **Haemorrhoids, fissures, fistulae:** common benign causes of bleeding and pain (examination, proctoscopy, fibre, topical therapy, banding; do not assume benign in older adults: investigate).

### 47.10 Gallstones and biliary disease

(See Chapters 25, 27, 42.)

- **Gallstones:** cholesterol (80%), pigment; risk: female, obesity, age, rapid weight loss, pregnancy, Crohn's, haemolysis. **Asymptomatic stones:** no treatment. **Biliary colic:** **laparoscopic cholecystectomy.** **Acute cholecystitis:** antibiotics, early cholecystectomy (within 72 hours–7 days). **Choledocholithiasis/cholangitis:** MRCP/EUS, **ERCP with sphincterotomy and stone extraction** (risk-stratify: high risk: CBD stone on imaging, ascending cholangitis, bilirubin >70 µmol/L with dilated CBD); **cholecystectomy** thereafter. **Gallstone pancreatitis:** cholecystectomy in the same admission. **Gallbladder polyps:** surgery if >10 mm, growing, or with risk factors; **porcelain gallbladder, gallbladder cancer** (poor prognosis).
- **Cholangiocarcinoma:** PSC, liver flukes (*Opisthorchis*), choledochal cysts, hepatolithiasis; painless jaundice, weight loss; MRCP, CT, EUS/ERCP with brushings; resection/transplant for selected hilar tumours, chemotherapy (gemcitabine–cisplatin + durvalumab), targeted therapy (FGFR2, IDH1).
- **Biliary dyskinesia, sphincter of Oddi dysfunction:** controversial, specialist evaluation.

### 47.11 Worked cases

#### Case 1: Dysphagia and weight loss

*A 67-year-old man with long-standing heartburn has 3 months of progressive dysphagia to solids and 8 kg weight loss. Upper endoscopy: ulcerated distal oesophageal mass with Barrett's mucosa above. Biopsy: adenocarcinoma. CT: no distant metastases; EUS: T3N1.*

**Reasoning.** **Oesophageal adenocarcinoma** arising in Barrett's oesophagus (alarm features).

**Action.** MDT: **neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy (CROSS) or perioperative chemotherapy (FLOT) followed by oesophagectomy,** nutritional support (stent or feeding tube if needed), stop smoking and alcohol; surveillance of Barrett's in at-risk patients may have prevented this.

#### Case 2: Duodenal ulcer and *H. pylori*

*A 41-year-old woman has 2 months of epigastric pain, worse at night and relieved by food. Not on NSAIDs. Stool antigen positive. Endoscopy: 8-mm duodenal ulcer.*

**Reasoning.** ***H. pylori*-associated duodenal ulcer.**

**Action.** **Bismuth quadruple therapy for 14 days** (or clarithromycin triple therapy if local resistance <15%) plus PPI; repeat test for eradication ≥4 weeks later; no NSAIDs; if refractory/recurrent: gastrin level (Zollinger–Ellison).

#### Case 3: Chronic hepatitis B

*A 38-year-old man from Vietnam is found to have HBsAg positive, HBeAg positive, ALT 110, HBV DNA 8.2 log IU/mL, elastography 9.5 kPa (F3). Anti-HIV and HCV negative.*

**Reasoning.** **Chronic hepatitis B, immune-active, with advanced fibrosis:** treatment indicated.

**Action.** **Tenofovir or entecavir** long term, hepatitis A vaccination, HCC surveillance (ultrasound ± AFP every 6 months), screen family members and partners for vaccination, monitor HBV DNA and ALT, avoid alcohol; counsel on reactivation risk if immunosuppression.

#### Case 4: Decompensated cirrhosis with SBP

*A 56-year-old man with alcohol-related cirrhosis has abdominal distension, fever 38.2 °C, confusion and abdominal tenderness. Ascitic fluid: neutrophils 620/µL, albumin 8 g/L, protein 10 g/L. Creatinine 140 µmol/L (baseline 85).*

**Reasoning.** **Spontaneous bacterial peritonitis** with early HRS-AKI risk and encephalopathy.

**Action.** **Ceftriaxone 2 g IV daily,** **albumin 1.5 g/kg day 1 and 1 g/kg day 3,** stop diuretics and nephrotoxins, lactulose for encephalopathy, nutrition, alcohol cessation support; secondary SBP prophylaxis; transplant assessment.

#### Case 5: Acute severe ulcerative colitis

*A 24-year-old woman with known ulcerative colitis has 10 bloody stools/day for 5 days, abdominal pain, HR 112, temperature 38.1 °C, Hb 98, CRP 92, albumin 28. Stool *C. difficile* negative. Abdominal X-ray: transverse colon 5 cm.*

**Reasoning.** **Acute severe UC** (Truelove–Witts criteria).

**Action.** Admit; **IV hydrocortisone 100 mg qds,** LMWH, IV fluids/electrolytes, nutrition, flexible sigmoidoscopy (severity, CMV); **joint gastroenterology–surgical review;** reassess day 3: persistent >8 stools/day or CRP >45 → **infliximab (or ciclosporin) rescue** or colectomy; avoid opioids/antimotility drugs; plan maintenance therapy.

#### Case 6: Crohn's disease with perianal fistula

*A 29-year-old man with 6 months of diarrhoea, weight loss, terminal ileitis on colonoscopy (granulomas on biopsy) and painful perianal swelling with discharge. MRI: transsphincteric fistula with abscess.*

**Reasoning.** **Ileocolonic Crohn's disease with complex perianal fistulising disease.**

**Action.** **Surgical drainage with seton,** antibiotics (ciprofloxacin/metronidazole short term), then **anti-TNF (infliximab ± azathioprine)** for fistula healing, stop smoking, TB/HBV screening before biologics, nutritional assessment, bone health; monitor with MRI and clinical review.

#### Case 7: MASLD with significant fibrosis

*A 52-year-old man with type 2 diabetes, BMI 34 and mildly raised ALT has hepatic steatosis on ultrasound. FIB-4 2.1; elastography 11.8 kPa; viral serology and iron studies negative.*

**Reasoning.** **MASLD with probable advanced fibrosis (F3).**

**Action.** Weight loss target ≥10% (Mediterranean diet, exercise; GLP-1 receptor agonist such as semaglutide or tirzepatide; consider bariatric surgery), optimise diabetes (SGLT2 inhibitor/GLP-1 RA, pioglitazone), statin, treat hypertension; **resmetirom** where available; HCC surveillance and variceal screening if cirrhosis; hepatology referral; vaccination.

### 47.12 Common pitfalls

- **Treating dyspepsia or reflux empirically** in patients with alarm features or age >55 without endoscopy.
- **Testing for *H. pylori* on a PPI** (false negatives); not confirming eradication in ulcer disease; using clarithromycin triple therapy where resistance is high.
- **Missing achalasia or eosinophilic oesophagitis** in young patients with dysphagia and "refractory reflux."
- **Failing to test for HBV before immunosuppression;** ignoring isolated anti-HBc; **not offering HCV treatment** (DAAs cure >95%).
- **Attributing raised transaminases to alcohol or fatty liver** without a screen (viral, autoimmune, Wilson, haemochromatosis, drugs).
- **Assuming normal LFTs exclude cirrhosis** (use elastography/platelets/FIB-4); **missing MASLD** and cardiovascular risk.
- **Not tapping ascites** in admitted cirrhotic patients; delaying antibiotics and albumin in SBP; overusing diuretics causing HRS/hyponatraemia.
- **Giving NSAIDs, benzodiazepines, or opioids** in cirrhosis; correcting INR with plasma routinely.
- **Missing HCC surveillance.**
- **Treating acute severe colitis without early surgical involvement,** or delaying rescue therapy beyond day 3–5; using antidiarrhoeals; missing *C. difficile* and CMV.
- **Starting biologics without screening for TB/HBV;** continuing steroids long term.
- **Labelling IBS in the presence of alarm features, raised calprotectin or anaemia.**
- **Missing coeliac disease** by testing after gluten withdrawal.
- **Skipping colonoscopy after diverticulitis,** or not screening/surveying for colorectal cancer in high-risk groups; not performing MMR testing for Lynch syndrome.

### 47.13 Key points

- Upper GI symptoms with alarm features need endoscopy; otherwise test-and-treat *H. pylori* and trial PPI; Barrett's and achalasia/EoE are important diagnoses.
- Viral hepatitis: interpret serology; treat HBV with tenofovir/entecavir when indicated; **cure HCV with DAAs;** vaccinate against HAV/HBV; screen before immunosuppression.
- Alcohol-related and MASLD are the main causes of chronic liver disease: stage fibrosis non-invasively (FIB-4, elastography), treat metabolic risk, and ensure abstinence in ALD.
- Cirrhosis: Child–Pugh/MELD; manage ascites (sodium restriction, spironolactone ± furosemide), SBP (ceftriaxone + albumin), varices (beta-blocker/EVL), HE (lactulose, rifaximin), HRS (terlipressin + albumin), HCC surveillance every 6 months; refer for transplant.
- Pancreatic disease: gallstones and alcohol for acute pancreatitis; chronic pancreatitis needs enzyme replacement and pain control; pancreatic cancer presents late (jaundice, weight loss).
- IBD: UC (continuous, bloody) vs Crohn's (transmural, skip lesions); stepwise therapy, early biologics in aggressive Crohn's; acute severe UC needs IV steroids, day-3 assessment and rescue therapy or colectomy.
- Colorectal cancer: screening (FIT/colonoscopy), Lynch syndrome testing, polyp surveillance, multimodal treatment, immunotherapy for dMMR.

### 47.14 Self-assessment

1. List the alarm features that mandate upper endoscopy in dyspepsia or reflux.
   *Answer: Age >55 with new symptoms, progressive dysphagia/odynophagia, persistent vomiting, GI bleeding or iron-deficiency anaemia, unexplained weight loss, palpable abdominal mass, jaundice, family history of upper GI cancer.*

2. Interpret HBsAg negative, anti-HBc positive, anti-HBs positive.
   *Answer: Resolved past HBV infection with immunity; at risk of reactivation if immunosuppressed (prophylaxis/monitoring needed).*

3. What is the treatment of SBP and why is albumin given?
   *Answer: IV ceftriaxone or cefotaxime (neutrophils ≥250/µL), plus albumin 1.5 g/kg day 1 and 1 g/kg day 3 to reduce HRS-AKI and mortality.*

4. How is acute severe ulcerative colitis managed on day 0 and day 3?
   *Answer: Day 0: IV hydrocortisone 100 mg four times daily, LMWH, fluids/electrolytes, exclude infection, early surgical review; day 3: if stool frequency >8/day or CRP >45 with 3–8 stools, rescue therapy (infliximab or ciclosporin) or colectomy.*

5. State the FIB-4 index and its use.
   *Answer: (Age × AST)/(platelets × √ALT); <1.3 low risk of advanced fibrosis, >2.67 high risk, 1.3–2.67 needs elastography; used to triage MASLD.*

6. Who should have Lynch syndrome testing and what surveillance applies?
   *Answer: All colorectal cancers (MMR immunohistochemistry/MSI) with germline testing if abnormal, and relatives; colonoscopy every 1–2 years from age 20–25, with aspirin chemoprevention considered.*
