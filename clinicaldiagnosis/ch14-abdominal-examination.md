## Chapter 14. Abdominal Examination

### Learning objectives

After studying this chapter you should be able to:

1. Prepare the patient and perform a complete abdominal examination in the correct order: inspect, auscultate, percuss, palpate.
2. Identify the causes of abdominal distension and recognise ascites with shifting dullness.
3. Distinguish voluntary guarding, involuntary guarding, rigidity and rebound tenderness, and explain their significance.
4. Palpate and describe the liver, spleen, kidneys, aorta and bladder, and recognise abnormal masses.
5. Examine for hernias and understand the signs of strangulation.
6. Perform and interpret a digital rectal examination, and know its indications.
7. Recognise the bedside patterns of peritonitis, bowel obstruction, chronic liver disease, and ruptured aortic aneurysm.
8. Take account of the atypical presentation of abdominal emergencies in older adults, pregnant women, and immunosuppressed patients.

---

### 14.1 Approach and preparation

The abdominal examination answers a series of questions:

1. **Is there peritonitis or another emergency?** (Rigid, silent abdomen; shock; pulsatile mass.)
2. **Where is the problem, and what is it?** (Site of tenderness, organ enlargement, mass.)
3. **Is there evidence of chronic liver disease, malignancy or systemic disease?**
4. **Is there obstruction, bleeding or perforation?**

The abdomen is a large space with few reliable external landmarks, and many structures overlap. The examination is a combination of **observation, gentle systematic palpation, and logical reasoning from the anatomy.**

#### Preparation

- Introduce yourself; explain what you will do; obtain consent; offer a **chaperone**, particularly when examining the groin, genitalia or performing a rectal or pelvic examination.
- Ensure **privacy** and **warmth**. Ask the patient to empty the bladder if necessary.
- Position: **supine and flat**, with **one pillow** under the head, arms by the sides (not behind the head, which tightens the abdominal wall), knees slightly bent if it relaxes the abdomen.
- **Exposure:** from the **xiphisternum to the symphysis pubis** (and inguinal areas/genitalia when assessing hernias). Cover the chest and the genitalia as far as possible.
- Warm hands, short nails, and a relaxed manner. Watch the patient's face during palpation (grimacing is a more honest sign than words).
- Ask the patient to **point to the painful area** and **start palpation as far from it as possible.**
- Stand at the patient's right side. Kneel or sit at the bedside for palpation of the liver and spleen so that your forearm is level with the abdomen.

#### Nine regions and four quadrants

The abdomen may be divided into **four quadrants** (right and left upper and lower, by horizontal and vertical lines through the umbilicus) or **nine regions** (right hypochondrium, epigastrium, left hypochondrium; right flank, umbilical, left flank; right iliac fossa, hypogastrium (suprapubic), left iliac fossa).

### 14.2 General inspection and the "end of the bed"

Before the abdomen, look at the whole patient (Chapter 10).

- **Appearance:** Do they look well? In pain? Lying still (peritonitis) or writhing (colic)? Knees drawn up? Jaundiced? Pale? Dehydrated?
- **Breathing:** Shallow breathing (peritonitis, pain), Kussmaul (acidosis).
- **Vital signs:** Tachycardia, hypotension, fever, raised respiratory rate.
- **Hands:** clubbing, leuconychia, palmar erythema, Dupuytren contracture, asterixis, pallor of palmar creases, koilonychia.
- **Face and neck:** jaundice (sclerae), anaemia (conjunctivae), parotid enlargement, angular stomatitis, glossitis, mouth ulcers (Crohn's), spider naevi, **Virchow's node** (left supraclavicular), foetor.
- **Chest:** gynaecomastia, loss of body hair, spider naevi (liver disease).
- **Mouth:** mucous membranes (hydration), tongue (dry, coated), dental state, ulcers, pigmentation (Peutz–Jeghers), candidiasis, breath odour.

### 14.3 Inspection of the abdomen

Look from the foot of the bed and from the side, at the level of the abdomen.

| Finding | Interpretation |
|---|---|
| **Contour: flat, scaphoid, distended, protuberant** | Distension: see below. Scaphoid: weight loss |
| **Symmetry** | Asymmetry: masses, hernias, organomegaly |
| **Movement with respiration** | Reduced or absent in peritonitis ("board-like" abdomen does not move) |
| **Scars** | Previous surgery (adhesions, hernia risk). Laparoscopy scars are small; Kocher (right subcostal, cholecystectomy); McBurney/gridiron (appendicectomy); midline laparotomy; Pfannenstiel (suprapubic, gynaecological and caesarean); loin (nephrectomy); stoma sites |
| **Stomas** | Ileostomy (right iliac fossa, spouted), colostomy (left iliac fossa, flush), urostomy |
| **Skin changes** | Striae (pregnancy, Cushing, weight change, ascites), spider naevi, bruising, rashes, herpes zoster, pigmentation |
| **Cullen sign** | Periumbilical bruising; haemoperitoneum or acute pancreatitis (retroperitoneal haemorrhage) |
| **Grey Turner sign** | Flank bruising; retroperitoneal haemorrhage (severe pancreatitis, ruptured AAA, trauma) |
| **Caput medusae** | Dilated veins radiating from the umbilicus; portal hypertension |
| **Dilated veins on the flanks (flow upward)** | Inferior vena cava obstruction |
| **Visible peristalsis** | Intestinal obstruction (small bowel in the central abdomen; pyloric stenosis in the epigastrium) |
| **Visible pulsation** | Abdominal aortic aneurysm (in thin patients), or normal transmitted pulsation |
| **Umbilicus** | Everted (ascites, hernia), Sister Mary Joseph nodule (periumbilical metastatic deposit: gastric, ovarian, pancreatic, colon) |
| **Hernias** | Visible bulges at the umbilicus, linea alba, groin, scars; ask the patient to cough or lift the head |
| **Masses** | Visible swelling |

#### Abdominal distension: the "5 Fs"

- **Fat** (obesity)
- **Fluid** (ascites, ovarian cyst, bladder)
- **Flatus** (bowel gas: obstruction, ileus)
- **Faeces** (constipation)
- **Fetus** (pregnancy)
- Also: **fibroids and tumours** (large masses).

Distinguish by examination: fat is uniformly soft; flatus is resonant on percussion; fluid is dull in the flanks with shifting dullness; faeces cause indentable masses; a pregnant uterus arises from the pelvis.

### 14.4 Auscultation

Auscultation comes **before palpation and percussion** in the abdomen, because palpation may alter bowel sounds. Use the diaphragm, applied lightly.

#### Bowel sounds

Listen for at least **30 seconds**, and if silent, for up to **2 minutes** in each quadrant (usually enough to listen in one or two places, as sounds transmit).

| Finding | Interpretation |
|---|---|
| **Normal** | Intermittent gurgles, 5–30 per minute |
| **Absent** (after 2 minutes) | Paralytic ileus, generalised peritonitis, late obstruction |
| **Hyperactive** | Gastroenteritis, early obstruction, inflammatory bowel disease, after meals |
| **Tinkling, high-pitched, rushes with colicky pain** | Mechanical small bowel obstruction |
| **Succussion splash** | Gastric outlet obstruction (hold the stomach; shake the patient gently; listen to a splash >3 hours after fluid intake) |

Bowel sounds are of limited accuracy; do not rely on them to rule in or out peritonitis, which is a clinical diagnosis based on the whole picture.

#### Bruits

Listen over the **aorta** (epigastrium), **renal arteries** (2–3 cm above and lateral to the umbilicus on each side), **iliac and femoral arteries**. A bruit suggests arterial stenosis (renal artery stenosis in resistant hypertension; atherosclerosis). A **hepatic bruit** suggests hepatocellular carcinoma or alcoholic hepatitis; a **hepatic rub** suggests recent biopsy, tumour or infection; a **venous hum** (epigastrium, portal hypertension).

### 14.5 Percussion

Percussion helps determine **organ size, fluid and gas.**

- **General percussion** in all nine regions: tympanic (resonant) over gas-filled bowel; dull over solid organs, fluid or faeces.
- **Liver span:** percuss from the resonant lung down the right mid-clavicular line until dullness is reached (upper border, usually 5th–6th intercostal space), and from the resonant abdomen up until dullness (lower border). The normal span is about **12 cm** (range 6–12 cm in the right mid-clavicular line; shorter in women). Liver span is more accurate than palpation alone; a palpable edge does not necessarily indicate hepatomegaly (see 14.6).
- **Spleen:** percuss the left lower anterior chest (Traube space) and the area of the left costal margin. Dullness in Traube's space suggests splenomegaly.
- **Bladder:** percuss upward from the pubic symphysis; dullness above the pubis suggests a distended bladder (urinary retention).
- **Suprapubic masses.**

#### Ascites

1. **Flank dullness:** with the patient supine, percuss from the midline (tympanic: gas-filled bowel floats on fluid) to the flanks; the transition to dullness is marked.
2. **Shifting dullness:** mark the point where the percussion note changes from resonant to dull, ask the patient to roll onto the opposite side and wait 30–60 seconds, then re-percuss: the dullness shifts to the now-dependent side, and the previously dull area becomes resonant. Requires about **1.5 litres or more** of fluid for clinical detection.
3. **Fluid thrill:** ask the patient (or an assistant) to put the edge of a hand firmly on the midline of the abdomen (to block transmission through the wall), then flick one flank while feeling the opposite flank for a ripple. Positive in large-volume ascites. Less sensitive than shifting dullness.
4. **Puddle sign** (rarely used): detects small volumes with the patient on hands and knees.

Ultrasound is more sensitive than examination for small volumes of ascites.

### 14.6 Palpation

#### Technique and principles

- **Light palpation first:** using the flat of your fingers (not the fingertips), press gently (about 1 cm) in each of the nine regions, beginning away from any area of pain. Observe for tenderness, guarding and superficial masses. Watch the patient's face.
- **Deep palpation:** press more firmly (about 4–5 cm) to detect organs and masses.
- Use a **smooth, gentle** touch. Distract the patient with conversation; ask them to breathe through the mouth (relaxes abdominal muscles).
- Examine the **painful area last.**
- Note the **site, size, shape, surface, edge, consistency, mobility, pulsation, tenderness, and whether the mass moves with respiration** of any mass.

#### Tenderness, guarding and rigidity

| Sign | Description | Significance |
|---|---|---|
| **Tenderness** | Pain on palpation | Localised pain points to the underlying organ |
| **Voluntary guarding** | Conscious tightening of the abdominal muscles; relaxes with distraction or reassurance | Anxiety, fear; not specific |
| **Involuntary guarding** | Reflex muscle spasm over an inflamed area; cannot be overcome by distraction | Localised peritoneal irritation (e.g. appendicitis, cholecystitis) |
| **Rigidity** | Persistent, board-like abdominal wall, diffuse | Generalised peritonitis (e.g. perforated viscus). A surgical emergency |
| **Rebound tenderness** | Pain worse on sudden release of pressure | Peritoneal inflammation. Because it is painful and unpleasant, avoid in the first instance; **percussion tenderness** or **pain on coughing or shaking the bed** are alternatives |
| **Percussion tenderness** | Pain on gentle percussion | Peritonitis (a gentler alternative to rebound) |
| **Carnett sign** | Pain unchanged or worse when the patient tenses the abdominal wall (raises the head or legs) | Abdominal wall pain (e.g. rectus sheath, nerve entrapment). Intra-abdominal pain is usually reduced |

#### Signs for specific diagnoses

- **Murphy sign** (cholecystitis): place fingers under the right costal margin at the mid-clavicular line, ask the patient to take a deep breath. Pain and inspiratory arrest as the inflamed gallbladder meets the fingers = positive. (Ultrasonographic Murphy sign is also used.)
- **Rovsing sign** (appendicitis): palpation of the left iliac fossa causes pain in the right iliac fossa.
- **Psoas sign** (retrocaecal appendicitis, psoas abscess): pain on extension of the right hip (patient lying on the left side).
- **Obturator sign** (pelvic appendicitis/abscess): pain on internal rotation of the flexed right hip.
- **McBurney point tenderness:** one third of the way from the anterior superior iliac spine to the umbilicus.
- **Courvoisier law:** a palpable, non-tender gallbladder in a jaundiced patient suggests malignant obstruction rather than gallstones.
- **Boas sign:** right subscapular hyperaesthesia in cholecystitis (rare).
- **Cullen and Grey Turner signs:** see above.
- **Kehr sign:** left shoulder tip pain (diaphragmatic irritation; ruptured spleen).

The diagnostic accuracy of individual signs for appendicitis is modest. A combination of migrating pain, anorexia, right iliac fossa tenderness and rigidity increases the likelihood, but scoring systems (Alvarado, AIR) and imaging are used in uncertain cases (Chapter 70).

#### The liver

**Technique.** Start in the right iliac fossa (to avoid missing a massive liver). With the right hand's flat of the fingers, placed lateral to the rectus muscle and pointing upward, ask the patient to breathe in deeply; as the liver descends, the fingers should feel the edge. Move up by 1–2 cm steps toward the costal margin. Each breath, press in on expiration and rise on inspiration.

**Assess:** edge (sharp or rounded), surface (smooth or nodular), consistency (soft, firm, hard), tenderness, pulsatility, size (centimetres below the costal margin in the mid-clavicular line, plus percussion for upper border).

- A palpable liver edge is not necessarily enlarged. The liver may be palpable up to 1–2 cm below the costal margin in a healthy, thin individual, or **displaced downward** by emphysema (check the upper border), or have a **Riedel lobe** (a tongue-like extension of the right lobe).
- **Causes of hepatomegaly:**

| Type | Causes |
|---|---|
| **Smooth, tender** | Hepatitis (viral, alcoholic), congestive cardiac failure (may be pulsatile in tricuspid regurgitation), Budd–Chiari syndrome, infection (abscess) |
| **Smooth, non-tender, firm** | Fatty liver, early cirrhosis, infiltration (amyloid, lymphoma, leukaemia), haemochromatosis, myeloproliferative disorders |
| **Hard, irregular, nodular** | Metastases, hepatocellular carcinoma, macronodular cirrhosis |
| **Massive hepatomegaly** | Metastases, polycystic liver, hydatid cyst, myelofibrosis (with massive splenomegaly), lymphoma, glycogen storage disease |
| **Small, shrunken liver** | Advanced cirrhosis |

#### The spleen

**Technique.** Start in the right iliac fossa (again to avoid missing a massive spleen) and move diagonally toward the left costal margin as the patient breathes. A normal spleen is not palpable; it must enlarge roughly **two to three times** before it is palpable. Feel for a **notch** on its medial border, a firm edge that moves down on inspiration, and "cannot get above it." Roll the patient onto the **right side** (right lateral position) and feel with the left hand lifting the left costal margin from behind.

- **Differentiating from the left kidney:** the spleen has a notch, descends toward the right iliac fossa, you cannot get above it, it is dull to percussion, and it does not usually ballot; the kidney is balloted bimanually, has a resonant percussion note (bowel anterior) and you can get above it.
- **Causes of splenomegaly:**

| Size | Causes |
|---|---|
| **Massive (extending below umbilicus)** | Chronic myeloid leukaemia, myelofibrosis, malaria (tropical splenomegaly), kala-azar (visceral leishmaniasis), Gaucher disease |
| **Moderate** | Portal hypertension (cirrhosis), lymphoma, chronic lymphocytic leukaemia, haemolytic anaemia, polycythaemia vera, thalassaemia, sarcoidosis, infections (endocarditis, malaria, TB, typhoid, brucellosis), rheumatoid arthritis (Felty syndrome) |
| **Mild** | Infectious mononucleosis, hepatitis, SLE, viral infection, congestion |

#### The kidneys

**Bimanual palpation (ballotting).** Place one hand behind the patient in the loin (below the 12th rib), and the other on the abdomen anteriorly, lateral to the rectus. On inspiration press the anterior hand down while lifting the posterior hand; a kidney will be felt between the hands (ballotable). Normal kidneys are often not palpable (the right may be felt in thin patients). **Enlarged kidneys:** polycystic kidney disease (bilateral, irregular), hydronephrosis, tumour (renal cell carcinoma; unilateral), renal vein thrombosis. Renal angle tenderness (percuss with a closed fist over the costovertebral angle) suggests pyelonephritis or a stone.

#### The aorta

Palpate in the epigastrium, just above the umbilicus, using both hands on either side of the midline. A normal aorta is felt as a pulsation (diameter up to ~2–3 cm). In an aneurysm the mass is **expansile** (the fingers are pushed apart in two directions, not just upward). Do not press hard on a tender pulsatile mass. Suspect an aneurysm particularly in older men with hypertension or smoking. A **ruptured aneurysm** presents with sudden severe abdominal, back or flank pain, shock, and a pulsatile mass, but may be misdiagnosed as renal colic.

#### The bladder

Palpable only when distended; dull to percussion in the suprapubic region, smooth and round, sometimes tender. Causes include prostatic enlargement, urethral stricture, neurogenic bladder, drug-induced retention (anticholinergics, opioids), constipation and delirium.

#### Other masses

Describe site, size, mobility, pulsatility, and relationship to organs. Common abdominal masses by region:

| Region | Possible masses |
|---|---|
| Right hypochondrium | Liver, gallbladder (empyema, mucocele, carcinoma), hepatic flexure, right kidney |
| Epigastrium | Stomach carcinoma, pancreatic pseudocyst or tumour, aortic aneurysm, left lobe of the liver |
| Left hypochondrium | Spleen, left kidney, splenic flexure |
| Flanks | Kidneys, colon |
| Umbilical region | Aneurysm, lymph nodes, small bowel masses, hernia |
| Right iliac fossa | Appendix mass/abscess, caecal carcinoma, Crohn's disease mass, ovarian cyst, ectopic pregnancy, transplanted kidney |
| Left iliac fossa | Sigmoid colon (faeces, tumour, diverticular mass), ovarian mass |
| Hypogastrium | Bladder, uterus (pregnancy, fibroids), ovarian tumours |

### 14.7 Hernias

Examine the umbilicus, linea alba, previous scars, and the **groin**.

- Ask the patient to stand (hernias reduce when supine) and **cough** or strain. Observe and palpate for a cough impulse.
- **Inguinal hernia:** a swelling above and medial to the pubic tubercle. **Indirect** (through the deep ring, may descend into the scrotum, controlled by pressure over the deep ring) vs **direct** (through the posterior wall of the inguinal canal, bulges forward, not controlled by deep ring pressure). The distinction is often academic: most inguinal hernias are repaired.
- **Femoral hernia:** a swelling **below and lateral to the pubic tubercle**, more common in women, high risk of strangulation. Femoral hernia must be considered in an elderly woman with small bowel obstruction.
- **Incisional, umbilical and epigastric hernias.**
- **Signs of obstruction/strangulation:** tense, tender, irreducible, discoloured swelling, absent cough impulse, vomiting, constipation. This is a surgical emergency.
- **Testicular and scrotal examination** (Chapter 19) in men with groin pain: torsion, epididymitis.

### 14.8 Digital rectal examination (DRE)

#### Indications

Gastrointestinal bleeding, change in bowel habit, iron-deficiency anaemia, abdominal pain (selected), faecal incontinence, suspected faecal impaction, anorectal symptoms (pain, mass, discharge), urinary symptoms in men (prostate), back pain with suspected cauda equina syndrome (anal tone, perianal sensation), pelvic pain, trauma, and suspected prostate disease.

It is **not** routine for all abdominal pain (it adds little in uncomplicated appendicitis), but should not be omitted when indicated.

#### Technique

1. Explain the procedure, obtain verbal consent, and offer a chaperone. Ensure privacy.
2. Position the patient in the **left lateral position** with knees drawn up toward the chest.
3. **Inspect** the perianal skin (haemorrhoids, fissures, fistulae, skin tags, rashes, warts, prolapse, scars, soiling).
4. Apply lubricant to a gloved index finger. Place the pad on the anal verge, ask the patient to relax and breathe; insert slowly.
5. **Assess:** anal tone (at rest and on squeezing), tenderness, masses (location, size, mobility, surface), stool (impacted, soft, hard), blood or mucus on the glove (colour: fresh red, melaena (black, tarry, foul), pale/clay-coloured in obstructive jaundice). **Prostate:** size, symmetry, consistency (smooth, firm, rubbery in BPH; hard nodular in cancer; boggy and tender in prostatitis); median sulcus. **Cervix** may be felt anteriorly in women.
6. Withdraw and examine the glove; clean the patient and allow them to dress.
7. Faecal occult blood testing from a digital rectal sample is **not recommended** as a screening test.

#### Findings

| Finding | Significance |
|---|---|
| Fresh blood on the glove | Haemorrhoids, fissure, rectal cancer, colitis, polyps |
| Melaena | Upper GI bleed |
| Hard stool, impaction | Constipation, faecal overflow |
| Empty, ballooned rectum | Intestinal obstruction, paralytic ileus, Hirschsprung (child), spinal cord compression |
| Tender mass on the right anterior wall | Pelvic abscess, appendicitis (pelvic) |
| Low anal tone | Spinal cord lesion, cauda equina syndrome, sphincter injury |
| Hard irregular nodular prostate | Prostate cancer |
| Rectal mass, ulcerated, fixed | Rectal cancer |

### 14.9 Completing the examination

- **Genitalia and groin** if indicated (Chapter 19).
- **Pelvic examination** in women with lower abdominal or pelvic pain, bleeding, discharge (Chapter 19).
- **Urine dipstick** (blood, protein, glucose, leucocytes, nitrites, ketones, bilirubin), **pregnancy test** in women of reproductive age.
- **Temperature, pulse, blood pressure, respiratory rate.**
- **Chest examination** (lower lobe pneumonia and pulmonary embolism cause abdominal pain), **cardiovascular examination** (AF with mesenteric ischaemia, aortic aneurysm).
- **Examination of the back and spine** (renal angle tenderness, spinal tenderness).
- **Lymph nodes:** supraclavicular, axillary, inguinal.
- **Peripheral oedema.**

**Present your findings** in a structured way: general appearance and vital signs, hands and face, abdomen (inspection, palpation, percussion, auscultation), hernial orifices, DRE if performed.

### 14.10 Clinical patterns

| Syndrome | Typical bedside findings |
|---|---|
| **Generalised peritonitis** (perforation) | Lies still, shallow breathing, tachycardia, fever, board-like rigidity, rebound/percussion tenderness, absent bowel sounds, shock in late stages |
| **Localised peritonitis (e.g. appendicitis)** | Right iliac fossa tenderness, guarding, rebound, Rovsing, psoas/obturator signs; low-grade fever; anorexia |
| **Acute cholecystitis** | Right upper quadrant pain and tenderness, Murphy sign, fever, leucocytosis |
| **Small bowel obstruction** | Colicky central pain, vomiting (bilious or faeculent), distension, constipation, tinkling bowel sounds, empty rectum; look for hernias and scars |
| **Large bowel obstruction** | Gradual distension, little vomiting, constipation, flank fullness; distended caecum risks perforation |
| **Acute pancreatitis** | Severe epigastric pain radiating to the back, relieved by leaning forward, vomiting; tenderness; in severe cases Cullen/Grey Turner signs |
| **Ruptured abdominal aortic aneurysm** | Sudden severe abdominal/back pain, collapse, pulsatile expansile mass, hypotension; may mimic renal colic |
| **Mesenteric ischaemia** | Severe pain out of proportion to examination findings, vomiting, AF or vascular disease, lactate raised; late peritonitis |
| **Chronic liver disease with portal hypertension** | Jaundice, palmar erythema, spider naevi, gynaecomastia, ascites, caput medusae, splenomegaly, asterixis, small liver |
| **Ectopic pregnancy** | Lower abdominal pain, amenorrhoea, vaginal bleeding, shock, adnexal tenderness; positive pregnancy test |
| **Renal colic** | Colicky loin-to-groin pain, patient restless, haematuria; non-tender abdomen |
| **Urinary retention** | Suprapubic pain/distension, dull palpable bladder |
| **Gastroenteritis** | Diffuse mild tenderness, hyperactive bowel sounds, no peritonism |
| **Diverticulitis** | Left iliac fossa tenderness, fever, localised guarding; sometimes a mass |
| **Inflammatory bowel disease** | Chronic diarrhoea, weight loss, mouth ulcers, RIF mass (Crohn's), perianal disease, extra-intestinal signs |

#### Special populations

- **Older adults:** Serious intra-abdominal disease (appendicitis, perforation, mesenteric ischaemia, cholecystitis) may present with **minimal pain, little fever, a normal white count** and subtle tenderness. A low threshold for imaging is essential.
- **Pregnant women:** The appendix is displaced upward, so appendicitis may present with right upper quadrant pain. Examination findings are blunted by the stretched abdominal wall. Always consider ectopic pregnancy in the first trimester, and pre-eclampsia/HELLP (epigastric pain) later.
- **Children:** Observe behaviour; a child who jumps from the bed or plays is unlikely to have peritonitis. Examine gently, with distraction; assess the child's response to a bump in the ambulance, walking, or hopping. Consider intussusception, malrotation with volvulus (bilious vomiting is an emergency), testicular torsion, DKA, pneumonia and UTI.
- **Immunosuppressed patients (steroids, chemotherapy, HIV, transplant):** Peritoneal signs may be absent; infections (neutropenic enterocolitis, CMV) may mimic other diseases. Maintain a high index of suspicion.
- **Patients with spinal injury or on opioids/sedation:** Masking of pain.

### 14.11 Worked cases

#### Case 1: Appendicitis

*A 19-year-old man has had 18 hours of abdominal pain that began around the umbilicus and moved to the right lower abdomen, with anorexia and one episode of vomiting. Temperature 37.9 °C, pulse 98. He lies still and winces when the bed is bumped. The abdomen: tenderness maximal at McBurney's point, with guarding and percussion tenderness. Palpation of the left iliac fossa causes pain in the right (Rovsing sign).*

**Reasoning.** Classic migrating pain, anorexia, low-grade fever, localised peritonism and Rovsing sign = **acute appendicitis** (Alvarado score high).

**Next steps.** Analgesia (does not mask the signs), fluids, blood tests (WBC, CRP), urine dipstick, imaging if the diagnosis is uncertain (ultrasound in children and young women; CT in adults), surgical review and appendicectomy. Antibiotics in line with local protocols.

#### Case 2: Decompensated cirrhosis

*A 55-year-old man with a long history of alcohol excess has abdominal swelling and ankle oedema for 2 months. He is jaundiced, with leuconychia, palmar erythema, gynaecomastia, spider naevi (more than 10), a flapping tremor, and dilated veins radiating from the umbilicus. The abdomen is distended with shifting dullness and a positive fluid thrill; the spleen is palpable 4 cm below the left costal margin; the liver is small and not palpable.*

**Reasoning.** Multiple stigmata of chronic liver disease, ascites, splenomegaly and caput medusae indicate **cirrhosis with portal hypertension**. Asterixis suggests hepatic encephalopathy. The small liver is typical of advanced cirrhosis.

**Next steps.** Diagnostic paracentesis (cell count, albumin, culture) to exclude spontaneous bacterial peritonitis, particularly if he has fever, abdominal pain or encephalopathy; liver function tests, coagulation, renal function, sodium; ultrasound for hepatocellular carcinoma and portal vein patency; endoscopy for varices; precipitating factors for decompensation (infection, bleeding, alcohol, drugs).

#### Case 3: Small bowel obstruction

*A 72-year-old woman with a previous hysterectomy 20 years ago has had 36 hours of colicky central abdominal pain, repeated vomiting of bilious fluid, and no passage of stool or flatus for 24 hours. The abdomen is distended and moderately tender centrally; there is no guarding. Bowel sounds are loud and tinkling. Rectum is empty. There is a firm, tender, irreducible swelling below and lateral to the pubic tubercle.*

**Reasoning.** Colicky pain, vomiting, constipation and distension: **mechanical small bowel obstruction.** The cause is found on examining the hernial orifices: an **irreducible femoral hernia**, probably strangulated (tender, irreducible). Previous pelvic surgery also raises the possibility of adhesions, which is why hernial orifices must be examined in every patient with obstruction.

**Next steps.** IV fluids, nasogastric tube, analgesia, urgent surgical review, bloods (lactate, electrolytes), imaging (CT abdomen), and emergency surgical repair if strangulation.

#### Case 4: Ruptured abdominal aortic aneurysm

*A 71-year-old man, a smoker with hypertension, has sudden severe left loin and abdominal pain and collapses. His pulse is 130, BP 78/50, and he is pale and sweaty. A pulsatile, expansile, tender mass is felt above the umbilicus. Femoral pulses are palpable but weak.*

**Reasoning.** Sudden pain, shock and a pulsatile mass: **ruptured AAA** until proven otherwise. The presentation can mimic renal colic.

**Action.** This is a surgical emergency. Activate the major haemorrhage protocol, two large-bore IV lines, cross-match blood, **permissive hypotension** (target systolic ~70–90 mmHg, avoiding large crystalloid volumes), immediate vascular surgical team and theatre. If stable enough, CT angiography; if unstable, proceed directly to theatre (or bedside ultrasound). Do not delay for imaging.

#### Case 5: Acute pancreatitis

*A 44-year-old man develops severe epigastric pain radiating to the back after a heavy weekend of drinking, worse lying flat and eased by leaning forward, with repeated vomiting. Pulse 112, temperature 38.0 °C, BP 118/70. The abdomen: marked epigastric tenderness and guarding, reduced bowel sounds. No jaundice.*

**Reasoning.** Epigastric pain radiating to the back with vomiting and a history of alcohol = **acute pancreatitis**. Cullen and Grey Turner signs appear only in severe haemorrhagic pancreatitis and are late.

**Next steps.** Serum lipase (or amylase), liver function tests, calcium, glucose, urea, full blood count, CRP, ABG, ultrasound (gallstones), aggressive but careful fluid resuscitation, analgesia, monitoring for organ failure; severity scoring (Glasgow-Imrie, BISAP, APACHE II); consider causes (gallstones, alcohol, hypertriglyceridaemia, hypercalcaemia, drugs).

#### Case 6: Splenomegaly

*A 44-year-old woman has fatigue, early satiety, night sweats and a left upper abdominal "fullness." She has pallor and a firm, non-tender mass extending 10 cm below the left costal margin and toward the umbilicus, with a notch on its medial edge, which moves with respiration and is dull to percussion. There is no hepatomegaly or ascites. No lymphadenopathy.*

**Reasoning.** A **massive splenomegaly** with night sweats and anaemia: differential includes chronic myeloid leukaemia, myelofibrosis, lymphoma, or (depending on geography) malaria or visceral leishmaniasis. Portal hypertension is less likely without liver stigmata.

**Next steps.** Full blood count with film (leucocytosis with immature forms in CML; teardrop cells and leucoerythroblastic picture in myelofibrosis), LDH, ultrasound, bone marrow biopsy, cytogenetics (BCR-ABL), appropriate infectious tests, haematology referral.

### 14.12 Common pitfalls

- **Examining in a rush, standing, with cold hands,** or with the patient's arms behind the head.
- **Starting palpation at the area of pain.**
- **Missing the hernial orifices** in a patient with bowel obstruction.
- **Using rebound tenderness as the first test,** causing unnecessary pain.
- **Relying on bowel sounds** to rule out or diagnose peritonitis.
- **Assuming that a palpable liver edge is hepatomegaly,** or that an impalpable liver is normal in size (use percussion).
- **Missing a ballotable kidney, or a distended bladder,** as the cause of a "mass."
- **Forgetting rectal and pelvic examination when indicated.**
- **Failing to consider chest and cardiac causes** of abdominal pain.
- **Failing to do a pregnancy test** in a woman of reproductive age.
- **Falling for the "benign abdomen" in older patients** and the immunosuppressed.
- **Attributing the pain of a pulsatile mass to renal colic.**
- **Not repeating the examination** in a patient in whom the diagnosis is uncertain (serial examination, ideally by the same clinician, Chapter 5).

---

### 14.13 Key points

- Prepare the patient (position, exposure, privacy, chaperone), look before touching, and examine in the order **inspect, auscultate, percuss, palpate.**
- Light palpation first; start away from pain; watch the patient's face.
- Distinguish voluntary guarding, involuntary guarding and rigidity; rigidity signals generalised peritonitis and needs urgent surgical assessment.
- Palpable liver edge does not equal hepatomegaly; use percussion to estimate liver span. The spleen enlarges toward the right iliac fossa and has a notch.
- Shifting dullness detects ascites (about 1.5 L or more).
- Always examine the hernial orifices in bowel obstruction; consider femoral hernia in elderly women.
- Use digital rectal examination when indicated; explain, obtain consent and use a chaperone.
- Older, pregnant and immunosuppressed patients may have serious pathology with subtle signs; maintain a low threshold for imaging.
- Complete the picture with urine dipstick, pregnancy test, and examination of the chest, heart and groin.

### 14.14 Self-assessment

1. List the "5 Fs" of abdominal distension.
   *Answer: Fat, fluid, flatus, faeces, fetus (also fibroids/tumour).*

2. Describe how to elicit shifting dullness, and roughly what volume of ascites is detectable.
   *Answer: Percuss from the centre (resonant) to the flank until dull and mark the border; roll the patient to the opposite side, wait 30–60 s, and re-percuss: the dullness shifts to the dependent side. About 1.5 L or more.*

3. Distinguish a spleen from a left kidney on palpation.
   *Answer: The spleen has a notch, enlarges toward the right iliac fossa, cannot be "got above," is dull to percussion and is not ballotable; the kidney is ballotable, has a resonant note (bowel in front) and you can get above it.*

4. What is Courvoisier's law?
   *Answer: A palpable gallbladder in the presence of painless jaundice is unlikely to be due to gallstones (usually malignant obstruction of the bile duct, e.g. pancreatic head carcinoma).*

5. Why should the hernial orifices be examined in every patient with small bowel obstruction?
   *Answer: A strangulated or incarcerated hernia is a common and easily missed cause of obstruction, and femoral hernias in elderly women are particularly easy to overlook.*

6. Name three conditions in which older patients may present with minimal abdominal signs despite serious pathology.
   *Answer (any three): appendicitis, perforated ulcer or diverticulum, cholecystitis, mesenteric ischaemia, ruptured aneurysm, bowel obstruction.*
