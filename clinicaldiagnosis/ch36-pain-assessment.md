## Chapter 36. Pain Assessment Across Ages and Cultures

### Learning objectives

After studying this chapter you should be able to:

1. Define pain, distinguish nociceptive, neuropathic and nociplastic mechanisms, and describe the biopsychosocial and "total pain" models.
2. Take a structured pain history, examine for neuropathic features, and recognise red flags and psychosocial risk factors for chronicity.
3. Choose and use validated pain assessment tools for adults, children, neonates, people with cognitive impairment, and critically ill patients.
4. Explain how age, culture, language, gender, ethnicity, disability and substance use history shape the expression of pain and clinicians' responses, and apply strategies to reduce disparities.
5. Apply the principles of safe, multimodal analgesia for acute pain, and identify the risks of opioids.
6. Approach chronic pain with a biopsychosocial plan focused on function, and understand the limited role of long-term opioids.
7. Manage pain in special situations: sickle cell crisis, renal colic, cancer pain, procedural pain, opioid-tolerant patients, and end of life.

---

### 36.1 Why pain assessment matters

Pain is the **most common reason** people seek medical help, the **commonest symptom** in many presentations covered in this part (chest pain, abdominal pain, headache, back and joint pain), and a central source of suffering and disability worldwide. It is also **systematically under-treated** in some groups (older adults, children, people with dementia, women, racial and ethnic minorities, people with substance use disorders, those who do not share the clinician's language) and **over-treated or harmfully treated** in others (long-term opioids for chronic non-cancer pain).

Pain assessment has two goals:

1. **Diagnostic:** pain is a clue to the cause (Chapters 22, 24, 25, 29). Its site, character, time course and modifiers help localise disease.
2. **Therapeutic:** understanding the pain's intensity, impact, meaning, and mechanisms guides treatment and monitors response.

A fundamental principle: **pain is whatever the person says it is.** It is a **subjective experience;** self-report is the gold standard, and the inability to communicate does not negate the possibility of pain.

### 36.2 Definitions and mechanisms

#### Definition (IASP, 2020)

> *"An unpleasant sensory and emotional experience associated with, or resembling that associated with, actual or potential tissue damage."*

Key points from the revised definition: pain is **always a personal experience** influenced to varying degrees by biological, psychological and social factors; pain and **nociception** are different phenomena (nociception is neural processing of noxious stimuli; pain cannot be inferred solely from activity in sensory neurons); a person's report of an experience as pain should be respected; verbal description is only one of several behaviours that express pain, and **inability to communicate does not negate** its possibility in humans and animals.

#### Types by mechanism

| Type | Mechanism | Clinical features | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Nociceptive (somatic)** | Activation of nociceptors in skin, muscle, bone, joint | Well-localised, aching, throbbing, sharp, worse with movement | Fracture, arthritis, post-surgical pain, musculoskeletal injury |
| **Nociceptive (visceral)** | Stretch, ischaemia or inflammation of hollow or solid organs | Dull, cramping, poorly localised, referred, with autonomic features (nausea, sweating) | Biliary colic, bowel obstruction, angina, pancreatitis |
| **Neuropathic** | Lesion or disease of the somatosensory nervous system | **Burning, shooting, electric-shock, stabbing, tingling, numbness;** allodynia (pain from non-painful stimuli), hyperalgesia, sensory loss in a neuroanatomical distribution | Diabetic neuropathy, post-herpetic neuralgia, trigeminal neuralgia, radiculopathy, post-stroke pain, chemotherapy-induced neuropathy, carpal tunnel syndrome |
| **Nociplastic (central sensitisation)** | Altered nociception without clear evidence of tissue damage or nerve lesion; amplified central processing | Widespread, multifocal pain, fatigue, sleep disturbance, cognitive difficulties, hypersensitivity to stimuli | Fibromyalgia, chronic widespread pain, some chronic low back pain, IBS, chronic primary headache |
| **Mixed** | | Many chronic pains | Chronic low back pain with radiculopathy; cancer pain |

#### Time course

- **Acute pain** (usually <3 months, serves a protective function; resolves with healing).
- **Chronic pain:** persistent or recurrent pain lasting or recurring for **>3 months** (ICD-11). **Chronic primary pain** (the pain is the problem: fibromyalgia, complex regional pain syndrome, chronic primary low back pain, chronic primary headache, IBS) vs **chronic secondary pain** (the pain is a symptom of an underlying condition: cancer pain, post-surgical, post-traumatic, neuropathic, visceral, musculoskeletal).
- **Breakthrough pain:** transient exacerbations on a background of controlled pain.
- **Incident pain:** pain precipitated by movement or specific activity.

#### The biopsychosocial model and "total pain"

- **Biological factors:** tissue injury, inflammation, nerve damage, genetics, sensitisation of peripheral and central pain pathways.
- **Psychological factors:** mood (depression, anxiety), catastrophising, fear-avoidance, beliefs about pain, previous experiences, post-traumatic stress.
- **Social factors:** work, finances, relationships, social support, cultural expectations, compensation, healthcare experiences.
- **Cicely Saunders' "total pain":** physical, psychological, social and spiritual dimensions are interwoven, particularly in advanced illness. Treat all dimensions.
- **Gate control and central modulation:** descending inhibitory and facilitatory pathways modulate pain; attention, emotion, expectation, sleep, and context influence the experience (placebo and nocebo effects are real physiological phenomena).

### 36.3 The pain history

Use SOCRATES (Chapter 6) and extend it with measures of impact. For acute pain the main goals are **location, mechanism, severity, red flags;** for chronic pain, **function, mood, sleep, beliefs, coping, previous treatments.**

#### Core elements

| Domain | Questions |
|---|---|
| **Site and radiation** | "Where is it? Point to it. Does it spread?" (Body diagram) |
| **Onset and time course** | Sudden or gradual; constant, intermittent, episodic; daily pattern; duration |
| **Character** | Aching, sharp, throbbing, burning, tearing, stabbing, electric, cramping, gnawing, numb, tingling |
| **Intensity** | 0–10 numeric scale: now, average, worst, least in the last 24 hours or week |
| **Aggravating and relieving factors** | Movement, rest, position, food, bowel/bladder, cough, heat/cold, touch, stress, medications |
| **Associated symptoms** | Fever, weight loss, neurological symptoms, nausea, autonomic features, mood, sleep |
| **Impact on function** | Walking, work, sleep, self-care, relationships, leisure, mood. "What can't you do now that you used to?" |
| **Previous treatments** | Drugs (dose, duration, effect, side effects), physiotherapy, injections, surgery, complementary therapies |
| **Patient's understanding and concerns** | "What do you think is causing the pain? What are you most worried about?" (ICE: ideas, concerns, expectations) |
| **Goals** | "What would you like to be able to do?" |
| **Background** | Past medical history, mental health, trauma, **substance use history,** family history, social circumstances, occupational factors, litigation or compensation |

#### Red flags

As for the relevant regional pain (e.g. cauda equina, malignancy, infection, vascular emergencies; Chapters 22–29). **New pain, pain of different character, or pain with systemic or neurological features** should prompt diagnostic evaluation before attributing it to a chronic condition. In patients with chronic pain, **never assume a new pain is "just the usual."**

#### Neuropathic pain screening

Clues: burning, shooting, electric shocks, pins and needles, numbness in the same area, allodynia, hyperalgesia, **plus** a plausible lesion or disease (diabetes, shingles, nerve injury, radiculopathy, stroke, chemotherapy). Screening tools: **DN4, painDETECT, LANSS, NPSI.**

**DN4 (Douleur Neuropathique 4):** seven symptom items (burning, painful cold, electric shocks, tingling, pins and needles, numbness, itching) and three examination items (hypoaesthesia to touch, hypoaesthesia to pinprick, brushing-induced pain); score ≥4/10 suggests neuropathic pain.

#### Psychosocial risk factors ("yellow flags") for chronicity

Catastrophising, fear-avoidance beliefs, depression or anxiety, low expectation of recovery, passive coping, high initial pain intensity, poor work satisfaction, litigation, social isolation, previous trauma or abuse, poor sleep. Tools: **STarT Back Tool, Örebro Musculoskeletal Pain Screening Questionnaire, Pain Catastrophizing Scale, PHQ-9, GAD-7.**

#### Examination

- **General and vital signs** (pain is not a substitute for vital signs: tachycardia, hypotension, fever).
- **Observation:** posture, guarding, facial expression, movement, gait, grimacing, behaviours (rubbing, splinting).
- **Examination of the painful area:** inspection (swelling, colour, deformity), palpation (tenderness, trigger points, masses), movement (active and passive range, resisted), special tests, neurological examination (strength, sensation, reflexes), vascular examination.
- **Sensory testing for neuropathic pain:** light touch (cotton wool), pinprick, temperature, vibration, **allodynia** (brush), **hyperalgesia,** summation, signs of autonomic change (oedema, colour, temperature, sweating, trophic changes: CRPS).
- **Abdominal pain:** signs of peritonism (Chapter 25); **chest pain:** cardiac and respiratory examination; do not delay analgesia for examination in severe acute pain.
- **Mental state and cognition.**

### 36.4 Measuring pain: assessment tools

No single tool fits all patients. The choice depends on **age, cognition, language, setting, and purpose.** The best measure for adults who can self-report is the patient's own rating.

#### Self-report scales (adults and older children who can communicate)

| Tool | Description | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Numeric Rating Scale (NRS 0–10)** | 0 = no pain, 10 = worst pain imaginable (verbal or written) | Most widely used, quick, valid; typical categories: 1–3 mild, 4–6 moderate, 7–10 severe (but individual) |
| **Visual Analogue Scale (VAS)** | 10-cm line | Needs visual and motor ability; less practical in acute settings |
| **Verbal Rating Scale (VRS)** | None, mild, moderate, severe, very severe | Useful in older adults and those who struggle with numbers |
| **Faces Pain Scale–Revised (FPS-R); Wong–Baker FACES** | Pictorial faces | **Children from about 4–5 years,** people with language or cognitive barriers; FPS-R avoids smiling/crying cues; cultural variations in interpreting faces |
| **Colour analogue, thermometer scales** | | Alternatives |

#### Multidimensional tools

| Tool | Measures |
|---|---|
| **Brief Pain Inventory (BPI)** | Pain severity (worst, least, average, now) and interference with function (activity, mood, walking, work, relations, sleep, enjoyment of life); widely used in cancer and chronic pain |
| **McGill Pain Questionnaire (short form)** | Sensory and affective descriptors |
| **PEG scale (3 items)** | Pain average, interference with Enjoyment of life, interference with General activity; useful in primary care to monitor chronic pain |
| **Pain Disability Index, Oswestry Disability Index, Roland-Morris** | Function in back pain |
| **PROMIS pain interference** | |
| **Pain Catastrophizing Scale, Fear-Avoidance Beliefs Questionnaire, PHQ-9, GAD-7, Insomnia Severity Index** | Psychosocial |

#### Observational (behavioural) tools: when self-report is not possible

| Population | Tools | What they observe |
|---|---|---|
| **Neonates** | **PIPP-R** (Premature Infant Pain Profile), **NIPS** (Neonatal Infant Pain Scale), **CRIES** (Crying, Requires O₂, Increased vital signs, Expression, Sleeplessness), **N-PASS** | Facial expression, cry, breathing, limb movement, arousal state, physiological signs (heart rate, SpO₂) |
| **Infants and young children (<4–5 years), non-verbal children** | **FLACC** (Face, Legs, Activity, Cry, Consolability; each 0–2, total 0–10), **COMFORT-B**, **r-FLACC** (children with cognitive impairment), **Oucher, CHEOPS** | Behaviours |
| **Adults and older adults with dementia or cognitive impairment** | **PAINAD** (Pain Assessment in Advanced Dementia: breathing, negative vocalisation, facial expression, body language, consolability; 0–10), **Abbey Pain Scale, DOLOPLUS-2, PACSLAC, MOBID-2** | Facial expression, vocalisation, body movements, changes in behaviour or activity (agitation, withdrawal, aggression, sleep, appetite) |
| **Critically ill, sedated or ventilated adults** | **Critical-Care Pain Observation Tool (CPOT),** **Behavioral Pain Scale (BPS)** | Facial expression, body movements, muscle tension, ventilator compliance or vocalisation |
| **Patients with intellectual disability, communication difficulties** | **NCCPC-R** (Non-communicating Children's Pain Checklist), **PAIC** | Carer observation and baseline behaviour |

**Practical principles:**

- **Ask first; trust self-report** when possible; if the person cannot self-report, use the best available observational tool and rely on family and carers' knowledge of baseline behaviour.
- **Look for behavioural changes in dementia:** new agitation, calling out, resistance to care, aggression, withdrawal, reduced appetite, sleep disturbance, grimacing, guarding, reduced mobility: pain is often **undetected** and may be treated with antipsychotics instead of analgesics. **Trial of regular analgesia** (e.g. paracetamol) in agitated patients with dementia is justified.
- **Use the same tool consistently** for repeated assessment, and **reassess after interventions** (typically 30–60 minutes after oral analgesia; 5–15 minutes after IV opioids).
- **Document** the score, the tool, the character and location, response to treatment, side effects, functional effects.
- **Avoid using pain scores alone to trigger prescribing.** "Pain as the fifth vital sign" campaigns, with mandated numerical targets, contributed to over-prescribing of opioids in some countries; the score must be interpreted with the clinical context and functional goals. A patient who reports 8/10 pain but is chatting and laughing, or 3/10 but is pale, sweating and tachycardic, requires judgement.

### 36.5 Pain across the lifespan

#### Neonates and infants

- **Neonates feel pain** (nociceptive pathways functional from the third trimester), and unrelieved pain has short- and long-term effects (altered pain responses, neurodevelopmental effects).
- **Assessment:** validated neonatal tools (PIPP-R, N-PASS, NIPS), considering gestational age, behavioural state, and physiological changes.
- **Procedural pain** (heel prick, venepuncture, cannulation, lumbar puncture, circumcision, ventilation): use **non-pharmacological measures** (breastfeeding, skin-to-skin contact, facilitated tucking, swaddling, non-nutritive sucking), **oral sucrose** (24%, 0.1–2 mL depending on weight and gestational age, 2 minutes before the procedure), **topical anaesthetics** (lidocaine-prilocaine, tetracaine) where appropriate, and **systemic analgesia** (paracetamol, opioids) for major procedures and surgery, with regional anaesthesia where possible.

#### Children

- **Developmental factors:** toddlers express pain by crying, withdrawal and behavioural change; preschoolers may deny pain (fear of injections) or interpret pain as punishment; school-age children can use numeric or faces scales; adolescents may under-report in front of peers.
- **Self-report from about age 4–5** (faces scales), numeric scales from about 7–8; always combine with behavioural observation, parental input, and physiological signs.
- **Principles:** prevent and treat pain proactively; **use age-appropriate explanation,** involve parents, **distraction** (toys, bubbles, music, tablets, virtual reality, storytelling), **topical anaesthesia before needles,** analgesic ladder (paracetamol, ibuprofen, then opioids), **avoid codeine and tramadol in children** (variable CYP2D6 metabolism: risk of respiratory depression; contraindicated after tonsillectomy/adenoidectomy in children <18 years in many countries), **weight-based dosing,** avoid aspirin in children (Reye syndrome), regional anaesthesia; manage **procedural anxiety and needle fear.**
- **Chronic pain in children and adolescents:** headache, abdominal pain, musculoskeletal pain, CRPS, fibromyalgia: school attendance and function are central; multidisciplinary management with CBT, physiotherapy, family involvement.
- **Do not dismiss pain in children with developmental disability;** use carer-based tools.

#### Adults

- Use the full assessment above; address sex-related and gender-related differences in clinical response (women have higher rates of many chronic pains; hormonal influences; analgesic response differences) while avoiding stereotyping.

#### Pregnancy and breastfeeding

- **Paracetamol** is the first-line analgesic; **NSAIDs** should be avoided in the third trimester (premature closure of the ductus arteriosus, oligohydramnios) and used with caution earlier; **opioids** (short courses) may cause neonatal withdrawal if used long term; **codeine** should be avoided in breastfeeding mothers with ultra-rapid CYP2D6 metabolism; non-pharmacological therapies (physiotherapy, TENS, heat, exercise). Evaluate pain red flags that may signify obstetric or other emergencies (Chapter 25).

#### Older adults

- **Pain is common (up to half of community-dwelling older adults) and under-recognised and undertreated.** Reasons: belief that pain is normal ageing, stoicism, fear of being a burden or of drugs, communication barriers (hearing, speech, cognition), atypical presentations (confusion, falls, reduced function or appetite, depression, agitation), clinicians' fears of adverse effects, polypharmacy.
- **Assessment:** ask directly using words other than "pain" ("aching," "soreness," "hurting," "discomfort"); verbal descriptor or faces scales rather than numeric scales; **functional measures** (walking, transfers); observational tools in dementia; screen for depression, sleep disturbance, social isolation; **ask carers.**
- **Treatment:** "start low, go slow, but go" (do not under-dose); **regular paracetamol is first-line;** topical NSAIDs for localised pain; **avoid or minimise systemic NSAIDs** (GI bleeding, renal, cardiovascular, hypertension, heart failure; with PPI if essential); opioids with care (renal and hepatic dosing, constipation prophylaxis, falls and sedation risk, avoid **pethidine** and with caution **tramadol** (serotonin syndrome, seizures, hyponatraemia, hypoglycaemia)), **gabapentinoids** carry sedation, falls, respiratory depression, and confusion risk (reduce doses for renal function), avoid long-term benzodiazepines and muscle relaxants; non-pharmacological: exercise, physiotherapy, heat, TENS, CBT, social engagement.
- **Delirium:** untreated pain is a precipitant, but opioids and anticholinergics may also provoke it (Chapter 33).

#### Palliative and end of life

- Pain is feared and common; aim for **comfort,** dignity, and function as appropriate; assess **total pain;** use the WHO approach, **titrate opioids to effect,** treat breakthrough, manage adverse effects (laxatives, antiemetics), involve palliative care early; in renal failure prefer **fentanyl, hydromorphone, oxycodone, methadone (with specialist input)** over morphine (accumulation of morphine-6-glucuronide); subcutaneous routes when oral is not possible; treat neuropathic components with adjuvants; non-drug measures (radiotherapy for bone metastases, nerve blocks); pain at the end of life should never be left untreated for fear of "hastening death" (the doctrine of double effect).

### 36.6 Culture, language, gender, race and bias

#### How culture and experience shape pain

- **Expression and communication of pain** vary: some cultures value stoicism and the avoidance of complaint (leading to under-reporting), others permit open expression (leading to misinterpretation as exaggeration by clinicians from a stoic culture). Words for pain, body regions, and emotions differ across languages and may not map directly to English descriptors.
- **Beliefs about causes and meaning of pain:** punishment, spiritual test, fate, bodily imbalance, stress, evil influence; **fear of addiction, side effects of medicines, or of being labelled** can lead patients to decline analgesia. Explore and respect these beliefs (Kleinman's questions, Chapter 8).
- **Family and social context:** who speaks for the patient, decision-making, gender norms about discussing pain, modesty and examination.
- **Religious and spiritual practices** (prayer, fasting, rituals) that may be sources of coping.
- **Use of traditional and complementary therapies,** often concurrently.
- **Avoid assumptions:** culture is not destiny; individuals differ; ask each patient.

#### Language barriers

- Use **professional interpreters** (Chapter 8) and ask the interpreter about the connotation of pain words; use pictorial tools (faces scales, body diagrams) and **translated, validated versions** of questionnaires; avoid relatives as interpreters (they may filter or minimise); be aware that pain behaviours (grimacing, moaning) can differ across cultures.
- Patients with limited English proficiency are **less likely to receive adequate analgesia** in some studies; document language barriers and use structured protocols.

#### Documented disparities and bias

Research, particularly in the United States and other multiethnic societies, shows consistent disparities in pain assessment and treatment:

- **Race and ethnicity:** Black, Hispanic and some other minority patients are **less likely to receive analgesia** in emergency departments for the same conditions (fractures, abdominal pain, back pain, appendicitis in children), and receive lower doses and delayed treatment. Contributing factors include **false beliefs about biological differences** (such as "Black people have thicker skin or less sensitive nerve endings"), implicit bias, mistrust, communication differences, and concern about opioid misuse. Such misbeliefs have been documented in a substantial proportion of medical students and residents.
- **Gender:** women's pain is more often **attributed to emotional or psychological causes,** with longer waits for analgesia in abdominal pain and cardiac conditions, and under-diagnosis of conditions such as endometriosis and cardiac disease; men are sometimes assumed to be stoic.
- **Age:** older adults and young children are under-treated.
- **Socioeconomic status, disability, obesity, immigration status, stigmatised diagnoses** (sickle cell disease, substance use, mental illness) are associated with scepticism and under-treatment.
- **Labels such as "drug-seeking," "frequent flyer," "malingerer," "difficult patient," "non-compliant"** distort assessment (Chapters 2 and 9) and should not be used as substitutes for an assessment.

**Strategies to reduce disparities:**

1. **Believe the patient's report** and use standardised assessment protocols and analgesic pathways (triage-initiated analgesia, order sets, time-to-analgesia targets).
2. **Use objective documentation** of pain assessment and response.
3. **Reflect on your own assumptions;** ask "Would I respond the same if this patient were different?"
4. **Adopt individualised care plans** for patients with recurrent pain (sickle cell disease: analgesia within 30–60 minutes; cancer pain; chronic pain flares) developed with the patient and specialists.
5. **Teach and learn about unconscious bias,** and about the history of unequal treatment.
6. **Provide interpreters and culturally appropriate information.**
7. **Audit** analgesia times and doses by patient group.
8. **Combine compassion with safe practice** (risk assessment, naloxone, monitoring) rather than withholding analgesia.

### 36.7 Principles of treatment (brief)

This section outlines general principles; full treatment depends on the condition and local protocols.

#### Acute pain

1. **Treat the cause** (fracture immobilisation, drainage, surgery, antibiotics) **and the pain simultaneously.**
2. **Multimodal analgesia:** combine agents with different mechanisms to reduce opioid dose: **paracetamol, NSAIDs** (if not contraindicated), **local or regional anaesthesia** (nerve blocks, epidurals, local infiltration), **opioids** (titrated), and adjuncts (ketamine at low dose, dexamethasone, alpha-2 agonists, gabapentinoids in selected settings), plus **non-pharmacological measures** (immobilisation, ice or heat, elevation, positioning, distraction, TENS).
3. **WHO analgesic ladder** (originally for cancer pain): step 1 non-opioid (± adjuvant); step 2 weak opioid (± non-opioid); step 3 strong opioid (± non-opioid). In acute severe pain, start at the level matched to intensity (e.g. IV opioid) and step down; the ladder is not rigidly sequential.
4. **Choose the route:** oral when possible; **IV titration** for severe acute pain (e.g. morphine 2–5 mg increments every 5 minutes in adults until relief, with reassessment of sedation and respiratory rate); intranasal (fentanyl, diamorphine, ketamine) or IM/SC when IV access is unavailable; **patient-controlled analgesia (PCA)** after major surgery; intranasal or inhaled analgesia (methoxyflurane, nitrous oxide) for short procedures.
5. **Dose according to age, weight, renal and hepatic function,** comorbidity (sleep apnoea, COPD), concurrent sedatives, opioid tolerance.
6. **Monitor:** pain score, **sedation score** (e.g. POSS, RASS), respiratory rate and SpO₂, blood pressure, nausea, pruritus, urinary retention, delirium. **Naloxone** for opioid-induced respiratory depression.
7. **Prevent and treat adverse effects:** **laxatives** with all regular opioids (stimulant plus osmotic), antiemetics, antipruritics.
8. **Reassess regularly,** step down quickly, and **plan analgesia for discharge** (short supply, clear instructions, avoid long-term opioid prescriptions after minor procedures, naloxone if risk).
9. **Special agents:** avoid **pethidine** (neurotoxic metabolite norpethidine, serotonin syndrome); **codeine** has unpredictable efficacy (CYP2D6) and should be avoided in children, in breastfeeding, and in ultra-rapid metabolisers; **tramadol** has seizure and serotonin risks; **NSAIDs:** risk of GI bleeding, acute kidney injury, cardiovascular events, asthma exacerbations, and are contraindicated in many conditions (e.g. renal impairment, heart failure, peptic ulcer, third-trimester pregnancy); **paracetamol** hepatotoxicity in overdose and in malnourished or alcohol-misusing patients (dose adjustment).

#### Neuropathic pain

- **First-line options:** **gabapentin or pregabalin; amitriptyline (or nortriptyline); duloxetine (SNRI);** carbamazepine or oxcarbazepine for trigeminal neuralgia; **topical lidocaine** (localised neuropathic pain) or **capsaicin 8% patch;** treat the cause (glycaemic control, decompression). Combination therapy where partial response; start low and titrate; counsel about sedation and dizziness. **Opioids and tramadol** are second- or third-line; avoid paracetamol and NSAIDs as single agents (poorly effective).
- **Interventions:** nerve blocks, spinal cord stimulation, peripheral nerve stimulation.

#### Chronic non-cancer pain

- **Goals: function, quality of life, and self-management,** not complete abolition of pain.
- **Core elements:**
  1. **Validation and explanation** (education on pain mechanisms: pain neuroscience education), set realistic expectations, develop a shared plan.
  2. **Physical activity and exercise** (graded, pacing; physiotherapy), weight management, **sleep hygiene,** healthy lifestyle.
  3. **Psychological therapies:** **CBT, acceptance and commitment therapy (ACT), mindfulness-based approaches,** pain coping skills training; treat depression, anxiety, trauma.
  4. **Medication,** limited and purposeful: paracetamol, topical NSAIDs, oral NSAIDs for short periods in inflammatory pain, **SNRI/TCA/gabapentinoid** for neuropathic or nociplastic pain (modest effect sizes), **avoid long-term opioids** in most chronic non-cancer pain.
  5. **Interventional procedures** (epidural steroid injections, facet joint or medial branch blocks, radiofrequency, neuromodulation) in selected patients with clear indication, as part of a multimodal programme.
  6. **Multidisciplinary pain programmes** for complex cases.
  7. **Social and occupational support** (return to work, benefits, relationships).
  8. **Review regularly,** stop ineffective treatments (de-prescribing).
- **Opioids in chronic non-cancer pain:** evidence of long-term benefit is limited; harms include **tolerance, physical dependence, opioid-induced hyperalgesia, constipation, sedation, falls, hormonal changes, sleep-disordered breathing, opioid use disorder, and overdose** (risk rises with dose, particularly >50 oral morphine milligram equivalents/day, and with benzodiazepines, alcohol, or gabapentinoids). **If used:** after non-opioid options, at the lowest effective dose for the shortest period, with a **clear goal and review,** **agreement and monitoring,** screening for risk (including mental health and substance use history), naloxone co-prescription, and **a plan to taper** if there is no meaningful improvement in function. **Do not stop long-term opioids abruptly** (withdrawal, increased pain, risk of suicide and overdose); taper slowly and involve the patient.
- **Opioid use disorder:** distinguish from physical dependence (expected) and tolerance; **treat with buprenorphine or methadone (opioid agonist therapy),** which are not a reason to deny analgesia for acute pain.

#### Procedural pain

- Anticipate and prevent: topical anaesthesia (EMLA, LMX4), local infiltration (buffered lidocaine), nerve blocks, nitrous oxide, procedural sedation (per local protocol and monitoring standards), distraction techniques, parental presence, clear explanation and **a plan to stop** if the patient cannot cope.

#### Pain in special clinical scenarios

| Scenario | Principles |
|---|---|
| **Sickle cell vaso-occlusive crisis** | Rapid assessment and **analgesia within 30–60 minutes of arrival,** IV opioids titrated (individualised care plans, patient's previous effective doses), PCA, NSAIDs/paracetamol adjuncts, hydration, oxygen if hypoxic, incentive spirometry; do not label patients as "drug-seeking;" watch for acute chest syndrome, infection, and other complications |
| **Renal colic** | **NSAID** (diclofenac IM/PR, ketorolac, ibuprofen) is first-line (as effective as opioids with fewer adverse effects) unless contraindicated; add opioids if needed; imaging (low-dose non-contrast CT or ultrasound); alpha-blockers for stones 5–10 mm remain controversial |
| **Cancer pain** | **Titrate opioids to effect** (oral morphine, oxycodone, hydromorphone, fentanyl patch for stable pain, methadone by specialists), **immediate-release for breakthrough pain (about 1/6th of the total daily dose),** treat neuropathic pain with adjuvants, **radiotherapy for bone metastases,** bisphosphonates/denosumab, nerve blocks and intrathecal therapy for refractory pain, treat constipation; address psychological and spiritual distress; palliative care involvement |
| **Opioid-tolerant patient (including those on methadone or buprenorphine) with acute pain** | **Continue the maintenance dose** (to prevent withdrawal); add multimodal analgesia, regional techniques, and **higher-than-usual doses of short-acting opioids** as needed (tolerance), monitoring for sedation; divided dosing of methadone or buprenorphine can provide analgesic effect; avoid undertreatment and avoid stigma; use specialist pain or addiction services |
| **Post-operative pain** | Pre-emptive multimodal plan (regional blocks, paracetamol, NSAIDs, gabapentinoids selectively, dexamethasone), early mobilisation; enhanced recovery pathways; opioid-sparing protocols; limited discharge opioid supplies |
| **Labour pain** | Non-pharmacological support, nitrous oxide, opioids (pethidine, remifentanil PCA), **epidural analgesia** (most effective), patient preference |
| **ICU pain and sedation** | Analgesia-first sedation (opioids, multimodal), CPOT/BPS monitoring, avoid deep sedation, delirium prevention |
| **Dementia** | **Regular scheduled paracetamol,** observational tools, analgesic trial for behavioural change, non-drug care; avoid inappropriate use of antipsychotics |
| **Intellectual disability / non-verbal patients** | Carer input, baseline behaviours, observational tools, low threshold to treat |
| **Chest pain, abdominal pain, headache etc.** | Treat pain alongside diagnostic work-up (Chapters 22, 24, 25) |

### 36.8 Documentation and communication

- Record the pain assessment (site, intensity, character, tool used), the plan, and response; reassess after interventions.
- **Communicate expectations:** pain relief may be partial, and function is a goal; explain the plan and risks; discuss concerns about addiction and side effects; avoid promising a "cure" in chronic pain.
- **Avoid stigmatising language** in the record ("drug seeker," "complains of"): use neutral descriptions ("reports," "describes," "requests") and objective observations.
- **Shared decision-making** for opioid prescribing, tapering and procedures; provide written information; include family where appropriate.
- **Safety:** assess opioid risk, offer naloxone, safe storage and disposal.

### 36.9 Worked cases

#### Case 1: Pain in dementia

*An 84-year-old woman with advanced dementia, living in a care home, has become agitated, resistant to care, and calling out when moved, over the past week, and is eating less. She has been given increasing doses of an antipsychotic. She has no verbal reports of pain. PAINAD score: 7 (grimacing, moaning, guarding on transfer).*

**Reasoning.** Behavioural change in a non-verbal patient with dementia is **pain until proven otherwise;** the antipsychotic is inappropriate first-line. Examine for causes (fracture, pressure injury, constipation, urinary retention, dental pain, arthritis).

**Action.** Examine carefully (hip movement, pressure areas, mouth, abdomen, rectal), regular paracetamol (e.g. 1 g every 6–8 hours, adjusted for weight), consider low-dose opioid or topical agents if needed, treat causes, stop or reduce antipsychotic, repeat PAINAD scoring, involve family and carers.

#### Case 2: Neonatal procedural pain

*A 3-day-old term infant needs a heel-prick blood test for newborn screening and a bilirubin measurement.*

**Reasoning.** Neonates need analgesia for procedures; **sucrose, breastfeeding and skin-to-skin contact** are effective.

**Action.** Breastfeed or skin-to-skin during the procedure, 24% sucrose (e.g. 0.5–1 mL) on the tongue 2 minutes before, use an automated lancet, avoid repeated squeezing, document; assess with a neonatal scale (e.g. NIPS) as appropriate.

#### Case 3: Sickle cell crisis and bias

*A 24-year-old Black man with sickle cell disease attends the emergency department with severe leg and back pain (10/10) after an infection. The triage nurse notes "known frequent attender." He waits 3 hours before analgesia, then receives a small dose of oral codeine. He is distressed and grimacing.*

**Reasoning.** The delay and inadequate analgesia reflect bias and inadequate pathways. **Vaso-occlusive crisis** needs rapid, effective treatment.

**Action.** **Assess and give IV opioid analgesia within 30–60 minutes** (using his individualised care plan, if available: previous effective dose), reassess every 15–30 minutes, hydrate, treat triggers, check for acute chest syndrome, infection, anaemia; use PCA if needed; involve haematology; reflect on and audit time-to-analgesia.

#### Case 4: Language barrier

*A 58-year-old woman who speaks little English presents with abdominal pain. The triage nurse records "pain 2/10" after asking her to point to numbers on a scale. She is pale, sweating, holding her abdomen, tachycardic, with a rigid abdomen.*

**Reasoning.** The numerical rating was misinterpreted; **behaviour and signs indicate severe pain and a possible peritonitic abdomen.** Use a professional interpreter.

**Action.** Interpreter (telephone or video), faces scale and body diagram; analgesia now; examine and investigate for acute abdomen (Chapter 25); document the use of an interpreter.

#### Case 5: Diabetic neuropathic pain

*A 62-year-old man with type 2 diabetes has 8 months of burning and electric-shock pain in both feet, worse at night, with loss of sensation to pinprick in a stocking distribution and allodynia from bed sheets. DN4 = 7/10. Paracetamol and ibuprofen give no relief.*

**Reasoning.** **Painful diabetic peripheral neuropathy.**

**Action.** Optimise glycaemic control and foot care; start **duloxetine** (60 mg), or **pregabalin** (starting low and titrating, adjusted for renal function), or amitriptyline (caution in older adults and cardiac disease); topical capsaicin or lidocaine as adjunct; avoid opioids; review at 4–6 weeks for effect and side effects; address sleep and mood.

#### Case 6: Chronic low back pain with high psychosocial risk

*A 45-year-old man has had low back pain for 14 months, with normal MRI apart from mild degenerative changes. He has stopped working, avoids all activity for fear of "damaging his back," scores high on catastrophising and PHQ-9 (16), and sleeps 4 hours. He is on daily codeine.*

**Reasoning.** **Chronic non-specific low back pain with fear-avoidance, depression and sleep disturbance;** opioids unlikely to help.

**Action.** Explain the benign nature and the role of movement, graded activity with physiotherapy, CBT/ACT, treat depression (e.g. SNRI such as duloxetine, which also helps pain) and sleep, plan codeine taper, set functional goals, occupational support, multidisciplinary pain programme if no progress.

#### Case 7: Complex regional pain syndrome

*A 38-year-old woman, 6 weeks after a wrist fracture treated with a cast, has severe burning pain disproportionate to the injury, allodynia, swelling, colour and temperature changes, sweating differences, and stiffness of the hand.*

**Reasoning.** Features of **CRPS type 1** (Budapest criteria: continuing pain disproportionate to the inciting event, with sensory, vasomotor, sudomotor/oedema, and motor/trophic symptoms and signs).

**Action.** Early multidisciplinary approach: physiotherapy and graded motor imagery, desensitisation, occupational therapy, analgesia (gabapentinoid or amitriptyline), a short course of oral corticosteroids or bisphosphonate in selected cases, psychological support; avoid immobilisation.

#### Case 8: Cancer pain with breakthrough

*A 66-year-old man with prostate cancer and bone metastases has severe low back and hip pain, 7/10, worsening with movement. He takes modified-release morphine 60 mg twice daily (120 mg/day) with episodes of severe pain on moving or in the evening.*

**Reasoning.** **Background pain** controlled partially; **incident and breakthrough pain** require a rescue dose.

**Action.** Immediate-release morphine about 20 mg (one-sixth of the total daily dose) as needed (with review of use: if >3 doses per day, increase the background dose), laxatives, antiemetic as needed; **radiotherapy for painful bone metastases,** bisphosphonate or denosumab, dexamethasone short course for flare, consider neuropathic adjuvant; palliative care review; screen for cord compression (Chapter 29).

#### Case 9: Buprenorphine and acute fracture

*A 41-year-old man with opioid use disorder, stable on buprenorphine-naloxone 16 mg daily for 3 years, sustains a femur fracture.*

**Reasoning.** An **opioid-tolerant** patient on partial agonist therapy needs effective analgesia; stopping buprenorphine is unnecessary and risks relapse.

**Action.** **Continue buprenorphine** (consider splitting the dose into 3–4 times daily for analgesic effect), add multimodal analgesia (paracetamol, NSAID, regional block (e.g. fascia iliaca block), ketamine low dose), add short-acting full-agonist opioids at higher doses with monitoring if needed; involve acute pain and addiction teams; avoid stigmatising language; plan for post-discharge care.

#### Case 10: Opioid taper

*A 59-year-old woman has been on high-dose oxycodone (180 MME/day) for 6 years for chronic back pain after surgery. Her function is poor, she has fatigue, constipation, and sedation, and she also uses lorazepam nightly. She has no meaningful pain relief.*

**Reasoning.** Long-term high-dose opioid with benzodiazepine and no functional benefit: **high overdose risk and no benefit.**

**Action.** Shared decision to **taper slowly** (e.g. 10% of the dose every 4 weeks, individualised, with pauses), prescribe **naloxone,** address benzodiazepine use carefully, treat depression and sleep, introduce non-opioid and non-drug therapies, assess for opioid use disorder (offer buprenorphine if present), frequent supportive follow-up; do not stop abruptly.

### 36.10 Common pitfalls

- **Using pain scores in isolation** without clinical context, or setting a number as the target of treatment.
- **Failing to ask about pain** in patients who cannot or do not report it (dementia, children, non-verbal, stoic, language barrier).
- **Interpreting behaviour in dementia as psychiatric** and prescribing antipsychotics before analgesics.
- **Assuming that older adults and children "tolerate" pain** or are at higher risk of opioid harm and therefore under-treating.
- **Delay or inadequate analgesia** because of bias, labels ("drug-seeking"), or fear of masking diagnosis (analgesia does not mask diagnosis in acute abdomen).
- **Using codeine and tramadol** indiscriminately, **pethidine,** or NSAIDs in at-risk patients.
- **Forgetting laxatives with opioids,** monitoring for sedation and respiratory depression, and naloxone.
- **Prescribing long-term opioids for chronic non-cancer pain** without clear benefit and review; abruptly stopping them.
- **Failing to treat neuropathic pain with appropriate agents** (using paracetamol/NSAIDs alone).
- **Neglecting psychosocial factors,** mood, sleep, and function in chronic pain.
- **Overinvestigating chronic pain** and reinforcing fear-avoidance; or **dismissing new pain** in a chronic pain patient.
- **Assuming pain is psychological** when examination and tests are normal; or insisting on a structural cause for nociplastic pain.
- **Withholding analgesia from people with substance use disorder.**
- **Ignoring cultural and language needs** in assessment.

### 36.11 Key points

- Pain is a subjective experience; self-report is the gold standard, and inability to communicate does not mean absence of pain.
- Distinguish nociceptive, neuropathic and nociplastic pain, and acute from chronic; use the biopsychosocial model.
- Take a structured history including impact on function, mood, sleep, beliefs, and previous treatments; screen for red flags and neuropathic features.
- Use age- and ability-appropriate tools: NRS or VRS (adults), faces scales (children and language barriers), FLACC/neonatal scales (infants), PAINAD/Abbey (dementia), CPOT/BPS (ICU).
- Pain is under-treated in older adults, children, women, minority groups, patients with substance use history, and those who do not share the clinician's language; use interpreters, structured protocols, and reflection on bias.
- Acute pain: multimodal analgesia, titrate opioids, monitor sedation, prevent constipation, reassess; avoid pethidine, and be careful with codeine, tramadol and NSAIDs.
- Neuropathic pain: gabapentinoids, SNRIs, TCAs, topical agents; chronic pain: education, exercise, psychological therapy, limited drugs, function-focused goals; avoid long-term opioids and never stop them abruptly.
- Individualise care for sickle cell disease, cancer pain, opioid-tolerant patients, dementia, and the end of life.

### 36.12 Self-assessment

1. Give the IASP definition of pain and one implication for patients who cannot speak.
   *Answer: Pain is an unpleasant sensory and emotional experience associated with, or resembling that associated with, actual or potential tissue damage; inability to communicate does not negate the possibility that a person is experiencing pain, so use observational tools and treat when indicated.*

2. Name appropriate pain tools for (a) a neonate, (b) a 3-year-old, (c) a patient with advanced dementia, (d) an intubated ICU patient.
   *Answer: (a) NIPS, PIPP-R, N-PASS or CRIES; (b) FLACC (or faces scale around 4–5 years); (c) PAINAD or Abbey Pain Scale; (d) CPOT or BPS.*

3. What features suggest neuropathic pain and which drugs are first-line?
   *Answer: Burning, shooting, electric-shock pain with allodynia, hyperalgesia and sensory deficit in a neuroanatomical distribution (DN4 ≥4); first-line: gabapentin or pregabalin, amitriptyline or nortriptyline, duloxetine, with topical lidocaine or capsaicin for localised pain.*

4. List strategies to reduce racial and ethnic disparities in analgesia.
   *Answer: Standardised assessment and analgesic protocols, triage-initiated analgesia, time-to-analgesia targets and audit, individualised care plans for conditions such as sickle cell disease, bias awareness training, interpreters, avoiding stigmatising labels.*

5. Why should long-term opioids not be stopped abruptly?
   *Answer: Risk of withdrawal, worsening pain, psychological distress, relapse or overdose after loss of tolerance, and suicide; taper gradually with shared decision-making and support.*

6. In an opioid-tolerant patient on buprenorphine with acute fracture, what is the recommended approach?
   *Answer: Continue buprenorphine (possibly split into divided doses), add multimodal analgesia and regional anaesthesia, and use additional short-acting full-agonist opioids at higher doses as needed, with monitoring.*
