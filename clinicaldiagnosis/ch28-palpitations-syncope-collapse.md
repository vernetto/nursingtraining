## Chapter 28. Palpitations, Syncope and Collapse

### Learning objectives

After studying this chapter you should be able to:

1. Characterise palpitations from the history and decide whether they are likely to be benign or arrhythmic, and which tests to order.
2. Recognise the ECG findings that signal dangerous arrhythmia substrates (pre-excitation, long QT, Brugada pattern, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, arrhythmogenic cardiomyopathy).
3. Define syncope and distinguish it from other causes of transient loss of consciousness (seizure, psychogenic, metabolic, concussion).
4. Classify syncope into reflex, orthostatic and cardiac syncope, and use history, witness account, examination and ECG to separate them.
5. Stratify risk with validated tools and decide who needs admission, monitoring or outpatient follow-up.
6. Choose appropriate investigations (orthostatic BP, ECG, echocardiography, ambulatory monitoring, tilt testing, carotid sinus massage) and understand their limits.
7. Approach the collapsed patient in the first minutes, and give basic management and driving or safety advice.

# Part A: Palpitations

### 28.1 What palpitations are

**Palpitations** are an unpleasant awareness of the heartbeat: a feeling that the heart is racing, pounding, fluttering, skipping or stopping. They are among the commonest reasons for cardiac consultation. The perception does not correlate well with the rhythm: patients with dangerous ventricular tachycardia may feel nothing, and many with benign ectopic beats are very aware of them.

Four mechanisms explain most palpitations:

1. **Premature beats** (atrial or ventricular ectopics): the pause and the forceful post-ectopic beat are felt as a "flip" or "missed beat."
2. **Tachyarrhythmias** (supraventricular tachycardia, atrial fibrillation or flutter, ventricular tachycardia).
3. **Bradyarrhythmias** or pauses (sick sinus syndrome, AV block).
4. **Sinus tachycardia** and hyperdynamic circulation (anxiety, caffeine, fever, thyrotoxicosis, anaemia, pregnancy, drugs), or heightened awareness of a normal heart rhythm.

The important question is: **is there a significant arrhythmia or structural heart disease?**

---

### 28.2 History

The history is the most useful part of the assessment. Characterise the **palpitation episode:**

| Feature | Question | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| **Description** | "Tap out what it feels like on the table." | Regular fast beats: SVT, atrial flutter, VT, sinus tachycardia. Irregularly irregular: AF, frequent ectopics. Isolated thumps or skipped beats: ectopics |
| **Onset and offset** | Sudden or gradual? | **Abrupt start and stop**: re-entrant SVT (AVNRT, AVRT), VT; **gradual** build-up and fade: sinus tachycardia, anxiety |
| **Rate** | Counted? | >150/min regular: SVT, flutter with 2:1 block, VT; rates of 100–120: sinus tachycardia |
| **Duration and frequency** | Seconds, minutes, hours? How often? | Brief isolated: ectopics; episodes of minutes to hours: SVT, paroxysmal AF |
| **Triggers** | Exercise, caffeine, alcohol, stress, lying on the left side, bending, sudden noise, sleep? | Exercise-triggered: catecholaminergic VT, long QT type 1, HCM; alcohol: "holiday heart" AF |
| **Associated symptoms** | Chest pain, breathlessness, dizziness, presyncope or syncope, sweating? | Syncope or chest pain: serious arrhythmia or structural disease |
| **Neck pulsations** | "Frog sign": rapid pounding in the neck | AVNRT (simultaneous atrial and ventricular contraction against a closed AV valve; cannon waves) |
| **Polyuria after the episode** | Passing large volumes of urine | Atrial natriuretic peptide release in SVT or AF |
| **Termination** | Vagal manoeuvres, coughing, breath-holding? | Terminates re-entrant SVT |
| **Previous episodes, diagnoses** | | |
| **Medications and substances** | Beta-agonists, theophylline, decongestants, thyroxine, antidepressants (TCAs), QT-prolonging drugs, caffeine, energy drinks, alcohol, cocaine, amphetamines, nicotine, cannabis, supplements | Many cause palpitations or arrhythmia |
| **Medical background** | Heart disease, valve disease, previous MI, cardiomyopathy, congenital heart disease, thyroid disease, anaemia, diabetes, lung disease, sleep apnoea, pregnancy | Structural disease raises the stakes |
| **Family history** | **Sudden death under 40**, cardiomyopathy, long QT, Brugada, unexplained drowning or accidents, pacemakers or defibrillators in young relatives | Inherited arrhythmia syndrome |
| **Psychological factors** | Panic attacks, anxiety, stress, sleep, mood | Common amplifiers |

#### Examination

- **Pulse** (rate, rhythm, character), **BP** (both arms if appropriate), **SpO₂**, **temperature**.
- **Thyroid:** goitre, tremor, eye signs.
- **Anaemia, signs of heart failure.**
- **Cardiac examination:** murmurs (aortic stenosis, mitral valve prolapse, HCM murmur that increases with Valsalva), displaced apex, signs of pulmonary hypertension.
- **If symptomatic:** ECG immediately.

### 28.3 Causes

| Category | Examples |
|---|---|
| **Cardiac arrhythmias** | Atrial and ventricular ectopics, sinus tachycardia, **AVNRT, AVRT (WPW syndrome)**, atrial tachycardia, **atrial fibrillation, atrial flutter**, ventricular tachycardia, bradyarrhythmias, pacemaker-mediated tachycardia |
| **Structural heart disease** | Valvular disease (mitral valve prolapse, mitral stenosis, aortic regurgitation), cardiomyopathies (hypertrophic, dilated, arrhythmogenic), congenital heart disease, myocarditis, coronary disease |
| **Inherited arrhythmia syndromes** | Long QT, short QT, Brugada syndrome, catecholaminergic polymorphic VT, WPW |
| **Non-cardiac, physiological** | Anxiety and panic, stress, exercise, **caffeine, alcohol, nicotine**, sleep deprivation, pregnancy, fever |
| **Endocrine and metabolic** | **Thyrotoxicosis**, hypoglycaemia, **phaeochromocytoma**, hypocalcaemia, hypokalaemia, hypomagnesaemia, menopause |
| **Haematological** | **Anaemia**, hypovolaemia, polycythaemia |
| **Respiratory** | Hypoxia, COPD, asthma (beta-agonists), pulmonary embolism, sleep apnoea |
| **Drugs** | Sympathomimetics (salbutamol, decongestants, amphetamines, cocaine), anticholinergics, thyroxine, theophylline, tricyclics, antipsychotics, macrolides and quinolones (QT prolongation), digoxin toxicity, vasodilators (reflex tachycardia) |
| **Psychiatric** | Panic disorder, generalised anxiety, somatic symptom disorder, depression |

---

### 28.4 Red flags

Further urgent evaluation (admission or same-day specialist assessment) is needed when palpitations are associated with:

- **Syncope or presyncope**, especially exertional or without warning
- **Chest pain, severe breathlessness, hypotension, heart failure**
- **Sustained palpitations lasting more than minutes with haemodynamic effect**
- **Known structural heart disease, previous MI, cardiomyopathy, or heart failure**
- **Family history of sudden cardiac death or inherited arrhythmia**
- **Abnormal ECG:** pre-excitation (short PR, delta wave), long QT (corrected QT >470 ms in men, >480 ms in women; >500 ms is high risk), Brugada pattern (coved ST elevation in V1–V3), epsilon waves, signs of hypertrophy, Q waves, bundle branch block, high-grade AV block, ventricular ectopy in runs
- **Wide-complex tachycardia** (treat as VT until proven otherwise)

---

### 28.5 Investigations

| Test | Role |
|---|---|
| **12-lead ECG** (at rest, and during an episode if possible) | The key diagnostic test. Look for pre-excitation, long or short QT, Brugada pattern, hypertrophy, Q waves, AV block, arrhythmia |
| **Blood tests** | FBC (anaemia), U&E, magnesium, calcium, glucose, TSH and free T4, troponin if chest pain, drug levels, pregnancy test |
| **Ambulatory ECG monitoring** | Match the monitor to the frequency of symptoms: **24–48 hour Holter** (daily symptoms); **7–14 day patch or external loop recorder** (weekly to monthly symptoms); **smartphone-based/ wearable ECG** (patient-activated); **implantable loop recorder** (infrequent, severe symptoms such as unexplained syncope, up to 3 years) |
| **Echocardiography** | Structural heart disease, valve disease, ventricular function, hypertrophy, pulmonary hypertension |
| **Exercise ECG** | Exercise-induced arrhythmia, ischaemia, catecholaminergic VT |
| **Cardiac MRI** | Cardiomyopathy, myocarditis, arrhythmogenic RV cardiomyopathy |
| **Electrophysiology (EP) study** | Select cases: pre-excitation, recurrent SVT, VT, unexplained syncope with structural disease |
| **Genetic testing** | Suspected inherited arrhythmia syndromes and cardiomyopathies |
| **24-hour urine metanephrines or plasma metanephrines** | Suspected phaeochromocytoma (paroxysmal hypertension, headache, sweating, palpitations) |

#### Recognising common tachycardias on ECG

| Rhythm | ECG features |
|---|---|
| **Sinus tachycardia** | Regular, P waves before each QRS, rate usually <150 |
| **AVNRT** | Regular narrow-complex tachycardia, 150–250/min, P waves hidden in or just after the QRS (pseudo R' in V1) |
| **AVRT (WPW)** | Regular narrow-complex (orthodromic) tachycardia; during sinus rhythm: short PR and delta wave |
| **Atrial flutter** | Regular, narrow-complex, typical sawtooth flutter waves (best seen in II, III, aVF), ventricular rate ~150 with 2:1 block |
| **Atrial fibrillation** | Irregularly irregular narrow-complex rhythm, no P waves, fibrillatory baseline |
| **Ventricular tachycardia** | Regular (monomorphic) wide-complex tachycardia (QRS ≥120 ms), AV dissociation, fusion and capture beats, concordance in precordial leads |
| **Torsades de pointes** | Polymorphic VT with twisting QRS around the baseline, occurring with long QT |
| **Pre-excited AF in WPW** | Irregular, very fast, wide-complex tachycardia of varied QRS morphology; avoid AV-nodal blocking drugs |

---

### 28.6 Acute management of tachyarrhythmias (summary)

- **Unstable** (hypotension, chest pain, heart failure, reduced consciousness): **synchronised DC cardioversion** with sedation or anaesthesia (for VT or any tachycardia causing instability); unsynchronised defibrillation for pulseless VT or VF.
- **Stable regular narrow-complex tachycardia:** **vagal manoeuvres** (modified Valsalva: strain for 15 seconds semi-recumbent, then lie flat with legs raised, increasing termination to about 40–45%); if unsuccessful, **adenosine** 6 mg rapid IV bolus, then 12 mg (and a further 12 mg if needed), warning the patient about chest tightness and a feeling of doom; contraindicated in severe asthma and in WPW with AF. If adenosine is contraindicated: verapamil or diltiazem. Flutter will show slowing but not termination with adenosine.
- **Stable broad-complex tachycardia:** treat as VT unless the diagnosis is certain: specialist advice; amiodarone, procainamide or synchronised cardioversion. **Do not give verapamil** to a patient with VT.
- **AF:** assess stability, rate control (beta-blocker, diltiazem/verapamil, digoxin) or rhythm control (cardioversion, antiarrhythmic drugs, ablation), and **stroke prevention** based on CHA₂DS₂-VASc (Chapter 70); search for triggers (thyrotoxicosis, alcohol, sepsis, PE, hypokalaemia, heart failure, valve disease).
- **Bradyarrhythmia:** atropine for symptomatic bradycardia; pacing for high-degree AV block or sick sinus syndrome.

---

### 28.7 Management of benign palpitations

- **Reassurance** when the ECG and examination are normal and no red flags exist; explain ectopics as common and benign.
- **Avoid triggers:** reduce caffeine, alcohol, nicotine, stimulants; manage stress and sleep; review medications.
- **Treat contributors:** anaemia, thyroid disease, anxiety (CBT, relaxation), electrolyte disturbance.
- **Education** about vagal manoeuvres for SVT and about when to seek help.
- **Catheter ablation** for recurrent symptomatic SVT, WPW, atrial flutter, and selected AF; **beta-blockers** for symptomatic ectopics.
- **Safety-net advice:** attend urgently if palpitations are accompanied by syncope, chest pain, or breathlessness.

# Part B: Syncope and Collapse

### 28.8 Definitions

**Transient loss of consciousness (TLOC)** is a self-limited loss of consciousness with a short duration, loss of awareness, and loss of postural tone. TLOC has several possible causes:

| Category | Examples |
|---|---|
| **Syncope** (TLOC due to transient global cerebral hypoperfusion) | Reflex (vasovagal), orthostatic hypotension, cardiac |
| **Epileptic seizures** | Generalised tonic–clonic, others |
| **Psychogenic TLOC** | Psychogenic non-epileptic seizures, pseudosyncope |
| **Other** | Concussion, hypoglycaemia (usually without complete loss of consciousness and with slower recovery), intoxication, cataplexy, vertebrobasilar TIA (rare as isolated TLOC), subarachnoid haemorrhage |

**Syncope** is characterised by **rapid onset, short duration (typically seconds to a minute or two), and spontaneous, complete and prompt recovery.** **Presyncope** is the feeling of impending faint without loss of consciousness. **Collapse** is a general term for falling to the ground or being unable to remain upright, and includes syncope, seizure, cardiac arrest, stroke, trauma, hypoglycaemia, intoxication, and others.

---

### 28.9 Why syncope matters

- It is common (up to about a third of people will have syncope at some point; peaks in adolescence and in older adults) and accounts for a large number of emergency visits and admissions.
- **Most syncope is benign (reflex or orthostatic),** but **cardiac syncope** carries a high short-term risk of death or serious events (up to 20–30% at one year in some series), and may be the first and only warning of sudden cardiac death.
- Even benign syncope can cause **injury** (head trauma, fractures, road traffic collisions).
- The challenge is to **identify the minority with serious causes** without overtesting the majority.

---

### 28.10 Classification and mechanisms

| Type | Mechanism | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Reflex (neurally mediated) syncope** | Autonomic reflex causing vasodilatation (vasodepressor) and/or bradycardia (cardioinhibitory) | **Vasovagal** (orthostatic or emotional trigger), **situational** (micturition, defecation, coughing, swallowing, post-exercise, laughing), **carotid sinus syndrome** |
| **Orthostatic hypotension (OH)** | Failure of compensatory vasoconstriction or volume on standing | Drug-induced (antihypertensives, diuretics, nitrates, alpha-blockers, antidepressants, antipsychotics, dopaminergic drugs), **volume depletion** (bleeding, diarrhoea, vomiting, poor intake), autonomic failure (diabetes, Parkinson disease, multiple system atrophy, amyloidosis, pure autonomic failure), post-prandial hypotension, alcohol |
| **Cardiac syncope: arrhythmia** | Bradycardia or tachycardia causing a fall in cardiac output | Sinus node dysfunction, **AV block**, ventricular tachycardia, supraventricular tachycardia, inherited syndromes (long QT, Brugada, CPVT), pacemaker malfunction, drug-induced |
| **Cardiac syncope: structural** | Fixed or dynamic obstruction to flow, or pump failure | **Aortic stenosis**, **hypertrophic cardiomyopathy**, mitral stenosis, atrial myxoma, **pulmonary embolism**, severe pulmonary hypertension, tamponade, acute MI, aortic dissection, congenital heart disease |

Other relevant mechanisms: **subclavian steal syndrome** (arm exercise), **hyperventilation**, **vertebrobasilar insufficiency** (usually accompanied by other brainstem symptoms), and **hypovolaemia** from haemorrhage (ruptured ectopic pregnancy, GI bleed, aortic aneurysm).

---

### 28.11 The history

The history, supported by a **witness account,** gives the diagnosis in most cases. Ask about three phases.

#### Before the event

- **Circumstances:** standing, sitting, lying; exertion or after exertion; emotional stress, pain, sight of blood, prolonged standing, hot environment, crowded room, urination, defecation, coughing, swallowing, turning the head (carotid sinus), arm exercise (subclavian steal).
- **Prodrome:** warmth, sweating, pallor, nausea, abdominal discomfort, tunnel vision, hearing muffled ("greying out"), lightheadedness: typical of **vasovagal syncope.**
- **Absence of prodrome** or very brief warning (<5 seconds): **arrhythmic syncope** or seizure.
- **Palpitations or chest pain before collapse:** arrhythmia, ischaemia, aortic stenosis.
- **Aura** (déjà vu, epigastric rising sensation, smell, taste): seizures.
- **Medications:** new or changed antihypertensives, diuretics, vasodilators, QT-prolonging drugs, antiarrhythmics, insulin or hypoglycaemic agents.

#### During the event (from witness)

- **Duration** of unconsciousness; **colour** (pallor in syncope; cyanosis in seizure or cardiac arrest).
- **Posture** during collapse: falls rapidly or slumps.
- **Movements:** brief myoclonic jerks (<30 seconds) are common in syncope and do not indicate epilepsy; **prolonged tonic–clonic activity** suggests a seizure; eyes open or closed; head-turning; automatisms.
- **Tongue biting:** **lateral** tongue biting is quite specific for seizure; tip biting is non-specific.
- **Incontinence:** non-specific.
- **Injury:** common in both cardiac and benign syncope.
- **Breathing and pulse:** if checked; snoring respirations may occur in syncope.

#### After the event

- **Recovery:** rapid and complete in syncope (clear within seconds to a minute); **prolonged confusion** (>5–10 minutes), drowsiness, muscle aches, headache are typical of seizure.
- **Nausea, sweating, pallor** after the event: vasovagal.
- **Chest pain, palpitations, breathlessness** afterward: cardiac.
- **Focal neurological symptoms:** stroke or TIA.

#### Past and family history

- Known heart disease, valve disease, heart failure, previous MI, cardiomyopathy, arrhythmia, pacemaker/ICD, congenital heart disease.
- Diabetes, neurological disease, epilepsy, psychiatric disorders, anaemia.
- **Family history of sudden death** (<40), unexplained accidents, drowning, epilepsy diagnosed in young relatives (long QT syndrome is often misdiagnosed as epilepsy), cardiomyopathy.
- **Previous syncope** and what was found.
- **Pregnancy:** supine hypotension, ectopic pregnancy, PE.

#### Clinical features that discriminate

| Feature | Favours |
|---|---|
| Triggered by pain, emotion, prolonged standing, heat, sight of blood; prodrome of nausea, sweating, pallor | **Vasovagal** |
| Occurs with micturition, coughing, swallowing, defecation, or after exercise | **Situational** |
| Occurs immediately on standing or after meals; in older people on antihypertensives | **Orthostatic hypotension** |
| **During exertion,** or while supine, or sudden without warning; palpitations; family history of sudden death; known structural heart disease; abnormal ECG | **Cardiac** |
| Turning head, shaving, tight collar | **Carotid sinus syndrome** |
| Prolonged unconsciousness, tongue lateral bite, prolonged confusion, aura, cyanosis | **Seizure** |
| Frequent episodes, long duration (minutes), eyes closed throughout, no injury, no colour change | **Psychogenic pseudosyncope** |
| Slow onset, sweating, tremor, in a diabetic on insulin | **Hypoglycaemia** |

---

### 28.12 Examination

- **Vital signs:** HR, BP in both arms, SpO₂, temperature; **orthostatic blood pressure** (below).
- **Cardiovascular:** rhythm; murmurs (ejection systolic murmur of aortic stenosis radiating to the carotids, HCM murmur increased by Valsalva); signs of heart failure; carotid bruits; peripheral pulses (dissection); right heart strain; pacemaker site.
- **Neurological:** focal deficits, gait, cerebellar signs, features of Parkinson disease (autonomic failure), neuropathy.
- **Signs of blood loss:** pallor, melaena (rectal examination), abdominal tenderness, pregnancy.
- **Injuries:** head, tongue, shoulder (dislocation after seizure).
- **Carotid sinus massage** (CSM) in patients **≥40 years with syncope of unknown cause** after exclusion of bruit, recent stroke or TIA (within 3 months), or MI: supine and then upright, **continuous ECG and BP monitoring,** massage for 5–10 seconds on each side (not both at the same time). A positive test (carotid sinus hypersensitivity): **ventricular pause >3 seconds and/or fall in systolic BP >50 mmHg** that reproduces symptoms.

#### Orthostatic blood pressure measurement

1. Patient lies supine for **at least 5 minutes.**
2. Measure BP and HR.
3. Patient stands; measure at **1 minute and 3 minutes** (and 5 or 10 minutes if suspicious).
4. **Classical orthostatic hypotension:** fall in systolic BP ≥20 mmHg or diastolic ≥10 mmHg, or systolic <90 mmHg, within 3 minutes of standing.
5. **Heart-rate response:** a minimal rise (<15 beats/min) with a fall in BP suggests **neurogenic** OH (autonomic failure); a marked rise (>30/min) suggests volume depletion or drug effect (also seen in postural orthostatic tachycardia syndrome, POTS: sustained rise ≥30/min within 10 minutes without OH, with symptoms).

---

### 28.13 Investigations

#### All patients

- **12-lead ECG:** the single most important test (rhythm, conduction, QT interval, pre-excitation, Brugada pattern, ventricular hypertrophy, Q waves, signs of RV strain, epsilon waves, bundle branch block, bifascicular block).
- **Orthostatic BP.**
- **Capillary glucose.**

#### Targeted tests (based on suspicion)

| Suspected cause | Tests |
|---|---|
| **Structural heart disease** (murmur, heart failure signs, abnormal ECG, exertional syncope) | **Echocardiography**; MRI if cardiomyopathy suspected |
| **Arrhythmia** | **Ambulatory ECG monitoring** (Holter, patch, external loop recorder; **implantable loop recorder** for infrequent syncope), EP study when structural disease or conduction abnormalities exist |
| **Exertional syncope** | Exercise testing, echocardiography, MRI |
| **Reflex syncope with unclear diagnosis** | **Tilt-table testing** (reproduces vasovagal syncope; positive in a minority and does not guide treatment strongly; helpful to demonstrate to patients and to distinguish from pseudosyncope or OH) |
| **Carotid sinus syndrome** | Carotid sinus massage |
| **Bleeding, anaemia** | FBC, ferritin, rectal examination, pregnancy test (ectopic) |
| **Cardiac ischaemia, PE** | Troponin, D-dimer, CT pulmonary angiography as appropriate |
| **Seizure vs syncope** | EEG (limited sensitivity), MRI brain, neurology referral; video recording of an event by a witness |
| **Metabolic** | U&E, calcium, glucose, HbA1c, cortisol, thyroid function |
| **Neurogenic OH** | Autonomic function tests, neurology referral |
| **Genetic syndromes** | Genetic testing, family screening |

Tests that are **generally unhelpful** in uncomplicated syncope with a normal ECG and examination include: **routine CT or MRI head, carotid Doppler, EEG, and extensive blood panels.**

---

### 28.14 Risk stratification

Several tools help decide who needs admission or monitoring; they supplement but do not replace clinical judgement.

#### ESC 2018 high-risk features

**Major:**
- **Clinical:** new chest discomfort, breathlessness, abdominal pain, headache; syncope during exertion or supine; sudden palpitations immediately followed by syncope.
- **Past medical history:** severe structural or coronary artery disease (heart failure, low LVEF, previous MI).
- **Physical examination:** unexplained systolic BP <90 mmHg; suspicion of GI bleed on rectal examination; persistent bradycardia (<40 bpm) in the awake state; undiagnosed systolic murmur.
- **ECG:** acute ischaemia; Mobitz II or third-degree AV block; slow AF (<40 bpm); persistent sinus bradycardia <40 bpm or repetitive sinoatrial block or pauses >3 seconds in awake and non-athletic patients; bundle branch block, intraventricular conduction disturbance, ventricular hypertrophy or Q waves consistent with ischaemic heart disease or cardiomyopathy; sustained and non-sustained VT; dysfunction of an implantable cardiac device; **Brugada pattern** (type 1); **QTc >460 ms** in repeated 12-lead ECGs (long QT syndrome).

**Minor** (high risk only if associated with a history consistent with arrhythmic syncope): **no** or only brief prodrome, family history of sudden cardiac death, atypical ECG findings (Mobitz I, asymptomatic sinus bradycardia 40–50 bpm), age >60.

#### San Francisco Syncope Rule (CHESS)

High risk if any of: **C**ongestive heart failure history; **H**aematocrit <30%; **E**CG abnormal (non-sinus rhythm or new changes); **S**hortness of breath; **S**ystolic BP <90 mmHg at triage. (Sensitivity varies between validation studies and is moderate.)

#### Canadian Syncope Risk Score

Scores predisposition to vasovagal syncope, heart disease history, systolic BP <90 or >180, elevated troponin, abnormal QRS axis, QRS duration >130 ms, QTc >480 ms, and ED diagnosis (vasovagal -2 points; cardiac +2 points), giving a risk of serious outcome at 30 days from very low to very high.

#### Disposition

- **High risk (cardiac features, abnormal ECG, major injury, syncope with chest pain, anaemia/bleeding):** admit, monitor, investigate urgently.
- **Intermediate:** observation and rapid-access syncope clinic.
- **Low risk (clear reflex or orthostatic syncope, normal ECG, no heart disease):** reassure, advise, outpatient follow-up.

### 28.15 Management by type

#### Reflex (vasovagal) syncope

- **Education and reassurance,** explaining the benign nature, avoidance of triggers (prolonged standing, heat, dehydration, alcohol), early recognition of prodrome.
- **Physical counter-pressure manoeuvres** at the onset of prodrome: leg crossing with muscle tensing, hand-grip, arm tensing, squatting.
- **Increase fluid and salt intake** (if no hypertension or heart failure), avoid large meals, review antihypertensives (reduce or stop).
- **Tilt training** (limited evidence), **midodrine or fludrocortisone** in selected cases.
- **Pacemaker** for older patients (generally >40) with recurrent unpredictable cardioinhibitory syncope documented by loop recorder or tilt table, and for carotid sinus syndrome with cardioinhibitory response.
- **Beta-blockers** are not recommended for prevention of vasovagal syncope.

#### Orthostatic hypotension

- **Remove precipitants:** stop or reduce antihypertensives, diuretics, nitrates, alpha-blockers, tricyclics, phenothiazines; treat dehydration, anaemia.
- **Non-drug measures:** slow positional change, fluid and salt (if appropriate), head-up sleeping (10–20°), compression stockings or abdominal binder, physical counter-manoeuvres, small frequent meals, avoid hot environment and alcohol.
- **Drugs for neurogenic OH:** fludrocortisone (watch for supine hypertension and hypokalaemia), midodrine, droxidopa, pyridostigmine.
- **Evaluate for neurodegenerative or neuropathic causes** (Parkinson disease, MSA, diabetic neuropathy, amyloidosis) when OH is persistent or severe.

#### Cardiac syncope

- Treat the cause: **pacemaker** for sinus node dysfunction or AV block; **catheter ablation** for SVT, flutter or VT in selected cases; **ICD** for ventricular arrhythmias, HCM with high risk, or inherited arrhythmia syndromes; **antiarrhythmic drugs**; **valve replacement** for aortic stenosis (TAVI or surgery); **surgery or catheter intervention** for other structural disease; treat PE with anticoagulation; manage ischaemia.
- **Genetic counselling and family screening** for inherited conditions.

#### Seizure and psychogenic TLOC

- Refer to neurology (epilepsy) or to psychological and neurological services for functional TLOC.

#### Driving and safety

- Provide advice according to **local legal regulations.** Generally, driving is restricted after unexplained syncope, longer for cardiac syncope or for professional/commercial drivers, with shorter or no restrictions after typical vasovagal syncope with clear triggers. Advise avoiding hazardous activities (heights, swimming alone, operating machinery) until diagnosed and treated.

### 28.16 The collapsed patient: the first minutes

If someone has collapsed (adult, in a clinic, ward or public place):

1. **Check for danger,** then check **responsiveness** (shout and shake gently).
2. If unresponsive: **call for help (emergency number) and a defibrillator;** **open the airway, check breathing** (look, listen, feel for up to 10 seconds).
3. **No normal breathing = start CPR** (30 compressions : 2 breaths, 100–120/min, depth 5–6 cm) and **attach an AED** as soon as it arrives. Do not delay compressions for pulse checks by lay responders.
4. If breathing and a pulse are present: **recovery position,** monitor, check **glucose,** vital signs; look for **injury and obvious cause** (bleeding, seizure, signs of stroke, anaphylaxis, intoxication).
5. **Syncope** typically recovers within moments once the patient is lying flat. Elevate the legs, loosen clothing, ensure cool air, and reassess before allowing to sit up slowly.
6. **Persistent unconsciousness or deterioration:** ABCDE approach (Chapter 37), monitor ECG, secure IV access, send blood tests, glucose, ECG, consider CT head if trauma or focal deficit.

**Causes of collapse (broader) to consider in the emergency setting:** cardiac arrest (VF/VT, asystole, PEA), massive PE, haemorrhage (GI, ruptured aneurysm, ectopic pregnancy, trauma), anaphylaxis, sepsis, hypoglycaemia, stroke or SAH, seizure, drug overdose, severe electrolyte disturbance, acute adrenal insufficiency, hypothermia or heat illness, carbon monoxide.

---

### 28.17 Special populations

- **Young athletes:** exertional syncope or palpitations is a **red flag.** Consider hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, anomalous coronary artery, arrhythmogenic RV cardiomyopathy, myocarditis, long QT, CPVT, Brugada syndrome, WPW, Marfan syndrome (aortic dissection), commotio cordis. Needs cardiology evaluation (ECG, echocardiography, exercise test, MRI) before return to sport.
- **Older adults:** multifactorial (OH, drugs, arrhythmia, aortic stenosis, carotid sinus syndrome); syncope may present as falls or confusion; high risk of injury and cardiac syncope; review medications; consider combined cardiac and falls assessment.
- **Children:** most are reflex or related to breath-holding spells; **long QT syndrome** can masquerade as epilepsy (collapse with exercise, swimming or startle); family history of sudden death and an ECG are essential.
- **Pregnancy:** supine hypotensive syndrome (compression of the IVC; lie on the left), PE, ectopic pregnancy, peripartum cardiomyopathy, arrhythmia; ECG and echocardiography are safe.

### 28.18 Worked cases

#### Case 1: Vasovagal syncope

*A 19-year-old student felt hot, nauseated and "grey-out" vision while standing at a crowded concert, then slumped to the floor for about 20 seconds with a few brief jerks, recovering quickly with pale, sweaty skin and no confusion. Examination, ECG and orthostatic BP are normal. No family history.*

**Reasoning.** Typical trigger, prodrome, rapid recovery, brief myoclonic jerks, normal ECG and examination: **vasovagal syncope.** No further investigation is needed.

**Action.** Reassure, explain triggers and counter-manoeuvres, advise fluids and salt, and safety-net advice. Seek further assessment if syncope is exertional, recurrent without trigger, or associated with injury or palpitations.

#### Case 2: Exertional syncope in a young man

*A 17-year-old footballer collapses without warning during a match and recovers within a minute. His uncle died suddenly at 35. Examination: ejection systolic murmur at the left sternal edge, louder on standing; ECG shows left ventricular hypertrophy with deep T-wave inversion in lateral leads.*

**Reasoning.** Exertional syncope, family history of sudden death, murmur that increases with reduced preload and ECG abnormalities: **hypertrophic cardiomyopathy** with risk of ventricular arrhythmia.

**Action.** Admit/urgent cardiology assessment: echocardiography, cardiac MRI, ambulatory monitoring, risk assessment for ICD, **no sport until evaluated**, family screening and genetic counselling.

#### Case 3: Aortic stenosis

*A 79-year-old man had a syncope while climbing stairs. He has exertional breathlessness and chest tightness. Examination: slow-rising pulse, narrow pulse pressure, ejection systolic murmur radiating to the carotids, soft second heart sound.*

**Reasoning.** Exertional syncope with the classic triad (syncope, angina, dyspnoea) and signs of severe **aortic stenosis.**

**Action.** Echocardiography, avoid vasodilators, refer to the heart team for **valve replacement (surgical or transcatheter)**; symptomatic severe AS has a poor prognosis without intervention.

#### Case 4: Heart block

*A 82-year-old woman has had three episodes of sudden collapse without warning in the last month, injuring her face on one occasion. Pulse 36, regular. ECG: complete heart block with a ventricular escape rhythm.*

**Reasoning.** Syncope due to **third-degree AV block** (Stokes–Adams attacks).

**Action.** Admit, continuous monitoring, atropine or temporary pacing if unstable, review of drugs (beta-blockers, calcium-channel blockers, digoxin), **permanent pacemaker.**

#### Case 5: Orthostatic hypotension

*A 76-year-old man with diabetes and Parkinson disease faints when standing from his chair in the morning; he takes tamsulosin, amlodipine and levodopa. Lying BP 150/84, standing at 1 minute 108/62, HR 70 (no rise).*

**Reasoning.** Fall of >40 mmHg systolic with no heart rate response: **neurogenic orthostatic hypotension** (diabetic neuropathy and Parkinson disease) aggravated by tamsulosin, amlodipine and levodopa.

**Action.** Withdraw or reduce antihypertensives and alpha-blocker, advise hydration, slow standing, compression stockings, head-up sleeping; consider midodrine or fludrocortisone; check for supine hypertension.

#### Case 6: Seizure or syncope?

*A 28-year-old man collapsed at work. His colleague describes that he fell stiffly, shook all limbs for 1–2 minutes, turned blue, bit his tongue on the side, was drowsy and confused for 20 minutes afterward and had a headache and muscle aches. No prodrome other than "a strange smell."*

**Reasoning.** Aura, prolonged tonic–clonic activity, cyanosis, lateral tongue bite, prolonged postictal confusion: **generalised tonic–clonic seizure**, not syncope. Neurology referral, EEG, MRI brain, and driving advice.

#### Case 7: Palpitations and pre-excitation

*A 24-year-old woman has episodes of sudden-onset, sudden-offset palpitations for 15–30 minutes, with a rate of about 200, ending with a cough or breath-hold. ECG in sinus rhythm shows a short PR interval and a slurred upstroke of the QRS (delta wave).*

**Reasoning.** Features of **Wolff–Parkinson–White syndrome** with orthodromic AVRT. Pre-excitation carries a small risk of sudden death if AF with rapid conduction over the accessory pathway occurs.

**Action.** Cardiology referral, echocardiography, **electrophysiology study and ablation** (curative); for acute AF with pre-excitation use procainamide or cardioversion and avoid AV-nodal blockers (adenosine, verapamil, digoxin, beta-blockers).

#### Case 8: Supraventricular tachycardia in the emergency department

*A 35-year-old woman presents with 2 hours of palpitations. HR 190 regular, BP 112/70, narrow QRS, no P waves visible. She is anxious but stable.*

**Reasoning.** Stable regular narrow-complex tachycardia: **AVNRT/AVRT.**

**Action.** Modified Valsalva manoeuvre; if unsuccessful, adenosine 6 mg rapid IV followed by saline flush with continuous ECG recording, then 12 mg if needed; synchronised cardioversion if unstable or refractory; refer for ablation if recurrent.

#### Case 9: Palpitations and AF

*A 68-year-old man with hypertension has 3 days of irregular palpitations and mild breathlessness. HR 124 irregularly irregular, BP 138/84. ECG: atrial fibrillation. TSH is normal.*

**Reasoning.** Newly diagnosed **atrial fibrillation.** CHA₂DS₂-VASc: age 65–74 (1), hypertension (1) = 2 → anticoagulation recommended (Chapter 70).

**Action.** Rate control (beta-blocker or diltiazem), start oral anticoagulant, check echocardiography, thyroid function, U&E; consider cardioversion if onset <48 hours (or after anticoagulation) and rhythm control strategy; treat contributing factors (alcohol, sleep apnoea, hypertension).

#### Case 10: Collapse with abdominal pain

*A 26-year-old woman collapses at work after a day of lower abdominal pain. She is pale and clammy, HR 124, BP 82/50. Her last period was 8 weeks ago.*

**Reasoning.** Collapse with hypotension, tachycardia, abdominal pain and amenorrhoea: **ruptured ectopic pregnancy** with haemorrhagic shock until proven otherwise.

**Action.** Resuscitation (two large-bore cannulae, fluids/blood), urgent pregnancy test and bedside ultrasound, emergency gynaecological surgery. Syncope in a woman of childbearing age requires a pregnancy test.

#### Case 11: Brugada pattern

*A 38-year-old man wakes at night gasping and collapses on one occasion; his brother died suddenly at 40 during sleep. ECG shows coved ST elevation in V1–V2 followed by negative T waves (type 1 Brugada pattern), more evident when he has a fever.*

**Reasoning.** Brugada syndrome with arrhythmic syncope: high-risk features (syncope, spontaneous type 1 pattern, family history).

**Action.** Cardiology/electrophysiology referral, **ICD** consideration, avoidance of triggers (fever treated promptly, certain drugs listed on Brugada resources, alcohol excess), family screening and genetic counselling.

### 28.19 Common pitfalls

- **Dismissing palpitations as anxiety** without an ECG or considering arrhythmia.
- **Failing to record an ECG during symptoms** or choosing a monitor that does not match symptom frequency.
- **Treating wide-complex tachycardia as SVT** (and giving verapamil).
- **Giving AV-nodal blocking drugs** in pre-excited AF.
- **Missing pre-excitation, long QT, Brugada pattern and cardiomyopathy** on a "normal" ECG.
- **Assuming a faint is vasovagal** when it was exertional, supine, or occurred without prodrome.
- **Misdiagnosing syncope as epilepsy** (long QT syndrome) or seizure as syncope.
- **Forgetting orthostatic blood pressure** and medication review.
- **Failing to examine for a murmur** (aortic stenosis, HCM).
- **Not performing a pregnancy test** in women of reproductive age.
- **Ordering CT head, carotid Doppler or EEG** routinely in uncomplicated syncope.
- **Relying on a normal Holter** to exclude infrequent arrhythmia.
- **Overlooking carotid sinus hypersensitivity** in older patients with unexplained falls or syncope.
- **Not giving driving advice** and safety-net instructions.
- **Sending a young person with exertional syncope home** without cardiac assessment.

### 28.20 Key points

- Palpitations: tap-out the rhythm, ask about onset and offset, triggers, associated syncope and family history; always obtain an ECG; match ambulatory monitoring to symptom frequency.
- Red flags: syncope, structural heart disease, family history of sudden death, abnormal ECG (pre-excitation, long QT, Brugada, hypertrophy), wide-complex tachycardia.
- Treat unstable tachyarrhythmia with synchronised cardioversion; stable narrow-complex SVT with vagal manoeuvres then adenosine; treat broad-complex tachycardia as VT.
- Syncope is rapid in onset, brief, with spontaneous complete recovery; distinguish from seizure, psychogenic TLOC, concussion and metabolic causes.
- Three main syncope types: reflex, orthostatic hypotension, cardiac. History plus witness plus ECG plus orthostatic BP gives the diagnosis in most.
- Cardiac syncope has a high short-term risk; risk-stratify using ESC features and validated scores; admit high-risk patients.
- Treat according to type: education and counter-manoeuvres (reflex), medication review and volume (OH), device or procedure (cardiac).
- In collapse, check response and breathing, start CPR and use an AED if no normal breathing, check glucose, and look for treatable causes.

### 28.21 Self-assessment

1. List five features of the history suggestive of vasovagal syncope.
   *Answer: Trigger (prolonged standing, heat, pain, emotion, sight of blood), prodrome of warmth, sweating, nausea, pallor, tunnel vision, gradual onset, rapid recovery, absence of heart disease.*

2. What defines classical orthostatic hypotension?
   *Answer: A fall in systolic BP ≥20 mmHg or diastolic ≥10 mmHg (or systolic <90 mmHg) within 3 minutes of standing.*

3. Name four ECG findings that make arrhythmic syncope more likely.
   *Answer (any four): bifascicular block or second-degree Mobitz II/third-degree AV block; sinus pauses >3 seconds or sinus bradycardia <40; pre-excitation; long or short QT; Brugada pattern; ventricular hypertrophy/Q waves; VT; epsilon waves.*

4. How does the first-line treatment differ between stable narrow-complex SVT and stable wide-complex tachycardia?
   *Answer: SVT: vagal manoeuvres then adenosine; wide-complex tachycardia is treated as VT (amiodarone/procainamide/cardioversion, specialist input) and AV-nodal blockers such as verapamil should be avoided.*

5. What features favour seizure over syncope?
   *Answer: Aura, prolonged tonic–clonic activity, cyanosis, lateral tongue biting, prolonged postictal confusion and drowsiness, headache and muscle aches afterward.*

6. Why should long QT syndrome be considered in a child diagnosed with epilepsy?
   *Answer: Arrhythmic collapse from torsades de pointes can mimic seizure (jerking from cerebral hypoperfusion), especially if triggered by exercise, swimming, startle, or with a family history of sudden death; an ECG with QTc measurement is needed.*
