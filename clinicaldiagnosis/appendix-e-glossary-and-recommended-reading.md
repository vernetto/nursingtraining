# Appendix E: Glossary and Recommended Reading

## E1. Glossary

Terms are defined as used in this manual. Chapter references point to the main discussion.

**Abbreviations used in clinical notes**
ABCDE: Airway, Breathing, Circulation, Disability, Exposure (Ch. 37). ACS: acute coronary syndrome. AF: atrial fibrillation. AKI: acute kidney injury. ALP/ALT/AST/GGT: liver enzymes (App. C). BNP: B-type natriuretic peptide. CKD: chronic kidney disease. CRP: C-reactive protein. CTPA: CT pulmonary angiogram. DKA: diabetic ketoacidosis. DVT: deep vein thrombosis. eGFR: estimated glomerular filtration rate. GCS: Glasgow Coma Scale. HHS: hyperosmolar hyperglycaemic state. INR: international normalised ratio. JVP: jugular venous pressure. MAP: mean arterial pressure. NEWS: National Early Warning Score. PE: pulmonary embolism. POCUS: point-of-care ultrasound. SpO₂: peripheral oxygen saturation. TIA: transient ischaemic attack.

**Anchoring.** Cognitive bias of fixing on early information and failing to adjust as new data arrive (Ch. 2).

**Availability bias.** Judging likelihood by how easily examples come to mind (Ch. 2).

**Base rate.** The prevalence of a condition in the relevant population, the starting point for probability (Ch. 1).

**Bayes' theorem.** Method for updating a probability using new evidence: post-test odds = pre-test odds × likelihood ratio (Ch. 1, 4).

**Can't-miss diagnosis.** A condition that is uncommon but dangerous or time-critical, kept on the differential regardless of probability until excluded (Ch. 3).

**Chief complaint.** The patient's main reason for seeking care, in their own words (Ch. 6).

**Clinical decision rule.** A validated tool combining history, examination and test findings into a probability or risk category (e.g. Wells, CURB-65, HEART). Applies only to the population and setting in which it was validated (Ch. 70).

**Collateral history.** Information from relatives, carers, records or witnesses when the patient cannot provide a full account (Ch. 8).

**Confirmation bias.** Seeking and over-weighting evidence that supports the working diagnosis (Ch. 2).

**Diagnostic momentum.** A label that gathers certainty as it is passed from clinician to clinician without being re-examined (Ch. 2).

**Diagnostic error.** A diagnosis that is missed, wrong or delayed, as judged by later information (Ch. 2).

**Differential diagnosis.** Ranked list of possible explanations for the patient's presentation (Ch. 3).

**Dual-process theory.** Model of reasoning contrasting fast, intuitive pattern recognition (System 1) with slow, analytic reasoning (System 2) (Ch. 1).

**Early warning score.** Aggregate of physiological observations that flags deterioration (Ch. 11, 44).

**False negative / false positive.** A negative result in someone with disease / a positive result in someone without it (Ch. 4).

**Framing effect.** Being influenced by how information is presented (Ch. 2).

**Gestalt.** The overall impression of a patient, often accurate for "sick/not sick" but fallible (Ch. 1, 10).

**Hypothetico-deductive reasoning.** Generating hypotheses early and testing them with targeted questions and examinations (Ch. 1).

**Illness script.** Mental summary of a disease's typical presentation, risk factors, course and mimics, built through experience (Ch. 1).

**Incidentaloma.** An unexpected finding on imaging or testing, unrelated to the clinical question (Ch. 69).

**Likelihood ratio (LR).** How much a result changes the odds of disease: LR+ = sensitivity ÷ (1 – specificity); LR– = (1 – sensitivity) ÷ specificity. LR+ >10 or LR– <0.1 are strong (Ch. 4).

**Medically unexplained symptoms / functional disorder.** Persistent symptoms without an identified structural or biochemical explanation; diagnosed by positive features, not only by exclusion (Ch. 60).

**Negative predictive value (NPV).** Probability that a person with a negative test does not have the disease; depends on prevalence (Ch. 4).

**Number needed to test / treat.** Number of people to test or treat to detect one case or prevent one event (Ch. 4, 69).

**Overdiagnosis.** Detecting a condition that would never have caused symptoms or harm (Ch. 69).

**Pattern recognition.** Rapid matching of a presentation to a known illness script (Ch. 1).

**Pearl.** A short clinical teaching point from experience; treat as hypothesis, not evidence.

**Positive predictive value (PPV).** Probability that a person with a positive test has the disease; rises with prevalence (Ch. 4).

**Pre-test probability.** Estimated probability of disease before testing, based on prevalence and clinical features (Ch. 1, 4).

**Premature closure.** Accepting a diagnosis before it has been fully verified (Ch. 2).

**Prevalence.** Proportion of a population with a condition at a given time (Ch. 1).

**Pretest/test–treat thresholds.** Probabilities below which one neither tests nor treats, and above which one treats without further testing (Ch. 4).

**Red flag.** Symptom, sign or context that raises the probability of serious disease (Ch. 72, App. D).

**Safety-netting.** Explicit advice on what to watch for, when and how to seek help, and arranged follow-up (Ch. 73).

**Sensitivity (SnNout).** Proportion of people with disease who test positive; high sensitivity, negative result helps rule out (Ch. 4).

**Shock index.** Heart rate divided by systolic blood pressure (Ch. 11).

**SOAP.** Subjective, Objective, Assessment, Plan: structure for clinical notes (Ch. 9).

**Specificity (SpPin).** Proportion of people without disease who test negative; high specificity, positive result helps rule in (Ch. 4).

**SOCRATES / OLDCARTS.** Mnemonics for pain and symptom history: Site, Onset, Character, Radiation, Associations, Time course, Exacerbating/relieving factors, Severity; Onset, Location, Duration, Character, Aggravating/Alleviating, Radiation, Timing, Severity (Ch. 6).

**Spectrum bias.** Test performance estimated in one population (usually sicker) does not apply to another (Ch. 4).

**Sutton's law.** "Go where the money is": test where the disease is most likely to be found (Ch. 3). Counter-balanced by **Occam's razor** (prefer a single explanation) and **Hickam's dictum** ("patients can have as many diseases as they damn well please"), particularly relevant in older adults.

**Triage.** Sorting patients by urgency (Ch. 37, 72).

**Uncertainty.** The unavoidable state of working with incomplete information; managed by shared decision-making, provisional diagnosis, time as a diagnostic tool and safety-netting (Ch. 75).

**VINDICATE.** Mnemonic for categories of disease: Vascular, Infectious, Neoplastic, Degenerative, Iatrogenic/Intoxication, Congenital, Autoimmune, Trauma, Endocrine/Metabolic (Ch. 3).

**Watchful waiting.** Planned observation over time, with defined review points (Ch. 73, 75).

**Wells score.** Pre-test probability tools for DVT and PE (Ch. 70).

**Medical terms commonly confused**

| Term pair | Distinction |
|---|---|
| Sign vs symptom | A sign is observed by the clinician; a symptom is reported by the patient |
| Acute vs chronic | Defined by onset and duration, with different cut-offs for each condition |
| Syndrome vs disease | A syndrome is a cluster of features; a disease has a defined cause or pathology |
| Idiopathic vs cryptogenic | Idiopathic: no identifiable cause; cryptogenic: cause hidden, still presumed |
| Pyrexia vs hyperthermia | Pyrexia: thermoregulatory setpoint raised; hyperthermia: heat overwhelms regulation |
| Hypoxia vs hypoxaemia | Hypoxia: low oxygen at tissue level; hypoxaemia: low oxygen in blood |
| Dyspnoea vs tachypnoea | Dyspnoea: sensation of breathlessness; tachypnoea: rapid breathing (a sign) |
| Syncope vs seizure | Syncope: transient cerebral hypoperfusion; seizure: abnormal electrical activity |
| Vertigo vs dizziness | Vertigo: illusion of movement; dizziness: broad term including light-headedness |
| Sensitivity vs positive predictive value | Sensitivity describes the test given disease; PPV describes disease given a positive test |
| Incidence vs prevalence | New cases over time vs total cases at a point in time |
| Screening vs diagnostic testing | Screening: asymptomatic population; diagnostic: symptomatic or screen-positive |

This list is selective and for orientation. Editions change; use the latest edition available and the guidelines current in your jurisdiction. No title here replaces local protocols.

## E3. How to keep the manual's content current

1. **Check dates.** Guidelines for sepsis, cardiac biomarkers, resuscitation, hypertension, anticoagulation and cancer referral change every few years.
2. **Prefer primary sources** (guidelines, systematic reviews, local protocols) to secondary summaries, including this manual.
3. **Verify every dose, threshold and cut-off** before applying it to a real patient. Where this manual and your local protocol disagree, follow the protocol and ask why.
4. **Keep a personal "error log"** (Ch. 77): cases where your reasoning failed, the cognitive trap involved, and what you changed.
5. **Teach what you learn.** Explaining a diagnosis aloud is the best test of understanding it.

*This manual is an educational text built from fictional composite cases. It is not a substitute for clinical training, supervision, local protocols or professional judgment, and it must not be used as the sole basis for decisions about any real patient's care.*
