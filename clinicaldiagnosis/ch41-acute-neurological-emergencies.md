## Chapter 41. Acute Neurological Emergencies

### Learning objectives

After studying this chapter you should be able to:

1. Recognise acute stroke with FAST/BE-FAST, distinguish ischaemic from haemorrhagic stroke, and identify common stroke mimics.
2. Describe the time-critical pathway for acute ischaemic stroke: imaging, intravenous thrombolysis, mechanical thrombectomy, antithrombotic treatment, blood pressure, glucose, and stroke unit care.
3. Manage intracerebral haemorrhage and subarachnoid haemorrhage, including blood pressure control, reversal of anticoagulation, and referral for neurosurgery.
4. Recognise and manage TIA, cerebral venous thrombosis, and cervical artery dissection.
5. Diagnose bacterial meningitis and encephalitis from clinical features and CSF findings, and give empirical antimicrobial and adjunctive treatment without delay.
6. Recognise raised intracranial pressure and herniation, and apply first-line measures.
7. Recognise acute neuromuscular emergencies (Guillain–Barré syndrome, myasthenic crisis), spinal cord compression, and traumatic brain injury, and know the red flags that demand urgent imaging and referral.

---

### 41.1 Overview: "time is brain"

The brain has virtually no energy reserve. In a large-vessel ischaemic stroke, an estimated **1.9 million neurons die each minute** that the vessel remains occluded, and benefit from reperfusion therapy declines steeply with time. Likewise, bacterial meningitis, status epilepticus, spinal cord compression, and raised intracranial pressure cause irreversible injury in hours.

Common principles across neurological emergencies:

1. **ABCDE first;** glucose in every patient with neurological symptoms (hypoglycaemia mimics stroke).
2. **Establish the time of onset (or "last known well")** precisely.
3. **Rapid, targeted examination:** GCS, pupils, focal deficits, meningism, fundi, signs of raised ICP; use the NIHSS in stroke.
4. **Emergency neuroimaging** (non-contrast CT head first in most emergencies; MRI when appropriate).
5. **Treat time-critical conditions in parallel with diagnosis** (antibiotics in suspected meningitis; thrombolysis in stroke; reversal of anticoagulation in haemorrhage; benzodiazepines in seizures).
6. **Call early for specialist help** (stroke team, neurosurgery, neurology, ICU).
7. **Prevent secondary brain injury:** avoid hypoxia, hypotension, hypo- or hypercapnia, hyper- and hypoglycaemia, hyperthermia, seizures, and raised ICP.

# Part A: Stroke and Related Vascular Emergencies

### 41.2 Recognition of stroke

**Stroke** is the sudden onset of a focal neurological deficit of presumed vascular origin lasting more than 24 hours (or causing death), or evidence of infarction or haemorrhage on imaging regardless of duration. **Transient ischaemic attack (TIA)** is a transient episode of neurological dysfunction due to focal brain, spinal cord or retinal ischaemia **without** acute infarction on imaging (the tissue-based definition).

#### Screening tools for the public and first responders

- **FAST:** **F**ace drooping, **A**rm weakness, **S**peech difficulty, **T**ime to call emergency services.
- **BE-FAST** adds **B**alance (sudden loss of balance) and **E**yes (sudden vision loss or double vision), improving detection of posterior circulation strokes.
- **ROSIER, Cincinnati Prehospital Stroke Scale, LAPSS, FAST-ED, RACE** (large-vessel occlusion screens used by paramedics).

#### Clinical syndromes (what to look for)

| Territory | Features |
|---|---|
| **Middle cerebral artery (MCA)** | Contralateral face and arm > leg weakness and sensory loss, **homonymous hemianopia,** gaze preference toward the lesion; **dominant hemisphere:** aphasia (Broca: non-fluent; Wernicke: fluent, impaired comprehension); **non-dominant:** neglect, anosognosia, constructional apraxia |
| **Anterior cerebral artery** | Contralateral leg weakness > arm, abulia, urinary incontinence |
| **Internal carotid artery occlusion** | MCA ± ACA syndrome; **amaurosis fugax** (ipsilateral transient monocular visual loss) |
| **Posterior circulation (vertebrobasilar)** | Vertigo, vomiting, diplopia, dysarthria, dysphagia, ataxia, **crossed signs** (ipsilateral cranial nerve deficit with contralateral motor/sensory loss), visual field loss (PCA), reduced consciousness; **basilar artery occlusion:** sudden coma, quadriparesis, locked-in syndrome, often preceded by prodrome (vertigo, headache, neck pain) |
| **Lateral medullary (Wallenberg) syndrome** | Vertigo, ipsilateral facial pain/numbness, Horner syndrome, ataxia, dysphagia; contralateral loss of pain and temperature in the body |
| **Cerebellar infarct/haemorrhage** | Vertigo, vomiting, headache, truncal ataxia, inability to walk; risk of **swelling, hydrocephalus, and brainstem compression** (needs neurosurgical review) |
| **Lacunar syndromes** (small deep vessel disease) | Pure motor hemiparesis, pure sensory stroke, sensorimotor stroke, ataxic hemiparesis, dysarthria–clumsy hand |
| **Central retinal artery occlusion** | Painless sudden monocular vision loss, cherry-red spot: treat as stroke emergency |

**Large vessel occlusion (LVO):** severe deficits (NIHSS ≥6), gaze deviation, severe aphasia, neglect, hemiplegia; candidates for **thrombectomy.**

#### The NIHSS (National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale)

A standardised 0–42 scale assessing level of consciousness, gaze, visual fields, facial palsy, motor arm and leg, ataxia, sensation, language, dysarthria, and extinction/inattention. Higher scores indicate more severe deficits (**0** none, **1–4** minor, **5–15** moderate, **16–20** moderate-to-severe, **21–42** severe). Used for triage, treatment decisions, and monitoring.

#### Stroke mimics (about 20–30% of suspected strokes)

| Mimic | Clues |
|---|---|
| **Hypoglycaemia** | Check glucose in all |
| **Seizure with postictal (Todd) paralysis** | Witnessed seizure, history of epilepsy |
| **Migraine with aura** | Positive visual phenomena, headache, gradual spread |
| **Functional neurological disorder** | Inconsistent signs, Hoover sign |
| **Sepsis/delirium, metabolic encephalopathy, hyponatraemia** | Fever, confusion, no focal signs |
| **Subdural haematoma, brain tumour, abscess** | Gradual progression, headache; CT |
| **Peripheral vestibular disorder, Bell palsy** | Vertigo without brainstem signs; isolated facial weakness including forehead |
| **Hypertensive encephalopathy/PRES, drug toxicity, Wernicke** | Confusion, ophthalmoplegia, ataxia |
| **Multiple sclerosis, acute demyelination, myasthenia gravis, GBS, spinal cord lesion** | Subacute, not vascular pattern |

**Practical rule:** **treat as stroke until proven otherwise;** do not withhold imaging or pathway activation for fear of a mimic. Thrombolysis in mimics is relatively safe.

### 41.3 Acute ischaemic stroke

#### Immediate assessment and actions (stroke code)

1. **Activate the stroke team** (pre-alert from the ambulance; "stroke call").
2. **ABC:** airway protection if GCS ≤8; oxygen **only if SpO₂ <94%** (routine oxygen is not beneficial and may be harmful); position (head flat is not necessary; sit up if breathless).
3. **Glucose** (treat hypoglycaemia; target 7.8–10 mmol/L (140–180 mg/dL) thereafter).
4. **IV access (two cannulae),** bloods: FBC, U&E, glucose, coagulation (INR, aPTT), troponin, lipids, HbA1c, pregnancy test if relevant, group and save; **ECG** (AF, MI).
5. **Record time of onset or last known well,** NIHSS, medications (especially anticoagulants), medical history.
6. **Nil by mouth until swallow screening** (aspiration risk).
7. **Immediate non-contrast CT head** to **exclude haemorrhage** and early infarct signs (ASPECTS); **CT angiography (head and neck)** for LVO, dissection, carotid stenosis; **CT perfusion or MRI (DWI–FLAIR mismatch)** for extended window or unknown onset. **Door-to-CT target ≤20–25 minutes; door-to-needle ≤60 minutes (optimally ≤30–45 minutes).**
8. **Do not delay thrombolysis for results of blood tests** (except glucose; INR/platelets only if there is a suspected coagulopathy).

#### Reperfusion therapies

**Intravenous thrombolysis**

- **Alteplase:** **0.9 mg/kg (maximum 90 mg);** 10% as a bolus over 1 minute, remainder over 60 minutes. **Tenecteplase** (0.25 mg/kg, maximum 25 mg, single bolus) is non-inferior in trials and is increasingly used (simpler, faster).
- **Time window:** **within 4.5 hours** of symptom onset (or last known well). **Extended window (up to ~9 hours or wake-up stroke)** selected by advanced imaging (CT perfusion mismatch or MRI DWI–FLAIR mismatch) in specialist centres.
- **Benefit:** greatest the earlier it is given (number needed to treat for an excellent outcome decreases with earlier treatment).
- **Key eligibility criteria and contraindications (follow local protocol):**
  - **Absolute:** intracranial haemorrhage on CT; clinical presentation suggestive of SAH; **blood pressure persistently >185/110 mmHg despite treatment;** **blood glucose <2.7 mmol/L** (correct first; may be a mimic); active internal bleeding; **platelets <100 × 10⁹/L; INR >1.7; therapeutic LMWH within 24 hours; DOAC within 48 hours** (unless specific test normal or reversed); aortic dissection; infective endocarditis; intracranial or intraspinal surgery, **serious head trauma or previous stroke within 3 months;** major surgery or trauma within 14 days (relative); GI or urinary bleeding within 21 days (relative); intracranial neoplasm (intra-axial), large AVM or aneurysm.
  - **Relative:** very mild or rapidly improving deficits (non-disabling; may be better with antiplatelets), seizure at onset with postictal deficit, recent MI, pregnancy, extensive early hypodensity (>1/3 MCA territory).
- **Risks:** symptomatic intracranial haemorrhage (about 2–6%); orolingual angioedema (especially with ACE inhibitors), major bleeding. Counsel the patient or family where possible, but **do not delay** for lengthy discussion.
- **Post-thrombolysis care:** BP <180/105 mmHg for 24 hours; neurological observations every 15 minutes for the first 2 hours, then 30 minutes for 6 hours, then hourly to 24 hours; **no antithrombotics for 24 hours;** repeat CT at 24 hours before starting antiplatelets; **if neurological deterioration or severe headache, vomiting, or acute hypertension:** stop the infusion, urgent CT, check coagulation, cryoprecipitate/tranexamic acid.

**Mechanical thrombectomy**

- **Indication:** **large vessel occlusion (internal carotid artery, M1 and proximal M2 segments of the MCA, basilar artery)** with disabling deficits, within **6 hours** of onset, and up to **24 hours in selected patients** with favourable imaging (small core and salvageable penumbra: DAWN, DEFUSE-3), and in selected large-core strokes; **can be given in addition to IV thrombolysis** (bridging) or alone if thrombolysis is contraindicated. Number needed to treat is about 2.6 for improved disability.
- Requires a comprehensive stroke centre with neurointerventional capability: **rapid transfer is essential** (drip-and-ship vs mothership).

#### Antithrombotic and other acute management

- **Aspirin 300 mg** as soon as possible (after haemorrhage has been excluded by imaging and **24 hours after thrombolysis**) for 14 days, then long-term antiplatelet (clopidogrel or aspirin–dipyridamole) for secondary prevention.
- **Dual antiplatelet therapy (aspirin + clopidogrel) for 21 days** in minor stroke (NIHSS ≤3) or high-risk TIA (ABCD2 ≥4), started within 24 hours (CHANCE, POINT, INSPIRES), then single antiplatelet.
- **Anticoagulation** for atrial fibrillation: DOAC started early (days 2–3 in minor stroke, day 6–7 in moderate, day 12–14 in major stroke, or earlier per ELAN trial guidance) after repeat imaging; **not** in the first hours.
- **Blood pressure:** **do not lower BP routinely** in the first 24–48 hours in ischaemic stroke (cerebral autoregulation is impaired; hypotension worsens the penumbra); treat only if **>220/120 mmHg** (reduce by about 15% in 24 hours), or **>185/110 before thrombolysis** (labetalol, glyceryl trinitrate patch, or nicardipine), or if there are other indications (aortic dissection, acute MI, heart failure, hypertensive encephalopathy). **Avoid hypotension;** resume antihypertensives after 24–72 hours when stable.
- **Glucose:** treat hyperglycaemia (>10 mmol/L) with insulin; prevent hypoglycaemia.
- **Temperature:** treat fever (paracetamol, search for infection); avoid hyperthermia.
- **Oxygen:** only if SpO₂ <94%.
- **Fluids:** isotonic saline or balanced crystalloid; avoid hypotonic fluids and overload.
- **Swallow screening** and dietitian; **nasogastric feeding** if unsafe swallow; oral hygiene (prevents aspiration pneumonia).
- **VTE prophylaxis:** intermittent pneumatic compression (IPC) stockings for immobile patients; LMWH in selected patients with high risk.
- **Early mobilisation** (but not very early intensive mobilisation within 24 hours); multidisciplinary rehabilitation (physiotherapy, occupational therapy, speech and language therapy); **stroke unit care** reduces death and disability.
- **Malignant MCA infarction (large hemispheric infarction with cerebral oedema and herniation):** **decompressive hemicraniectomy** within 48 hours in patients **≤60 years** (and selected older patients, after discussion), reduces mortality; **cerebellar infarction with hydrocephalus or brainstem compression:** **posterior fossa decompression** and/or EVD.
- **Seizures** (treat if they occur; no routine prophylaxis).
- **Statin** (high-intensity), antihypertensive therapy after the acute phase, glycaemic control, smoking cessation, lifestyle advice.
- **Search for aetiology:** carotid and vertebral imaging, echocardiography (including bubble study for PFO in young patients), ECG monitoring for AF (prolonged monitoring up to 30 days or implantable loop recorder in cryptogenic stroke), vasculitis/thrombophilia testing in young patients, dissection, patent foramen ovale closure in selected cases.
- **Carotid revascularisation:** **carotid endarterectomy within 2 weeks** (ideally within days) for **symptomatic 70–99% (or 50–69% in selected men) stenosis;** carotid stenting for high-risk surgical patients.

#### Transient ischaemic attack (TIA)

- **TIA is a stroke emergency:** the **risk of stroke after TIA** is about **5–10% within 2 days, up to 10–15% within 90 days** (higher with carotid stenosis, atrial fibrillation, crescendo events, and large-vessel disease).
- **Assessment:** **within 24 hours** by a stroke specialist (not "next available outpatient"): **brain MRI with DWI** (CT if MRI unavailable), vascular imaging (CTA, MRA, carotid duplex), ECG, blood tests, risk stratification (ABCD2 score has limitations; imaging and aetiology are more informative).
- **Treatment:** **aspirin 300 mg immediately** (after excluding haemorrhage), **dual antiplatelet therapy for 21 days** if high risk, statin, BP management, anticoagulation for AF, urgent carotid intervention, driving advice (follow local regulations).
- **TIA mimics:** migraine aura, focal seizures, hypoglycaemia, syncope, vestibular disorders, transient global amnesia.

### 41.4 Intracerebral haemorrhage (ICH)

**Features:** sudden headache, vomiting, **rapid deterioration in consciousness,** focal deficit progressing over minutes to hours, seizures, markedly raised blood pressure; indistinguishable from ischaemic stroke clinically: **CT is mandatory.**

**Causes:** **hypertension** (deep: basal ganglia, thalamus, pons, cerebellum), **cerebral amyloid angiopathy** (lobar haemorrhage in older adults), anticoagulant and antiplatelet therapy, coagulopathy, vascular malformations (AVM, cavernoma), aneurysm, tumour, venous thrombosis, haemorrhagic transformation of infarct, drugs (cocaine, amphetamines), vasculitis, moyamoya, reversible cerebral vasoconstriction syndrome, eclampsia.

**Management:**

1. **ABCDE,** airway protection if GCS ≤8, glucose, avoid hypoxia.
2. **CT head ± CT angiography** (spot sign, aneurysm, AVM; contrast extravasation predicts expansion).
3. **Blood pressure:** early **intensive reduction** to a **systolic target of about 130–150 mmHg** (within 1 hour; avoid <130 mmHg; INTERACT-3/ATACH-2 context) using IV labetalol, nicardipine, or GTN; avoid excessive variability.
4. **Reverse anticoagulation immediately:**
   - **Warfarin:** **4-factor prothrombin complex concentrate (PCC)** (25–50 IU/kg depending on INR) plus **IV vitamin K 5–10 mg.**
   - **Dabigatran:** idarucizumab 5 g IV; **factor Xa inhibitors** (rivaroxaban, apixaban, edoxaban): andexanet alfa or PCC (50 IU/kg), activated charcoal if within 2 hours.
   - **Heparin:** protamine.
   - **Antiplatelet-associated ICH:** **platelet transfusion is not recommended** (PATCH trial showed harm) unless a neurosurgical procedure is planned.
   - Correct thrombocytopenia (<50–100), DDAVP in uraemic platelet dysfunction.
5. **Neurosurgical referral:** **evacuation** for **cerebellar haemorrhage >3 cm, with brainstem compression or hydrocephalus,** **superficial lobar haemorrhage with deterioration,** **intraventricular haemorrhage with hydrocephalus (EVD),** vascular lesions (AVM, aneurysm) requiring treatment; minimally invasive evacuation techniques are being studied. Large deep haemorrhages are usually managed medically.
6. **Intracranial pressure management** (head up 30°, osmotherapy if herniation, ventilation).
7. **Seizures:** treat clinical seizures; EEG for unexplained depressed consciousness.
8. **Supportive:** normoglycaemia, normothermia, IPC for VTE prophylaxis (add LMWH after 24–48 hours if stable), swallow assessment, stroke unit care.
9. **Prognosis tools** (ICH score) should not drive premature withdrawal of care ("self-fulfilling prophecy"); avoid early DNAR decisions based solely on the score in the first 24–48 hours.
10. **Secondary prevention:** BP control (target <130/80), reassess antithrombotic therapy individually; screen for causes.

### 41.5 Subarachnoid haemorrhage (SAH)

(Clinical features and diagnosis in Chapter 24.) Acute management after confirmation:

- **Transfer to a neurosurgical centre;** resuscitation (ABC), analgesia, antiemetics, **keep BP <160 mmHg systolic** until the aneurysm is secured (avoid hypotension), bed rest, **reverse anticoagulants,** correct coagulopathy.
- **CT angiography or digital subtraction angiography** to identify the aneurysm; **early (within 24 hours) securing of the aneurysm** by **endovascular coiling** (preferred when feasible) or **neurosurgical clipping** to prevent rebleeding (which carries ~70% mortality).
- **Nimodipine 60 mg orally every 4 hours for 21 days** (improves outcomes by reducing delayed cerebral ischaemia; reduce dose or split if hypotension).
- **Hydrocephalus:** acute (hours) → external ventricular drain.
- **Delayed cerebral ischaemia (DCI) from vasospasm:** days 3–14; monitor neurologically and with transcranial Doppler/CT angiography; maintain euvolaemia, avoid hypotension; endovascular rescue (intra-arterial vasodilators, angioplasty).
- **Hyponatraemia** (cerebral salt wasting vs SIADH): avoid fluid restriction; use isotonic saline ± fludrocortisone.
- **Seizures** (treat; no routine prophylaxis), **cardiac complications** (stress cardiomyopathy, arrhythmias, troponin elevation), **neurogenic pulmonary oedema,** **hyperglycaemia,** **fever.**
- **Grading:** Hunt–Hess, WFNS, Fisher/modified Fisher scales; prognosis relates to the initial neurological grade and age.
- **Screening** of first-degree relatives in selected familial cases.

### 41.6 Cerebral venous thrombosis (CVT), dissection, and other vascular syndromes

#### Cerebral venous thrombosis

- **Presentation:** headache (often progressive or thunderclap), **seizures** (focal or generalised, in 40%), focal deficits, papilloedema, altered consciousness; **risk factors:** pregnancy and puerperium, oestrogen-containing contraceptives, thrombophilia, dehydration, infection (ear, sinus, face), malignancy, inflammatory disease, obesity, JAK2 mutations, COVID-19 vaccine–induced thrombotic thrombocytopenia (VITT, rare).
- **Diagnosis:** CT venography or **MR venography;** CT may show hyperdense sinus ("cord sign"), venous infarcts (haemorrhagic, not corresponding to arterial territories), or be normal.
- **Treatment:** **therapeutic anticoagulation** (LMWH or unfractionated heparin) **even in the presence of haemorrhagic infarction,** then oral anticoagulation for 3–12 months; treat seizures, raised ICP (acetazolamide, lumbar puncture, decompressive surgery); endovascular thrombectomy in selected severe cases; platelets and heparin-induced thrombocytopenia evaluation in VITT (use non-heparin anticoagulant and IVIG).

#### Cervical artery dissection

- Neck pain, headache, **Horner syndrome (partial: ptosis and miosis),** pulsatile tinnitus, cranial nerve palsies; followed by TIA/stroke; risk factors: minor trauma, connective tissue disorders, hypertension; **CT or MR angiography.** **Antithrombotic therapy** (antiplatelet or anticoagulation for 3–6 months) and stroke prevention; thrombolysis is not contraindicated in extracranial dissection.

#### Reversible cerebral vasoconstriction syndrome (RCVS) and PRES

- **RCVS:** recurrent thunderclap headaches, triggered by exertion, sex, SSRIs, triptans, cannabis, postpartum; segmental vasoconstriction ("string of beads") resolves in 12 weeks; treat triggers, calcium channel blockers; avoid steroids.
- **PRES (posterior reversible encephalopathy syndrome):** headache, visual disturbance, seizures, confusion; severe hypertension, eclampsia, renal failure, immunosuppressants (ciclosporin, tacrolimus), chemotherapy; MRI shows vasogenic oedema in the parieto-occipital regions; treat BP, seizures, cause.

# Part B: CNS Infections

### 41.7 Bacterial meningitis

**Bacterial meningitis is a medical emergency;** mortality is 10–30% and survivors may have deafness, cognitive impairment, epilepsy, and motor deficits. **Delay in antibiotics is associated with worse outcomes.**

#### Organisms (by age and risk)

| Group | Common pathogens |
|---|---|
| **Neonates (<1 month)** | Group B streptococcus, *E. coli,* *Listeria monocytogenes* |
| **Infants and children** | *Neisseria meningitidis,* *Streptococcus pneumoniae,* *Haemophilus influenzae* type b (now rare with vaccination) |
| **Adolescents and young adults** | *N. meningitidis,* *S. pneumoniae* |
| **Adults** | *S. pneumoniae* (most common), *N. meningitidis* |
| **Older adults (>50), pregnancy, immunosuppressed, alcohol misuse** | ***Listeria monocytogenes,*** *S. pneumoniae,* Gram-negative bacilli |
| **Post-neurosurgery, head trauma, CSF shunt** | Staphylococci, Gram-negative bacilli, *Pseudomonas* |
| **Tuberculous meningitis** | *Mycobacterium tuberculosis* (subacute, cranial nerve palsies, basal enhancement) |

#### Clinical features

- **Classic triad: fever, neck stiffness, altered mental status** (present together in only about 40–50% of adults); **headache** (almost universal), photophobia, vomiting, **seizures** (20–30%), focal deficits, cranial nerve palsies, reduced consciousness, **petechial or purpuric rash** (meningococcal disease in about half; may be absent early); shock and DIC (meningococcaemia).
- **Kernig and Brudzinski signs** have **poor sensitivity** (about 5–10% in some studies); jolt accentuation of headache is also limited. **Their absence does not exclude meningitis.**
- **Infants:** non-specific: fever, irritability, poor feeding, vomiting, **bulging fontanelle,** high-pitched cry, lethargy, hypotonia, seizures, hypothermia; neck stiffness often absent.
- **Older adults and immunosuppressed:** fever and meningism may be absent; confusion may dominate.

#### Immediate management

1. **ABCDE; treat shock** (fluids, vasopressors), hypoxia, seizures, hypoglycaemia.
2. **Take blood cultures (and glucose, lactate, FBC, CRP, coagulation, U&E, PCR for meningococcus/pneumococcus)** **but do not delay antibiotics.**
3. **Give IV antibiotics immediately** (**within 1 hour,** ideally within 30 minutes of recognition; in the community, **IM/IV benzylpenicillin** or ceftriaxone if meningococcal disease is suspected and transfer will be delayed):

| Group | Empirical regimen (follow local guidelines) |
|---|---|
| **Adults <50 years** | **Ceftriaxone 2 g IV every 12 hours** (or cefotaxime 2 g every 4–6 hours) **+ vancomycin** where pneumococcal resistance is a concern |
| **Adults ≥50, pregnancy, immunosuppression, alcohol misuse** | **Add amoxicillin 2 g IV every 4 hours (or ampicillin) for *Listeria*** |
| **Children** | Ceftriaxone ± vancomycin (dose by weight) |
| **Neonates** | **Cefotaxime + amoxicillin (± gentamicin)** |
| **Post-neurosurgical/shunt** | Vancomycin + meropenem or ceftazidime |
| **Penicillin-allergic (severe)** | Chloramphenicol or meropenem/aztreonam options; specialist advice |

4. **Dexamethasone 10 mg IV every 6 hours for 4 days,** ideally **before or with the first dose of antibiotics** (reduces death and neurological sequelae in pneumococcal meningitis; do not start >12 hours after antibiotics; stop if meningococcal or another aetiology is confirmed, depending on local protocol).
5. **Neuroimaging before lumbar puncture only if there are contraindications to immediate LP:** **GCS <10–12 or falling, focal neurological deficit, new-onset seizure (recent), papilloedema, severe immunocompromise, signs of raised ICP, or known mass lesion/coagulopathy,** and **never delay antibiotics.** CT does not rule out raised ICP risk fully; clinical judgement is key.
6. **Lumbar puncture (when safe)** and **CSF analysis** (see below).
7. **Public health:** notify; **chemoprophylaxis** for close contacts of meningococcal disease (ciprofloxacin 500 mg single dose, rifampicin, or ceftriaxone), vaccination.
8. **ICU** for shock, GCS <12, seizures, or respiratory compromise.
9. **Supportive:** cautious fluids (avoid overload: cerebral oedema), monitor sodium (SIADH), treat seizures, pain, fever, manage raised ICP, check hearing before discharge (audiology).

#### CSF interpretation

| | Normal | **Bacterial** | **Viral** | **Tuberculous** | **Fungal (cryptococcal)** |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Opening pressure (cmH₂O)** | 10–20 (up to 25) | **Raised** (>30) | Normal/mildly raised | Raised | **Very high** |
| **Appearance** | Clear | Turbid | Clear | Clear/cobweb | Clear |
| **WBC (cells/µL)** | 0–5 | **100–10,000** (neutrophils) | 10–1,000 (lymphocytes; early neutrophils) | 10–500 (lymphocytes) | 0–200 (lymphocytes) |
| **Protein (g/L)** | 0.15–0.45 | **>1.0** | 0.5–1.0 | **1–5** | 0.5–2 |
| **Glucose (CSF:plasma ratio)** | >0.6 | **<0.4** (very low) | Normal (>0.6) | **<0.5 (low)** | Low to normal |
| **Gram stain / other** | – | **Positive in 60–90%** | Negative; PCR positive (enteroviruses, HSV, VZV) | AFB smear (low sensitivity), PCR (Xpert), culture | India ink, **cryptococcal antigen** |

Always send **paired serum glucose,** CSF culture and multiplex PCR panel; **CSF lactate** (>3.5–4 mmol/L supports bacterial); **procalcitonin** may help distinguish. **Partially treated** bacterial meningitis can produce atypical CSF findings.

#### Complications

Septic shock and DIC (meningococcal), cerebral oedema and herniation, hydrocephalus, subdural effusion/empyema, cerebral infarction/venous thrombosis, seizures, **sensorineural deafness (up to 10–30% in pneumococcal),** cranial nerve palsies, cognitive and behavioural sequelae, SIADH, arthritis. **Prevention:** vaccination (Hib, pneumococcal, meningococcal ACWY and B), chemoprophylaxis.

### 41.8 Viral meningitis and encephalitis

- **Viral (aseptic) meningitis:** enteroviruses (most common), HSV-2, VZV, mumps; headache, fever, meningism, normal consciousness, self-limited; **LP supports diagnosis (lymphocytic pleocytosis, normal glucose, PCR);** supportive treatment; consider antibiotics until bacterial meningitis is excluded.
- **Encephalitis:** brain parenchymal inflammation: **fever, headache, altered mental state (confusion, personality change, drowsiness), seizures, focal deficits,** movement disorders.
  - **HSV encephalitis** (most common treatable sporadic cause): temporal lobe features (aphasia, behaviour change, memory loss, seizures); **start aciclovir 10 mg/kg IV every 8 hours immediately** and continue for 14–21 days (hydrate; monitor renal function) **until HSV PCR is negative twice;** MRI (temporal lobe T2/FLAIR hyperintensity), EEG (periodic lateralising epileptiform discharges), CSF (lymphocytes, red cells, raised protein, HSV PCR; may be negative in the first 72 hours: repeat).
  - **Other causes:** VZV, enteroviruses, arboviruses (West Nile, Japanese encephalitis, tick-borne encephalitis), rabies, HIV, **autoimmune encephalitis** (anti-NMDA receptor: psychiatric symptoms, seizures, dyskinesia, autonomic instability; anti-LGI1, anti-GABA-B; immunotherapy: steroids, IVIG, plasma exchange, rituximab; tumour search), Lyme neuroborreliosis, syphilis, TB, listeriosis, toxoplasmosis (immunosuppressed), PML, prion disease.
  - **Investigation:** MRI, EEG, LP (PCR panel, autoantibodies), blood cultures, HIV, serology; travel and exposure history.

### 41.9 Brain abscess and subdural empyema

- **Brain abscess:** headache (most common), fever (only about half), focal deficits, seizures, raised ICP signs; sources: **direct spread** (otitis media, mastoiditis, sinusitis, dental infection), **haematogenous** (endocarditis, lung abscess, cyanotic congenital heart disease, IV drug use), **trauma or neurosurgery.** Organisms: streptococci (including *S. milleri* group), anaerobes, *Staphylococcus aureus,* Enterobacteriaceae; in immunosuppressed: *Toxoplasma,* *Nocardia,* fungi, TB.
- **Diagnosis:** **CT or MRI with contrast** (ring-enhancing lesion with restricted diffusion on DWI), blood cultures, echocardiography, **avoid LP** (risk of herniation).
- **Treatment:** **neurosurgical aspiration/drainage** (for lesions >2.5 cm, with mass effect or unknown organism), **prolonged IV antibiotics (6–8 weeks;** ceftriaxone + metronidazole ± vancomycin), treat the source, antiseizure drugs, steroids only for significant oedema.
- **Subdural empyema:** rapidly progressive (sinusitis in adolescents); fever, headache, focal signs, seizures; **neurosurgical emergency.**

# Part C: Raised Intracranial Pressure and Trauma

### 41.10 Raised intracranial pressure (ICP)

#### Features

**Headache** (worse on waking, coughing, straining, lying flat), **vomiting** (often projectile, without nausea), **papilloedema** (not always present early or in acute presentations), **sixth nerve palsy (false localising sign),** blurred vision, drowsiness, confusion, seizures; **Cushing triad** (hypertension, bradycardia, irregular respiration) is **late;** **herniation syndromes** (Chapter 33).

#### Causes

Mass lesions (tumour, haematoma, abscess), cerebral oedema (infarct, trauma, hepatic encephalopathy, hyponatraemia), hydrocephalus (obstructive, communicating), venous outflow obstruction (CVT), idiopathic intracranial hypertension, meningitis/encephalitis, status epilepticus, hypercapnia, severe hypertension (PRES).

#### Management

1. **ABCDE;** secure airway if GCS ≤8; **avoid hypoxia, hypotension, hypercapnia.**
2. **Head up 30°,** neck midline (avoid tight neck ties), minimise venous obstruction.
3. **Ventilate to normocapnia (PaCO₂ 4.5–5.0 kPa / 35–38 mmHg);** **brief hyperventilation (PaCO₂ 4.0–4.5 kPa)** only as a temporising measure for impending herniation.
4. **Osmotherapy:** **3% hypertonic saline** 2–5 mL/kg over 10–20 minutes (or 23.4% 30 mL via central line) **or mannitol 0.25–1 g/kg IV** (monitor osmolality, volume status, sodium; avoid hypovolaemia).
5. **Treat seizures;** correct hyponatraemia (keep sodium 140–150 in raised ICP); avoid hypotonic fluids (use isotonic saline).
6. **Sedation and analgesia;** **normothermia;** **control hypertension cautiously** (avoid precipitous drops).
7. **Dexamethasone** (10 mg IV then 4 mg every 6 hours) for **vasogenic oedema from brain tumour or abscess;** **no benefit and possible harm in traumatic brain injury** (CRASH trial) and ischaemic or haemorrhagic stroke.
8. **Neurosurgical intervention:** evacuation of mass lesions, **external ventricular drainage (EVD)** for hydrocephalus, **decompressive craniectomy** for refractory ICP, ICP monitoring.
9. **Second-tier therapies (ICU):** barbiturate coma, hypothermia, high-dose osmotherapy.
10. **Lumbar puncture is contraindicated** in suspected mass lesion or obstructive hydrocephalus.

### 41.11 Traumatic brain injury (TBI): essentials

- **Classification (GCS):** **mild (13–15), moderate (9–12), severe (≤8).** **Prevent secondary brain injury** (hypoxia, hypotension, hypo- or hypercapnia, hyperthermia, hypoglycaemia, seizures, raised ICP).
- **CT head criteria (adults)** (e.g. NICE/Canadian CT Head Rule): GCS <13 initially, or <15 at 2 hours; suspected open or depressed skull fracture; **signs of basal skull fracture** (haemotympanum, "panda eyes," Battle sign, CSF leak); post-traumatic seizure; focal neurological deficit; **vomiting >1 episode;** **age ≥65** (and amnesia >30 minutes, dangerous mechanism); **anticoagulation or bleeding disorder** with any head injury; amnesia >30 minutes before impact.
- **Patterns:**
  - **Extradural (epidural) haematoma:** lens-shaped, **middle meningeal artery,** temporal bone fracture, **lucid interval** then rapid deterioration; **neurosurgical emergency.**
  - **Acute subdural haematoma:** crescent-shaped, high-energy trauma, bridging vein tear, underlying brain injury, poor prognosis; **chronic subdural:** older adults, minor or forgotten trauma, anticoagulants, weeks of headache, confusion, falls, hemiparesis.
  - **Subarachnoid haemorrhage (traumatic), contusions, diffuse axonal injury (DAI)** (severe coma with a normal CT).
- **Management:** ABCDE with **cervical spine protection;** treat hypoxia and hypotension; **tranexamic acid (1 g within 3 hours of injury)** for mild-to-moderate TBI (CRASH-3); **reverse anticoagulation;** **neurosurgery** for surgical lesions; **ICP monitoring and ICU** for severe TBI (GCS ≤8); avoid steroids; seizure prophylaxis for 7 days in severe TBI (levetiracetam); discharge advice for mild injury (observer, return precautions: worsening headache, vomiting, drowsiness, confusion, weakness, seizure, fluid from ear or nose), concussion management (graded return to activity).
- **Non-accidental head injury** in infants (retinal haemorrhages, subdural haematomas, rib fractures): safeguarding.

# Part D: Acute Neuromuscular and Spinal Emergencies

### 41.12 Guillain–Barré syndrome (GBS)

- **Acute immune-mediated polyradiculoneuropathy:** often 1–3 weeks after infection (*Campylobacter jejuni,* CMV, EBV, Zika, COVID-19, vaccination rarely). **Presentation:** ascending symmetrical weakness and paraesthesia over days (nadir by 4 weeks), **areflexia or hyporeflexia,** back or limb pain, facial weakness (bilateral), bulbar weakness, **autonomic dysfunction** (labile BP, arrhythmia, ileus, urinary retention), respiratory failure (20–30%).
- **Diagnosis:** clinical; **CSF: raised protein with normal cell count (albuminocytological dissociation)** (may be normal in the first week); **nerve conduction studies** (demyelinating or axonal patterns); exclude cord compression (MRI spine), myasthenia, botulism, tick paralysis, acute intermittent porphyria, critical illness neuropathy, hypokalaemia, poliomyelitis.
- **Management:** **admit to HDU/ICU if rapid progression;** **monitor vital capacity (FVC) 4-hourly, bulbar function, ECG, BP;** **IVIG 0.4 g/kg/day for 5 days** or **plasma exchange** (5 exchanges) within 2–4 weeks of onset (equal efficacy; do not combine); steroids are ineffective; **intubate early** for FVC <15–20 mL/kg or falling >30%, bulbar failure, ineffective cough (Chapter 40); **DVT prophylaxis,** pain management (gabapentinoids), nutrition, pressure area care, rehabilitation. **Prognosis:** most recover over months; about 10% have severe residual disability; mortality 3–7%.

### 41.13 Myasthenic crisis

- **Neuromuscular junction disease (anti-AChR or anti-MuSK antibodies)** with fluctuating, fatigable weakness (ptosis, diplopia, bulbar, limb, respiratory). **Crisis:** respiratory or bulbar failure requiring ventilatory support; precipitants: infection, surgery, drugs (aminoglycosides, macrolides, fluoroquinolones, magnesium, beta-blockers, neuromuscular blockers), steroid initiation, pregnancy.
- **Management:** ICU, **monitor FVC and negative inspiratory force,** early intubation or NIV, **IVIG or plasma exchange,** withhold anticholinesterases during crisis (secretions), treat triggers, avoid precipitating drugs, then immunosuppression and thymectomy planning.
- **Cholinergic crisis** is rare; **Lambert–Eaton syndrome** (paraneoplastic) and **botulism** (descending paralysis, dilated pupils, dry mouth; antitoxin) are differentials.

### 41.14 Spinal cord compression and acute myelopathy

(See Chapter 29.) **Same-day MRI** of the whole spine for suspected cord compression (metastasis, abscess, haematoma, disc, trauma): high-dose dexamethasone (10 mg IV then 16 mg/day for malignant compression), urgent oncology/neurosurgery, decompression within hours to days. **Acute transverse myelitis** (viral, MS, neuromyelitis optica, post-infectious): MRI, LP, high-dose methylprednisolone, plasma exchange in refractory cases. **Spinal cord infarction** (anterior spinal artery: sudden back pain, flaccid paraplegia, loss of pain and temperature with preserved dorsal columns). **Cauda equina syndrome** (Chapter 29).

### 41.15 Other time-critical neurological conditions

- **Status epilepticus** (Chapter 33).
- **Wernicke encephalopathy:** IV thiamine (Chapter 33).
- **Hypoglycaemia, hyponatraemia, hepatic encephalopathy, hypercapnia:** metabolic (Chapter 33, 43).
- **Acute angle-closure glaucoma, optic neuritis, giant cell arteritis (visual loss):** urgent ophthalmology and steroids (Chapters 24, 55).
- **Acute disseminated encephalomyelitis, multiple sclerosis relapse:** high-dose methylprednisolone.
- **Neuroleptic malignant syndrome, serotonin syndrome:** stop drugs, cooling, benzodiazepines, dantrolene or cyproheptadine (Chapter 21).
- **Acute hydrocephalus:** headache, vomiting, drowsiness, impaired upgaze; EVD.
- **Locked-in syndrome:** basilar artery occlusion.
- **Brain death evaluation** (Chapter 33).

### 41.16 Worked cases

#### Case 1: Acute ischaemic stroke: thrombolysis and thrombectomy

*A 68-year-old man with hypertension and AF (not anticoagulated) is found at 10:30 with right arm and leg weakness, aphasia and left gaze deviation; last seen well at 09:45. NIHSS 18. Glucose 6.5; BP 172/96. CT head: no haemorrhage, early hypodensity in the left MCA (ASPECTS 9). CT angiography: left M1 occlusion.*

**Reasoning.** **Large vessel occlusion** stroke within the window, no contraindications.

**Action.** Stroke code; **IV thrombolysis** (tenecteplase 0.25 mg/kg or alteplase 0.9 mg/kg) at about 11:05 (door-to-needle <35 minutes) while **transferring immediately for mechanical thrombectomy;** BP <185/110 before and <180/105 after; neurological observations; repeat CT at 24 hours; start antithrombotics after; AF work-up and anticoagulation timing; swallow screen; rehabilitation.

#### Case 2: Intracerebral haemorrhage on warfarin

*A 79-year-old woman on warfarin for AF has sudden headache, vomiting, right hemiparesis and drowsiness (GCS 11). BP 205/110. INR 3.8. CT: left basal ganglia haemorrhage (30 mL) with intraventricular extension.*

**Reasoning.** **Warfarin-associated ICH.**

**Action.** **PCC plus IV vitamin K** immediately, BP lowering to systolic ~140 (labetalol or nicardipine), airway assessment, neurosurgical opinion (EVD if hydrocephalus), seizure management, ICU/stroke unit, discuss prognosis and goals of care without premature withdrawal; reassess anticoagulation long-term.

#### Case 3: TIA with carotid stenosis

*A 61-year-old smoker has 10 minutes of right arm weakness and speech disturbance, fully resolved. BP 158/90, AF absent. MRI DWI: small left frontal diffusion restriction (so this is a minor stroke by tissue definition). CT angiography: 80% left internal carotid stenosis.*

**Reasoning.** **High-risk minor stroke/TIA** with symptomatic carotid stenosis.

**Action.** **Aspirin 300 mg (plus clopidogrel loading for 21 days),** high-intensity statin, BP management, **carotid endarterectomy within 2 weeks** (ideally days), smoking cessation, urgent stroke clinic.

#### Case 4: Bacterial meningitis

*A 19-year-old university student has 12 hours of headache, fever 39.4 °C, photophobia, vomiting, neck stiffness, and is drowsy. A few petechiae are present on the legs. BP 100/55, HR 128.*

**Reasoning.** **Meningococcal meningitis/septicaemia.**

**Action.** **Blood cultures, then immediately ceftriaxone 2 g IV (do not wait for LP or CT);** dexamethasone 10 mg IV; fluids; ICU liaison; PCR on blood and CSF when safe; notify public health; chemoprophylaxis for contacts; reassess for shock and DIC; hearing screen.

#### Case 5: Epidural haematoma

*A 17-year-old is hit on the head by a cricket ball, loses consciousness briefly, recovers, and then becomes progressively drowsy 3 hours later with a fixed dilated left pupil and right hemiparesis. CT: biconvex hyperdense left temporal extradural haematoma with midline shift.*

**Reasoning.** **Extradural haematoma** with lucid interval and uncal herniation.

**Action.** **Emergency neurosurgical evacuation,** airway protection and ventilation, osmotherapy (mannitol or hypertonic saline), head up, transfer to a neurosurgical centre; excellent prognosis if treated early.

#### Case 6: Cerebral venous thrombosis

*A 28-year-old woman, 10 days postpartum, has progressive headache, then a focal seizure and left hand weakness. CT head: normal. MR venogram: superior sagittal sinus thrombosis with a small right parietal haemorrhagic infarct.*

**Reasoning.** **Cerebral venous thrombosis** in the puerperium.

**Action.** **Therapeutic LMWH** (despite haemorrhage), antiseizure medication, manage raised ICP, anticoagulate for 3–12 months, thrombophilia screening, contraception counselling.

#### Case 7: Guillain–Barré syndrome

*A 52-year-old man, 10 days after a diarrhoeal illness, has bilateral leg weakness spreading to his arms over 4 days, areflexia, facial weakness, and difficulty swallowing. FVC 1.7 L (from 3.2 L).*

**Reasoning.** **GBS with respiratory involvement.**

**Action.** ICU, **IVIG 0.4 g/kg/day for 5 days,** intubate if FVC falls or bulbar failure, autonomic monitoring, DVT prophylaxis, pain control, rehabilitation (Chapter 40).

#### Case 8: Cerebellar infarct

*A 65-year-old man with hypertension has sudden vertigo, vomiting, headache, and inability to walk. He is alert at presentation. Two days later he becomes drowsy with a slow pulse. CT: large right cerebellar infarct with fourth ventricle compression and hydrocephalus.*

**Reasoning.** **Cerebellar infarct with swelling, hydrocephalus and brainstem compression** (deterioration at 48–96 hours is typical).

**Action.** **Urgent neurosurgery:** EVD and **suboccipital decompression;** ICU; avoid sedation delays; monitor GCS in all patients with large cerebellar infarcts.

### 41.17 Common pitfalls

- **Delaying imaging or reperfusion decisions** for unnecessary tests; **not recording onset time.**
- **Missing hypoglycaemia** as a mimic or as a cause.
- **Dismissing posterior circulation stroke** (isolated vertigo, normal initial CT).
- **Lowering BP aggressively in ischaemic stroke** without indication; **under-treating BP in ICH.**
- **Giving antithrombotics before CT** or within 24 hours of thrombolysis; platelet transfusion in antiplatelet-associated ICH.
- **Not reversing anticoagulation promptly** in ICH.
- **Not treating TIA as an emergency** (delayed outpatient review).
- **Waiting for LP or CT before giving antibiotics** in suspected meningitis; omitting dexamethasone and *Listeria* cover.
- **Relying on Kernig/Brudzinski** to exclude meningitis.
- **Stopping aciclovir** before PCR results (or failing to repeat PCR).
- **Performing LP** in raised ICP or mass lesion.
- **Giving steroids** for traumatic brain injury or stroke oedema.
- **Missing cerebellar haemorrhage or infarct** and its swelling.
- **Relying on gases in GBS or myasthenia;** delaying ventilatory support.
- **Withdrawing care early** in ICH based on prognostic scores alone.
- **Missing venous thrombosis** in a young woman with headache and seizures; withholding anticoagulation due to haemorrhage.

### 41.18 Key points

- Recognise stroke with FAST/BE-FAST and NIHSS; check glucose; **time of onset is critical;** CT first, then CTA/perfusion.
- Ischaemic stroke: **IV thrombolysis within 4.5 hours** (extended window by imaging), **thrombectomy for LVO up to 24 hours in selected patients,** aspirin after exclusion of haemorrhage (24 hours after lysis), avoid lowering BP unless >220/120 (or >185/110 pre-lysis), stroke unit care, carotid surgery within 2 weeks.
- TIA = stroke emergency: urgent assessment within 24 hours, DAPT for 21 days in high-risk, imaging and carotid evaluation.
- ICH: BP control (systolic ~130–150), immediate reversal of anticoagulants, neurosurgical referral for cerebellar, hydrocephalus and selected lobar cases.
- SAH: CT, secure aneurysm within 24 hours, nimodipine, manage hydrocephalus and vasospasm.
- Bacterial meningitis: **antibiotics immediately** (ceftriaxone ± amoxicillin ± vancomycin) with dexamethasone; LP only if safe; HSV encephalitis: **aciclovir immediately.**
- Raised ICP: head up, normocapnia, osmotherapy, treat cause, neurosurgery; no LP; steroids only for tumour/abscess oedema.
- GBS and myasthenic crisis: follow FVC, intubate early, IVIG or plasma exchange.
- TBI: CT criteria, anticoagulant reversal, TXA within 3 hours, neurosurgery for extradural/subdural lesions.

### 41.19 Self-assessment

1. State the time windows and key eligibility checks for IV thrombolysis and mechanical thrombectomy in acute ischaemic stroke.
   *Answer: Thrombolysis within 4.5 hours of onset (or extended windows with advanced imaging), no haemorrhage on CT, BP <185/110, glucose >2.7 mmol/L, no recent major bleeding/surgery, INR <1.7, platelets >100, no recent DOAC; thrombectomy for LVO (ICA/M1/M2/basilar) within 6 hours and up to 24 hours in selected patients by imaging.*

2. How is blood pressure managed in ischaemic stroke versus intracerebral haemorrhage?
   *Answer: Ischaemic: avoid lowering unless >220/120 (or >185/110 before thrombolysis, <180/105 after); ICH: lower early and intensively to a systolic target of about 130–150 mmHg.*

3. List the immediate steps for suspected bacterial meningitis.
   *Answer: ABCDE, blood cultures, IV ceftriaxone (± amoxicillin, vancomycin) immediately, dexamethasone, fluids/shock management, CT only if contraindication to LP, LP when safe, ICU, notification and prophylaxis.*

4. Interpret CSF: neutrophils 3,000/µL, protein 2.1 g/L, glucose 0.8 mmol/L (serum 6.0), Gram stain Gram-positive diplococci.
   *Answer: Bacterial (pneumococcal) meningitis.*

5. What are the red flags for raised ICP and the first-line measures?
   *Answer: Morning headache, vomiting, papilloedema, drowsiness, sixth nerve palsy, Cushing triad; head up 30°, normocapnia, hypertonic saline or mannitol, treat cause, neurosurgery, avoid LP.*

6. When should a patient with Guillain–Barré syndrome be intubated?
   *Answer: FVC <15–20 mL/kg or falling >30%, bulbar failure, ineffective cough, hypercapnia or exhaustion; ideally before respiratory arrest.*
