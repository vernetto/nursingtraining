## Chapter 42. Acute Abdomen, Trauma and Hemorrhage

### Learning objectives

After studying this chapter you should be able to:

1. Recognise the patient with a surgical acute abdomen (peritonitis, perforation, obstruction, ischaemia, intra-abdominal haemorrhage) and initiate resuscitation, analgesia, antibiotics, imaging and early surgical referral.
2. Perform the primary survey of the trauma patient in the **xABCDE** sequence, identify and treat immediately life-threatening injuries, and use FAST and imaging appropriately.
3. Classify haemorrhage, apply damage-control resuscitation (haemorrhage control, balanced blood products, tranexamic acid, calcium, avoidance of the "lethal triad"), and use massive transfusion protocols.
4. Recognise and manage the major thoracic, abdominal, pelvic, limb, spinal and burn injuries.
5. Diagnose abdominal and limb compartment syndromes, and know when to decompress.
6. Manage obstetric haemorrhage and bleeding from ruptured aneurysm or ectopic pregnancy as emergencies.
7. Adapt the approach to children, pregnant women, and older adults, and plan secondary and tertiary surveys.

---

### 42.1 Principles

Surgical and traumatic emergencies share a common logic: **life-threatening problems are found and treated in order of how quickly they kill** (catastrophic haemorrhage, airway, breathing, circulation, disability, exposure), **bleeding is controlled definitively by surgical or radiological means** (resuscitation only buys time), **infection is controlled at source,** and **delay in definitive treatment is the commonest cause of preventable death.**

Three concepts recur:

- **The "golden hour":** the first hour after serious injury or onset of a surgical catastrophe, in which rapid assessment and treatment have the greatest effect on survival. (The exact duration is less important than the principle: speed with precision.)
- **Damage-control resuscitation and surgery:** in unstable patients with severe haemorrhage, do the minimum operation to control bleeding and contamination, correct physiology in ICU (temperature, acidosis, coagulopathy), and return for definitive repair.
- **Team approach:** a designated team leader, clear roles, closed-loop communication, and early involvement of surgery, anaesthesia, radiology, ICU, and the blood bank.

# Part A: The Acute Abdomen

### 42.2 Recognition and initial management

The **acute abdomen** is acute abdominal pain with signs suggesting an intra-abdominal condition that may require urgent surgery. (The detailed differential and history are in Chapter 25; here the emphasis is on the **sick patient.**)

#### Features that indicate a surgical emergency

- **Peritonitis:** rigid ("board-like") abdomen, guarding, rebound or percussion tenderness, absent bowel sounds, patient lying still, pain on coughing or jarring the bed.
- **Shock or sepsis:** tachycardia, hypotension, cool mottled skin, fever or hypothermia, raised lactate, confusion, oliguria.
- **Obstruction with strangulation:** severe constant pain after initial colic, tender irreducible hernia, fever, tachycardia, raised lactate.
- **Pain out of proportion to signs:** acute mesenteric ischaemia.
- **Pulsatile mass and collapse:** ruptured AAA.
- **Free intraperitoneal air, fluid or blood** on imaging.
- **Failure to improve or deterioration** on observation.
- **Peritonism in an immunosuppressed, older or very young patient** (signs may be muted).

#### First steps (in parallel)

1. **ABCDE** and **early senior surgical review.**
2. **Oxygen, two large-bore IV cannulae, bloods** (FBC, U&E, creatinine, LFTs, **lipase,** CRP, glucose, **lactate** and blood gas, coagulation, **group and cross-match,** blood cultures, troponin, β-hCG in women of reproductive age), **ECG.**
3. **IV fluid resuscitation** (balanced crystalloid in boluses with reassessment); **blood products** early if haemorrhage.
4. **Analgesia:** titrated IV opioid (morphine 2–5 mg increments) ± paracetamol; **analgesia does not mask diagnosis or impair surgical assessment.**
5. **Nil by mouth,** **antiemetic,** **nasogastric tube** for vomiting, obstruction, or ileus (decompression).
6. **IV antibiotics** (broad-spectrum with Gram-negative and anaerobic cover) **within the first hour** for peritonitis, perforation, cholangitis, abscess, sepsis; give after blood cultures but without delaying.
7. **Urinary catheter** to monitor urine output.
8. **Imaging** (below), **but never delay surgery** in the unstable patient with obvious peritonitis or a ruptured aneurysm.
9. **Pre-operative optimisation:** correct electrolytes, glucose, anticoagulation reversal (as per Chapter 35), VTE prophylaxis, steroid cover for patients on long-term steroids, risk assessment (e.g. NELA score), early discussion of goals of care with the patient and family in frail or high-risk patients.

#### Imaging

| Modality | Use |
|---|---|
| **Erect chest X-ray** | Free air under the diaphragm (perforation); basal pneumonia; pleural effusion |
| **Abdominal X-ray** | Obstruction (dilated loops, air–fluid levels), volvulus, toxic megacolon; low sensitivity; not routinely recommended |
| **Ultrasound (including FAST)** | Free fluid (blood), AAA, biliary disease, gynaecological causes, appendicitis in children and pregnancy |
| **CT abdomen/pelvis with IV contrast** | **Best for most acute abdominal conditions in stable adults:** perforation, abscess, obstruction (level and cause), ischaemia (CT angiography), pancreatitis and complications, appendicitis, diverticulitis, haemorrhage, aortic pathology; oral contrast rarely needed |
| **MRI** | Pregnancy, selected biliary and pelvic problems |
| **Diagnostic laparoscopy/laparotomy** | When diagnosis remains uncertain in a deteriorating patient |

### 42.3 Specific surgical emergencies

#### Perforated viscus and generalised peritonitis

- **Causes:** perforated **peptic ulcer** (sudden severe epigastric pain, board-like rigidity, NSAIDs, steroids), **diverticular perforation** (purulent or faecal peritonitis; Hinchey III–IV), **perforated appendicitis,** **colorectal cancer,** **ischaemic bowel,** **toxic megacolon,** **perforated gallbladder,** **anastomotic leak** (post-operative), trauma, foreign bodies, **iatrogenic perforation** (endoscopy).
- **Features:** abrupt severe pain, **lying still,** shallow breathing, tachycardia, fever, rigidity, free air on erect chest X-ray or CT; septic shock.
- **Management:** resuscitation, broad-spectrum IV antibiotics (e.g. piperacillin-tazobactam; carbapenem if risk of resistance), analgesia, **emergency laparotomy or laparoscopy** (closure or omental patch for perforated ulcer; resection ± stoma for colonic perforation; drainage; peritoneal lavage), ICU post-operatively. Mortality rises with delay and with older age, shock, and faecal peritonitis. **Hinchey classification of diverticulitis:** I (paracolic abscess), II (pelvic/distant abscess), III (purulent peritonitis), IV (faecal peritonitis); abscess >4–5 cm: percutaneous drainage; III–IV: surgery (Hartmann's procedure or primary anastomosis ± diversion).

#### Intestinal obstruction

- **Small bowel obstruction (SBO):** adhesions (most common, previous surgery), hernias, malignancy, Crohn's, volvulus, intussusception, gallstone ileus, foreign body. **Features:** colicky pain, vomiting (bilious or faeculent), distension, absolute constipation, tinkling bowel sounds; **look at all hernial orifices.**
- **Strangulation/ischaemia:** continuous severe pain, fever, tachycardia, peritonism, raised lactate, leucocytosis, bowel wall thickening or non-enhancement, "closed loop" on CT: **emergency surgery.**
- **Management of uncomplicated adhesive SBO:** "drip and suck": IV fluids and electrolytes, **nasogastric decompression,** nil by mouth, analgesia, antiemetics, **water-soluble contrast (Gastrografin) study** (diagnostic and therapeutic), serial examination, surgery if no resolution in 48–72 hours or if complications.
- **Large bowel obstruction:** colorectal cancer (most common), **sigmoid or caecal volvulus,** diverticular stricture, pseudo-obstruction (Ogilvie syndrome). **Closed-loop obstruction with competent ileocaecal valve → caecal distension (>9–10 cm) → perforation risk.** **Sigmoid volvulus** (older, institutionalised, constipated; "coffee-bean" sign on X-ray): decompression by flexible sigmoidoscopy and rectal tube, then elective resection; surgery if gangrene or failed decompression. **Caecal volvulus:** surgery. **Obstructing colon cancer:** stent as bridge or emergency resection. **Ogilvie:** treat precipitants, neostigmine, decompression.
- **Gastric outlet obstruction, gallstone ileus, intussusception (children, adults with lead point), paediatric malrotation with volvulus (bilious vomiting in infants is an emergency).**

#### Acute mesenteric ischaemia

- **Causes:** **embolic** (AF; sudden severe pain, SMA), **thrombotic** (atherosclerosis; prior postprandial pain, weight loss), **non-occlusive** (low flow in shock, cardiac failure, vasopressors), **mesenteric venous thrombosis** (hypercoagulable states, cirrhosis).
- **Clinical:** **severe, diffuse pain out of proportion to examination;** vomiting, diarrhoea or bloody stool, shock; late peritonitis.
- **Diagnosis:** **CT angiography** (urgent), raised lactate (late), leucocytosis; high suspicion in AF patients with abdominal pain.
- **Management:** resuscitation, **IV heparin** immediately, broad-spectrum antibiotics, **revascularisation** (surgical embolectomy or endovascular), **resection of non-viable bowel** (second-look laparotomy after 24–48 hours), treat shock (avoid vasoconstrictors if possible). Mortality 50–70%, driven by delay.

#### Acute pancreatitis (severe)

- Diagnosis: **two of three:** characteristic epigastric pain; **lipase (or amylase) >3 × upper limit;** characteristic imaging. **Severity:** revised Atlanta classification (mild; moderately severe: transient organ failure <48 hours or local complications; **severe: persistent organ failure >48 hours**); scores: BISAP, Glasgow-Imrie, APACHE II.
- **Management:** early **aggressive-but-careful IV fluids** (balanced crystalloid, e.g. 1.5 mL/kg/hour with reassessment; avoid overload), **analgesia,** oxygen, **early oral/enteral feeding** as tolerated, ICU for organ failure, **ERCP within 24–72 hours** if cholangitis or persistent biliary obstruction, antibiotics only for proven infection (infected necrosis), **cholecystectomy during the same admission** for mild gallstone pancreatitis, necrosectomy delayed (≥4 weeks) with step-up approach (percutaneous/endoscopic drainage first). Complications: necrosis, pseudocyst, haemorrhage, abdominal compartment syndrome, ARDS, AKI, DIC.

#### Acute cholangitis and cholecystitis

- **Cholangitis:** fever, jaundice, RUQ pain (Charcot's triad), shock and confusion (Reynolds' pentad); **antibiotics and urgent biliary drainage (ERCP; percutaneous if ERCP fails)** (Chapter 27). **Acute cholecystitis:** fever, RUQ pain, Murphy sign, leucocytosis; antibiotics, early laparoscopic cholecystectomy (within 72 hours to 7 days), percutaneous cholecystostomy in unfit patients; **gangrenous or emphysematous cholecystitis** (diabetic, older): emergency surgery.

#### Acute appendicitis, diverticulitis, hernias

- **Appendicitis:** diagnosis by clinical scoring and imaging; appendicectomy (laparoscopic) or antibiotics-first strategies in selected uncomplicated cases; perforated appendicitis with abscess: drainage and antibiotics.
- **Acute diverticulitis:** uncomplicated: antibiotics (or observation in selected patients), diet; complicated: abscess drainage, surgery for peritonitis.
- **Strangulated hernia:** emergency surgery.

#### Ruptured abdominal aortic aneurysm (AAA)

(Chapter 25.) **Triad:** abdominal/back pain, hypotension, pulsatile abdominal mass. **Management:** **massive haemorrhage protocol, permissive hypotension (systolic ~70–90 mmHg),** avoid large crystalloid volumes, **immediate transfer to theatre** (open repair or **endovascular aneurysm repair, EVAR,** if anatomy suitable and the patient is stable enough for CT); **do not delay for imaging in an unstable patient.** Mortality of ruptured AAA reaching hospital about 40–50%. **Symptomatic but intact AAA:** urgent repair within hours. **Aortic dissection:** Chapter 22.

#### Gynaecological and obstetric emergencies

- **Ruptured ectopic pregnancy,** **ovarian torsion** (severe unilateral pain, vomiting; laparoscopy to detorse), **ruptured ovarian cyst,** **pelvic inflammatory disease/tubo-ovarian abscess** (Chapter 25), **placental abruption, uterine rupture.**

#### Abdominal compartment syndrome (ACS)

- **Intra-abdominal hypertension (IAH):** sustained intra-abdominal pressure (IAP) **≥12 mmHg;** **ACS:** sustained IAP **>20 mmHg** (with or without abdominal perfusion pressure <60 mmHg) **with new organ dysfunction.**
- **Causes:** severe pancreatitis, major trauma with massive resuscitation, ruptured AAA, bowel obstruction/ileus, retroperitoneal haemorrhage, burns, sepsis, tight abdominal closure, ascites, massive fluid resuscitation (>5 L).
- **Features:** tense distended abdomen, oliguria/AKI, hypotension (reduced venous return), raised airway pressures, hypoxia, raised ICP.
- **Measurement:** **bladder pressure** (via Foley catheter, with 25 mL saline instilled, patient supine, end-expiration, zeroed at the mid-axillary line).
- **Management:** sedation, analgesia, neuromuscular blockade, NG/rectal decompression, drainage of ascites or collections, avoid positive fluid balance (consider diuresis/ultrafiltration), and **decompressive laparotomy** (temporary abdominal closure) for refractory ACS.

#### Others

- **Toxic megacolon** (ulcerative colitis, *C. difficile*): IV steroids, antibiotics, early surgical review; colectomy for failure.
- **Acute colonic ischaemia, acute limb ischaemia, necrotising enterocolitis (neonates), spontaneous bacterial peritonitis,** **perforated or incarcerated hiatal hernia,** **acute severe ulcerative colitis.**

# Part B: Major Trauma

### 42.4 Organisation of trauma care

- **Pre-hospital:** scene safety, rapid assessment, **catastrophic haemorrhage control,** minimal on-scene time, pre-alert to the trauma centre, **triage** to an appropriate trauma centre by criteria: **physiological** (GCS ≤13, systolic BP <90, respiratory rate <10 or >29), **anatomical** (penetrating wounds to head, neck, torso; flail chest; two or more proximal long-bone fractures; unstable pelvis; open or depressed skull fracture; paralysis; amputation proximal to wrist/ankle), **mechanism** (fall >6 m (adults), high-speed collision, ejection, death in same compartment, pedestrian struck), **special groups** (age >55, children, pregnancy, anticoagulants).
- **Trauma team activation** with defined roles: leader, airway, circulation, scribe, radiographer, surgeon, orthopaedics, anaesthetist/ICU.
- **Principles** (ATLS and related courses): **treat the greatest threat to life first,** the absence of a definitive diagnosis must not delay treatment, and **reassess repeatedly.**

---

### 42.5 The primary survey: xABCDE

The sequence addresses what kills fastest: **x**sanguination, **A**irway, **B**reathing, **C**irculation, **D**isability, **E**xposure/environment. Treat each life threat as it is found.

#### x: Exsanguinating (catastrophic) haemorrhage

- **Control immediately:** **direct pressure,** pressure dressing, **tourniquet** for limb haemorrhage (apply high and tight on the proximal limb; note time; convert later), **haemostatic dressings** (junctional wounds: groin, axilla), **pelvic binder** (at the level of the greater trochanters) for suspected pelvic haemorrhage, splinting of long-bone fractures.
- **Tranexamic acid** 1 g IV over 10 minutes, then 1 g over 8 hours, **within 3 hours of injury** (CRASH-2).

#### A: Airway with cervical spine protection

- **Assess:** voice, patency, secretions, blood, foreign bodies, facial/neck injury, stridor, burns.
- **Manoeuvres:** jaw thrust, suction, adjuncts; **manual in-line stabilisation** of the cervical spine (do not remove the collar if present until clinically cleared); **definitive airway** (rapid-sequence intubation with in-line stabilisation and video laryngoscopy) for GCS ≤8, airway obstruction, inhalation injury, severe facial trauma, impending airway compromise, need for ventilation, or anticipated deterioration; **surgical airway** for CICO (Chapter 40).
- **Oxygen** at high flow.

#### B: Breathing and ventilation

- **Life-threatening chest injuries to identify and treat immediately ("ATOM FC"):** **A**irway obstruction, **T**ension pneumothorax, **O**pen pneumothorax, **M**assive haemothorax, **F**lail chest, **C**ardiac tamponade.
  - **Tension pneumothorax:** needle decompression (second intercostal space midclavicular line, or fifth intercostal space anterior axillary line; use a long, wide cannula) or **finger thoracostomy,** then chest drain (**clinical diagnosis;** do not wait for X-ray).
  - **Open pneumothorax:** occlusive dressing taped on three sides, chest drain remote from the wound.
  - **Massive haemothorax** (>1,500 mL initial drainage, or >200 mL/hour over 2–4 hours, or haemodynamic compromise): large-bore chest drain (28–32 Fr), blood transfusion, **thoracotomy** (and bleeding source control); autotransfusion.
  - **Flail chest** (≥3 adjacent ribs fractured in ≥2 places): analgesia (regional: epidural, paravertebral, serratus anterior block), oxygen, ventilatory support if failing; underlying pulmonary contusion is the main cause of hypoxia; **avoid excessive crystalloid.**
  - **Cardiac tamponade:** Beck triad (hypotension, raised JVP, muffled heart sounds), often penetrating injuries; **echo/FAST;** **thoracotomy or sternotomy** (pericardiocentesis is a temporary measure).
- **Other thoracic injuries (secondary survey):** pulmonary contusion, simple pneumothorax, rib fractures, **blunt aortic injury** (widened mediastinum, deceleration injury: CT angiography, endovascular repair), **tracheobronchial injury** (persistent air leak, surgical emphysema), **blunt cardiac injury,** **diaphragmatic rupture,** **oesophageal injury.**
- **Monitoring:** SpO₂, capnography, respiratory rate, chest X-ray (portable) and **eFAST** (extended FAST: pneumothorax and haemothorax).

#### C: Circulation with haemorrhage control

- **Recognise shock early:** tachycardia, cool clammy skin, narrow pulse pressure, delayed capillary refill, low urine output, altered mental state; **hypotension is a late sign** (ATLS classes below).
- **Access:** two large-bore peripheral cannulae or **intraosseous** (adults and children); **blood tests** (FBC, coagulation, U&E, lactate, gas, group and cross-match, toxicology), **ECG.**
- **Find the source of bleeding (the five places blood is lost: "on the floor and four more"):** **external, chest, abdomen, pelvis/retroperitoneum, long bones** (femur fracture may lose 1–1.5 L).
- **Investigations in the resus bay:** **FAST** (focused assessment with sonography in trauma: pericardial, perihepatic (Morison pouch), perisplenic, and pelvic views, plus lungs in eFAST), **chest X-ray, pelvic X-ray;** **CT** in stable patients (whole-body CT in major trauma reduces mortality) after initial stabilisation.
- **Resuscitation strategy:** **damage-control resuscitation** (see 42.6).

**ATLS classes of haemorrhage (70 kg adult):**

| Class | Blood loss | Heart rate | BP | Pulse pressure | Respiratory rate | Urine output | Mental state |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **I** | <15% (<750 mL) | Normal | Normal | Normal | 14–20 | >30 mL/h | Slightly anxious |
| **II** | 15–30% (750–1,500 mL) | 100–120 | Normal | Reduced | 20–30 | 20–30 mL/h | Mildly anxious |
| **III** | 30–40% (1,500–2,000 mL) | 120–140 | **Decreased** | Reduced | 30–40 | 5–15 mL/h | Anxious, confused |
| **IV** | >40% (>2,000 mL) | >140 | **Markedly decreased** | Reduced | >35 | Negligible | Confused, lethargic |

(Older adults, athletes, pregnant women, and patients on beta-blockers or pacemakers do not follow this pattern reliably.)

#### D: Disability

- **GCS and pupils,** limb movements, signs of spinal cord injury, glucose; **avoid hypoxia and hypotension** (secondary brain injury); head injury management (Chapter 41). **Analgesia and sedation** if needed.

#### E: Exposure and environmental control

- **Fully undress** (cut clothes), **log-roll** with spinal precautions (inspect back, perineum, palpate spine, **rectal examination only when indicated**), look for all injuries, **prevent hypothermia** (warm room, blankets, warmed IV fluids, forced-air warming): **hypothermia (<35 °C) increases mortality** and worsens coagulopathy.

#### Adjuncts to the primary survey

- **ECG, SpO₂, capnography, arterial gas, lactate and base deficit,** urinary catheter (contraindicated if blood at the meatus, scrotal haematoma, high-riding prostate: perform urethrogram) and **gastric tube** (orogastric if skull base fracture is suspected), **chest and pelvic X-rays, FAST, CT.**
- **Analgesia:** titrated IV opioid (fentanyl, morphine), **ketamine** (0.1–0.3 mg/kg for analgesia), regional blocks (fascia iliaca for femoral fracture), nitrous oxide; **tetanus prophylaxis.**

### 42.6 Damage-control resuscitation and massive haemorrhage

**Lethal triad (or diamond):** **hypothermia, acidosis, coagulopathy** (plus hypocalcaemia): a self-reinforcing cycle that kills trauma patients; **early prevention and treatment are essential.**

#### Principles

1. **Stop the bleeding** (the definitive treatment): surgery, interventional radiology, tourniquets, pelvic binder, haemostatic agents, **resuscitative endovascular balloon occlusion of the aorta (REBOA)** in selected centres.
2. **Permissive hypotension** (target systolic ~80–90 mmHg, or palpable radial pulse and mental status) in **penetrating torso trauma and uncontrolled haemorrhage** until bleeding is controlled; **not** in traumatic brain injury (target SBP ≥110, MAP ≥80 per guidelines) or spinal cord injury; avoid prolonged hypotension.
3. **Minimise crystalloid:** limit to about 1 L (or less), **early use of blood products** in a balanced ratio.
4. **Massive transfusion protocol (MTP):** activate when **ongoing bleeding with predicted need for ≥4 units of red cells in 1 hour, or ≥10 units in 24 hours,** using scores such as **ABC (Assessment of Blood Consumption: penetrating mechanism, SBP ≤90, HR ≥120, positive FAST: ≥2 criteria),** TASH, or shock index >1.
   - **Ratio:** **red cells : plasma : platelets about 1:1:1** (PROPPR trial) via pre-packed packs (e.g. 4–6 units red cells, 4–6 units plasma, 1 apheresis platelet dose per round); **O-negative blood** for women of childbearing age (O-positive acceptable for men) until cross-matched; **warm all products.**
   - **Whole blood** (low-titre O) in some systems.
5. **Tranexamic acid** within 3 hours (and with ongoing bleeding).
6. **Calcium:** **calcium chloride 1 g IV (or gluconate 3 g)** with each 4 units of blood products, and for ionised calcium <1.1 mmol/L (citrate binds calcium; hypocalcaemia impairs coagulation and cardiac function).
7. **Fibrinogen:** replace if <1.5–2.0 g/L (cryoprecipitate or fibrinogen concentrate); **viscoelastic testing (TEG/ROTEM)** to guide products; **prothrombin complex concentrate** in selected cases; **platelets** to maintain >50 (>100 in TBI or ongoing bleeding).
8. **Prevent hypothermia** (fluid warmers, forced-air warmers, rapid infuser), **correct acidosis** by restoring perfusion (bicarbonate rarely), **maintain normocalcaemia and avoid hyperkalaemia.**
9. **Early surgical damage control** (packing, vessel ligation/shunting, bowel resection without anastomosis, temporary abdominal closure), then ICU resuscitation, and planned re-operation at 24–48 hours.
10. **Monitor:** lactate (every 30–60 minutes), base deficit, temperature, coagulation/TEG, potassium, calcium, haemoglobin, urine output.
11. **Complications:** transfusion reactions (TRALI, TACO), hyperkalaemia, citrate toxicity, abdominal compartment syndrome, ARDS, AKI, DIC, MODS.

### 42.7 Injuries by region

#### Abdominal trauma

- **Mechanisms:** **blunt** (road traffic collisions, falls, assault: spleen, liver, mesentery, bowel, kidney, pancreas) and **penetrating** (stab: liver, small bowel, diaphragm, colon; gunshot: high energy, multiple organs).
- **Assessment:** haemodynamics, abdominal examination (tenderness, distension, seat-belt sign, guarding, evisceration), **FAST** (free fluid; limited for retroperitoneal and hollow viscus injury), CT (most sensitive in stable patients), **diagnostic peritoneal lavage** (rarely).
- **Indications for laparotomy:** **haemodynamic instability with positive FAST or peritonitis,** evisceration, free air, gunshot wound crossing the peritoneum (most), stab with peritonitis or instability, CT evidence of bowel perforation, ongoing bleeding.
- **Non-operative management** of **haemodynamically stable solid organ injuries** (spleen, liver, kidney) with monitoring and angioembolisation as needed (success rates >80–90% for splenic and hepatic injuries).
- **Hollow viscus injuries** can be delayed in presentation (peritonitis hours later): serial examinations.
- **Pancreatic and duodenal injuries** (retroperitoneal): handlebar injuries in children; amylase and CT.
- **Genitourinary injuries:** haematuria with a stable patient: CT cystogram/urethrogram as indicated.

#### Pelvic fractures

- **High-energy** (road traffic collisions, falls, crush) or low-energy in older patients; **life-threatening haemorrhage** (venous plexus, arterial branches of the internal iliac, bone surfaces).
- **Signs:** pelvic pain, perineal bruising, scrotal or labial haematoma, blood at the meatus, leg length discrepancy, instability; **do not "spring" the pelvis repeatedly** (clot disruption).
- **Management:** **pelvic binder** at the greater trochanters (applied early), **massive transfusion,** TXA, **external fixation, preperitoneal pelvic packing, angioembolisation** (haemodynamically unstable patients with negative FAST: pelvic bleeding is likely), REBOA zone 3; exclude other sources (chest, abdomen); associated urethral, bladder, rectal, vaginal and nerve injuries; open pelvic fractures (high mortality: colostomy, antibiotics).

#### Limb injuries

- **Priorities:** **life over limb;** tourniquets for exsanguinating bleeding; splint fractures; **neurovascular examination** (pulses, capillary refill, sensation, motor function) before and after reduction.
- **Hard signs of vascular injury:** absent pulses, active haemorrhage, expanding or pulsatile haematoma, bruit or thrill, signs of ischaemia (6 Ps): **immediate surgical exploration or CT angiography if stable.** **Soft signs** (diminished pulses, adjacent nerve injury, proximity of wound): ankle–brachial index (<0.9 requires imaging).
- **Open fractures:** photograph/dress and cover with a saline-soaked dressing, **IV antibiotics within 1 hour** (cefazolin or co-amoxiclav; add gentamicin for Gustilo type III), **tetanus prophylaxis,** urgent debridement and stabilisation within 12–24 hours; classification (Gustilo–Anderson I–III).
- **Traumatic amputation:** control bleeding; preserve the part (wrapped in saline-moistened gauze in a sealed bag, on ice, not directly on ice); early replantation assessment.
- **Femoral shaft fractures:** blood loss up to 1.5 L; traction splint; early fixation; fat embolism syndrome (dyspnoea, petechiae, confusion 24–72 hours later).
- **Crush injury and rhabdomyolysis:** hyperkalaemia, AKI; early fluids, urine alkalinisation not routinely recommended, monitor potassium, dialysis if needed.

#### Compartment syndrome

- **Mechanism:** raised pressure in a closed fascial compartment (**tibia fractures, forearm fractures, crush, reperfusion, tight casts or dressings, burns, anticoagulation, prolonged immobilisation**) reduces perfusion leading to ischaemia and necrosis within **4–6 hours.**
- **Features:** **pain out of proportion** to the injury, **pain on passive stretch** of the muscles in the compartment (earliest and most reliable sign), tense swollen compartment, paraesthesia, later pallor and paralysis; **pulses are often present** (pulselessness is a very late sign). In children, increasing analgesic requirement, anxiety, and agitation.
- **Diagnosis:** clinical; **compartment pressure measurement** if equivocal or unconscious (delta pressure [diastolic BP − compartment pressure] <30 mmHg, or absolute >30 mmHg).
- **Management:** **remove tight dressings, casts and circumferential bandages;** position at heart level (not elevated); correct hypotension and hypoxia; **emergency fasciotomy** of all compartments (four-compartment fasciotomy of the leg). **Delay results in permanent damage.**

#### Spinal injuries

- **Cervical spine assessment:** **NEXUS criteria** (no midline tenderness, no focal deficit, normal alertness, no intoxication, no painful distracting injury) or **Canadian C-spine rule** (high-risk factors: age ≥65, dangerous mechanism, paraesthesia; low-risk factors allowing assessment of range of motion) to decide on imaging; **CT cervical spine** for high-risk patients or those who cannot be clinically cleared; **maintain spinal immobilisation** (collar, blocks, flat supine or log-roll) until cleared; **avoid prolonged rigid immobilisation** where feasible (pressure injury, airway risk).
- **Spinal cord injury:** motor and sensory level, priapism, hypotension and bradycardia (neurogenic shock; Chapter 38), respiratory compromise (C3–C5), rectal tone; **ASIA classification;** early surgical stabilisation and decompression (within 24 hours for incomplete injuries), MAP ≥85 mmHg for 5–7 days; **steroids are not recommended** (NASCIS controversy).
- **Thoracolumbar injury,** associated visceral injuries; CT imaging of the whole spine as part of trauma CT.

#### Head and face (summary)

See Chapter 41 for TBI. **Facial fractures:** airway (bleeding, swelling), Le Fort fractures, orbital blowout (diplopia, enophthalmos), mandibular fractures; **base-of-skull fracture** (no nasal tubes). **Eye injuries:** vision, pupils, hyphaema, open globe: shield, antibiotics, ophthalmology.

#### Burns

- **Assessment:** **stop the burning process; cool the burn** with running tepid water for 20 minutes (within 3 hours), remove jewellery and clothing, keep the patient warm, assess **ABCDE** (airway and inhalation injury in enclosed-space fires, facial burns, singed hairs, hoarseness, soot in the mouth: early intubation; **carbon monoxide and cyanide poisoning**).
- **Depth:** superficial (epidermal: red, painful, no blisters), **partial thickness** (superficial dermal: blisters, moist, painful, blanching; deep dermal: dry, pale, reduced sensation), **full thickness** (white, leathery, painless).
- **Extent (total body surface area, TBSA):** **rule of nines** (adult: head 9%, each arm 9%, each leg 18%, front trunk 18%, back trunk 18%, perineum 1%), **Lund and Browder chart** (accurate, age-adjusted), palm method (patient's palm including fingers ≈ 1% TBSA); count partial and full thickness only.
- **Fluid resuscitation (adults, burns >15–20% TBSA; children >10%):** **Parkland formula:** **4 mL × body weight (kg) × %TBSA** of crystalloid (Ringer's lactate) over 24 hours from the time of injury; **half in the first 8 hours,** half over the next 16 hours; titrate to **urine output 0.5 mL/kg/hour** (1 mL/kg/hour in children), avoid over-resuscitation ("fluid creep"); colloids in the second 24 hours as per local protocol; Parkland is a starting point.
- **Analgesia** (opioids, ketamine), tetanus, wound care (non-adherent dressings, no creams initially), early excision and grafting of deep burns.
- **Circumferential burns of limbs or chest:** **escharotomy** to relieve compartment syndrome or restricted ventilation.
- **Special:** electrical burns (cardiac arrhythmia, rhabdomyolysis, hidden injury, compartment syndrome; ECG, CK, myoglobinuria), chemical burns (copious irrigation, specific antidotes: hydrofluoric acid (calcium gluconate)), non-accidental burns in children.
- **Referral to a burns centre (ABA criteria):** partial-thickness burns >10% TBSA; burns of the face, hands, feet, genitalia, perineum or major joints; full-thickness burns; electrical or chemical burns; inhalation injury; burns with trauma or comorbidity; children; non-accidental injury.

---

### 42.8 Secondary and tertiary surveys

- **Secondary survey:** after the primary survey and resuscitation, a **head-to-toe examination** (including the back, scalp, ears, nose, mouth, neck, chest, abdomen, pelvis, perineum, limbs, neurological examination) with **AMPLE history** (Allergies, Medications, Past medical history, Last meal, Events), further imaging (CT, X-rays), review of investigations, tetanus, and analgesia. Identify injuries not found earlier.
- **Tertiary survey:** within 24 hours (after stabilisation and recovery of consciousness) to find **missed injuries** (up to 10% in major trauma: hand, foot, spine, small fractures): repeat examination, review of all imaging by a radiologist, and documentation.
- **Safeguarding:** consider abuse and intimate partner violence.

---

### 42.9 Special populations

#### Children

- Children have **a higher surface-area-to-volume ratio (hypothermia), pliable skeletons (organ injury without fracture), large heads (head injury, cervical injury high).** Compensate until late: **tachycardia and poor perfusion are early signs; hypotension is pre-terminal.**
- Use **weight-based dosing and length-based tapes** (Broselow), **normal ranges by age** (Chapter 37), **intraosseous access** early, **10–20 mL/kg crystalloid boluses** (then blood 10 mL/kg), keep warm; avoid hypoglycaemia; TXA (15 mg/kg). **Spinal cord injury without radiographic abnormality (SCIWORA).** **Non-accidental injury** (inconsistent history, patterned injuries, multiple fractures of different ages, retinal haemorrhages).
- Early referral to a paediatric trauma centre.

#### Pregnancy

- **Physiological changes:** increased blood volume (up to 30–35% blood loss before maternal signs), **fetal compromise early;** supine hypotension syndrome (tilt left after 20 weeks); displaced abdominal organs.
- **Priorities:** resuscitate the mother first (best treatment for the fetus), **left lateral tilt,** fetal heart monitoring (≥24–26 weeks), obstetric involvement; **placental abruption** (revealed or concealed haemorrhage, pain, DIC), **uterine rupture,** **fetomaternal haemorrhage** (Kleihauer–Betke test; **anti-D immunoglobulin** in Rh-negative mothers), do not withhold imaging (CT, X-ray) when indicated, **perimortem caesarean** at 4 minutes if no ROSC and gestation ≥20 weeks.

#### Older adults

- **Under-triage** is common: **occult hypovolaemia** (hypertension, beta-blockers, pacemakers), **anticoagulants and antiplatelets** (intracranial haemorrhage with minor trauma; reverse promptly), **comorbidity,** **frailty,** **rib fractures** (mortality increases with number: analgesia, regional blocks, early critical care), **hip fractures,** **low threshold for CT,** and early goals-of-care discussions. **A systolic BP <110 mmHg may represent shock in older adults.**

#### Anticoagulated patients

- Treat bleeding with **reversal (PCC, vitamin K, idarucizumab, andexanet),** **low threshold for CT head** after any head injury.

# Part C: Other Major Haemorrhage

### 42.10 Obstetric haemorrhage

- **Antepartum haemorrhage (APH):** placental abruption (painful, tense uterus, concealed haemorrhage, DIC), **placenta praevia** (painless, bright red; no digital examination), uterine rupture, vasa praevia.
- **Postpartum haemorrhage (PPH):** blood loss **>500 mL vaginal** or **>1,000 mL caesarean** (or any blood loss with haemodynamic compromise); **primary** (within 24 hours) or **secondary** (24 hours–12 weeks). **Causes ("4 Ts"):** **T**one (uterine atony: 70%), **T**rauma (tears, uterine rupture, inversion), **T**issue (retained placenta/membranes), **T**hrombin (coagulopathy). **Risk factors:** prolonged labour, multiparity, macrosomia, polyhydramnios, multiple pregnancy, previous PPH, placenta praevia, chorioamnionitis, anticoagulants.
- **Management (stepwise, simultaneously):** call for help (obstetrician, anaesthetist, midwife, haematology, blood bank); **ABCDE,** two large-bore IV lines, **blood tests including fibrinogen,** cross-match; **bimanual uterine compression and uterine massage,** empty the bladder; **uterotonics: oxytocin 5–10 IU IV slowly (then infusion), ergometrine 0.5 mg IM/IV (avoid in hypertension), carboprost 250 µg IM (avoid in asthma), misoprostol 800 µg;** **tranexamic acid 1 g IV within 3 hours** (WOMAN trial; repeat after 30 minutes if bleeding continues); **fluids and blood** (MTP; fibrinogen replacement); **examine for tears and retained products** (theatre if needed); **balloon tamponade** (Bakri), **uterine compression sutures (B-Lynch), uterine artery embolisation, internal iliac ligation,** **hysterectomy** as lifesaving. **HAEMOSTASIS** mnemonic summarises the steps.
- **Prevention:** active management of the third stage (oxytocin, controlled cord traction), risk assessment.

### 42.11 Other sources of major haemorrhage (cross-references)

- **GI haemorrhage:** Chapter 27. **Ruptured ectopic pregnancy:** resuscitation and emergency surgery (Chapter 25). **Ruptured splenic artery aneurysm, hepatic tumour rupture, spontaneous retroperitoneal haemorrhage (anticoagulated),** **post-operative haemorrhage** (return to theatre), **haemoptysis** (Chapter 23), **epistaxis** (anterior packing, posterior balloon, tranexamic acid, sphenopalatine ligation), **post-tonsillectomy bleeding,** **vascular access bleeding.**
- **Coagulopathy and anticoagulant reversal:** Chapter 35.
- **Cell salvage** and **point-of-care coagulation testing** reduce allogeneic transfusion; **Jehovah's Witness** and patients declining transfusion: early planning.

### 42.12 Worked cases

#### Case 1: Perforated peptic ulcer

*A 62-year-old man on regular ibuprofen has sudden severe epigastric pain "like being stabbed," lying very still. HR 118, BP 100/60, temperature 37.9 °C, rigid abdomen with absent bowel sounds. Erect chest X-ray: free air under the right hemidiaphragm. Lactate 3.4.*

**Reasoning.** **Perforated duodenal ulcer** with generalised peritonitis and early sepsis.

**Action.** Resuscitation (fluids, oxygen), analgesia, **piperacillin-tazobactam,** PPI, NG tube, urgent surgical review, **laparoscopic or open omental patch repair,** *H. pylori* testing, stop NSAIDs.

#### Case 2: Strangulated small bowel obstruction

*A 70-year-old woman with a previous hysterectomy has 24 hours of colicky pain, vomiting, constipation and distension; pain is now constant. HR 110, temperature 38.2 °C, lactate 3.8, WBC 18. CT: closed-loop obstruction with mesenteric swirl and reduced wall enhancement.*

**Reasoning.** **Closed-loop obstruction with strangulation** (adhesive band).

**Action.** IV fluids, NG decompression, antibiotics, **emergency laparotomy** with resection of non-viable bowel, ICU.

#### Case 3: Acute mesenteric ischaemia

*A 78-year-old woman with AF (not anticoagulated) has sudden severe periumbilical pain, vomiting, and passage of loose stool with blood. The abdomen is soft with only mild tenderness. Lactate 5.2; CT angiography: SMA embolus with absent enhancement of the small bowel.*

**Reasoning.** **Embolic SMA occlusion.**

**Action.** Heparin infusion, resuscitation, antibiotics, **emergency revascularisation** (embolectomy or endovascular) and bowel resection with second look; anticoagulation thereafter.

#### Case 4: Blunt trauma: positive FAST

*A 30-year-old driver in a high-speed collision: HR 134, BP 78/40, RR 30, GCS 14, abdominal tenderness and seat-belt bruising. Airway patent; chest clear; FAST: free fluid in Morison pouch and pelvis. Pelvis stable.*

**Reasoning.** **Class III–IV haemorrhagic shock from intra-abdominal bleeding** (liver/spleen), unresponsive to fluids.

**Action.** Activate the **massive transfusion protocol,** TXA, warm products, permissive hypotension, **immediate transfer to theatre for damage-control laparotomy** (packing, splenectomy, haemostasis), calcium, lactate monitoring, ICU rewarming, planned re-look.

#### Case 5: Pelvic fracture with shock

*A 45-year-old pedestrian hit by a car: HR 140, BP 70/40, pelvic ring unstable, scrotal bruising; FAST negative; chest X-ray clear; pelvic X-ray shows an open-book fracture.*

**Reasoning.** **Exsanguination from pelvic fracture** (retroperitoneal).

**Action.** **Pelvic binder at the greater trochanters,** massive transfusion, TXA, early preperitoneal packing and external fixation or **angioembolisation;** check urethra (no catheter if blood at the meatus), hypothermia prevention.

#### Case 6: Penetrating chest trauma: tamponade

*A 24-year-old man with a stab wound to the left parasternal chest: HR 140, BP 70/50, JVP raised, muffled heart sounds, pale and agitated. FAST: pericardial fluid.*

**Reasoning.** **Cardiac tamponade** (Beck triad).

**Action.** Immediate **thoracotomy or sternotomy** to release tamponade and repair the cardiac wound in theatre (or resuscitative thoracotomy in the ED if peri-arrest); blood transfusion; do not delay for further imaging.

#### Case 7: Burns

*An 80-kg man has flame burns to the whole anterior trunk and both arms (front and back), with partial-thickness blistering, after a house fire; hoarse voice and soot around the nose. TBSA about 36% (anterior trunk 18% + both arms 18%).*

**Reasoning.** **Major burn with inhalation injury risk.**

**Action.** **Early intubation** (airway oedema), 100% oxygen, treat carbon monoxide (and consider cyanide), cooling of burns, analgesia, **Parkland: 4 × 80 × 36 = 11,520 mL over 24 hours (about 5,760 mL in the first 8 hours, i.e. ~720 mL/h),** adjust to urine output 0.5 mL/kg/h, tetanus, transfer to a burns centre, escharotomy for circumferential limb burns.

#### Case 8: Compartment syndrome

*A 22-year-old footballer has a closed tibial shaft fracture treated with a cast. At 6 hours, he has severe pain unrelieved by opioids, pain on passive toe extension, and a tense, swollen calf. Foot pulses are present.*

**Reasoning.** **Acute compartment syndrome of the leg.**

**Action.** Remove the cast and bandages, check pressure if uncertain, **urgent four-compartment fasciotomy;** avoid elevating above heart level; monitor for rhabdomyolysis.

#### Case 9: Postpartum haemorrhage

*A 33-year-old woman, 20 minutes after a vaginal delivery of a 4.2-kg infant, has heavy bleeding (1,400 mL so far), a soft boggy uterus, HR 124, BP 90/50.*

**Reasoning.** **Primary PPH from uterine atony.**

**Action.** Call for help, bimanual compression and uterine massage, oxytocin bolus and infusion, **tranexamic acid 1 g IV,** ergometrine/carboprost/misoprostol, two IV lines, fluids and blood (MTP if ongoing), catheterise, check for trauma and retained tissue, Bakri balloon, theatre for compression sutures/embolisation/hysterectomy if refractory.

#### Case 10: Older adult, minor head injury on anticoagulants

*An 84-year-old man on apixaban falls at home and hits his head, GCS 15, small scalp haematoma, no neurology. BP 130/80, HR 88 (on beta-blocker).*

**Reasoning.** **High-risk head injury (age and anticoagulation):** risk of delayed intracranial haemorrhage; "normal" vital signs do not exclude occult injury.

**Action.** **CT head immediately,** check for other injuries (hip, ribs, spine), **if bleeding: andexanet or PCC,** observation, review anticoagulation, falls assessment.

### 42.13 Common pitfalls

- **Delaying analgesia or surgical review** in the acute abdomen; waiting for laboratory or imaging confirmation in an unstable patient with peritonitis or ruptured aneurysm.
- **Missing mesenteric ischaemia** ("pain out of proportion," AF) and attributing symptoms to gastroenteritis.
- **Overlooking hernial orifices** in obstruction.
- **Fixating on the dramatic injury** (distracting injury) and missing life threats; **not following xABCDE.**
- **Delay in controlling external haemorrhage** (tourniquets, pressure).
- **Excessive crystalloid** in haemorrhagic shock (dilutional coagulopathy, hypothermia, acidosis).
- **Failing to give tranexamic acid early,** to keep patients warm, and to give calcium.
- **Attributing hypotension to neurogenic shock** or head injury (in adults) without excluding haemorrhage.
- **Missing hypothermia, compartment syndrome, rhabdomyolysis, and abdominal compartment syndrome.**
- **Needle decompression delay or waiting for X-ray** in tension pneumothorax.
- **Removing pelvic binders or "springing" the pelvis.**
- **Under-triage of older and anticoagulated patients;** no CT head after minor head trauma on anticoagulants.
- **Missed injuries:** no tertiary survey.
- **Not considering pregnancy** and not tilting the pregnant trauma patient.
- **Burn mismanagement:** failure to cool, intubate early, or over-resuscitate.
- **Not calling for help or activating the massive haemorrhage protocol early.**

---

### 42.14 Key points

- The acute abdomen with peritonitis, shock, or strangulation needs resuscitation, analgesia, antibiotics, imaging when safe, and **early surgery;** do not delay for investigations in unstable patients.
- Mesenteric ischaemia, ruptured AAA, and strangulated obstruction are time-critical; pain out of proportion is a clue.
- Trauma: **xABCDE;** control catastrophic haemorrhage first (direct pressure, tourniquet, pelvic binder, TXA).
- Treat life-threatening chest injuries immediately (tension pneumothorax, open pneumothorax, massive haemothorax, flail chest, tamponade).
- Damage-control resuscitation: balanced blood products (1:1:1), permissive hypotension (not in head injury), limited crystalloid, TXA, calcium, warmth, early surgical or radiological haemorrhage control.
- Use FAST and CT appropriately; non-operative management for stable solid organ injury.
- Recognise compartment syndromes (pain on passive stretch) and act with fasciotomy.
- Adapt to children, pregnancy, and older or anticoagulated patients; repeat surveys to find missed injuries.
- Obstetric haemorrhage: 4 Ts, uterotonics, tranexamic acid, early escalation.

### 42.15 Self-assessment

1. List the components of the xABCDE primary survey and the first action in each.
   *Answer: x: control catastrophic external haemorrhage (pressure, tourniquet); A: airway with C-spine protection; B: breathing (decompress tension pneumothorax, seal open pneumothorax); C: circulation (IV/IO access, blood, control bleeding, FAST); D: disability (GCS, pupils, glucose); E: exposure with prevention of hypothermia.*

2. Describe damage-control resuscitation.
   *Answer: Stop bleeding; permissive hypotension (not in head injury); minimal crystalloid; balanced red cells:plasma:platelets about 1:1:1; tranexamic acid, calcium, fibrinogen; prevent hypothermia, acidosis, coagulopathy; early surgery for haemorrhage and contamination control.*

3. Which findings support emergency laparotomy in blunt abdominal trauma?
   *Answer: Haemodynamic instability with positive FAST, peritonitis, evisceration, free air or hollow viscus injury on CT, or ongoing bleeding.*

4. What are the early features of compartment syndrome, and what is the treatment?
   *Answer: Pain out of proportion, pain on passive stretch, tense swollen compartment, paraesthesia; remove constricting dressings and perform urgent fasciotomy; pulses may remain present.*

5. Calculate the first 8-hour fluid requirement by the Parkland formula for a 70-kg adult with 40% TBSA burns.
   *Answer: 4 × 70 × 40 = 11,200 mL in 24 hours; half (5,600 mL) in the first 8 hours from the time of injury.*

6. What are the "4 Ts" of postpartum haemorrhage and the first-line drugs?
   *Answer: Tone, Trauma, Tissue, Thrombin; oxytocin, ergometrine, carboprost, misoprostol, and tranexamic acid.*
