## Chapter 33. Altered Consciousness, Confusion and Seizures

### Learning objectives

After studying this chapter you should be able to:

1. Define consciousness, delirium, coma and related states, and assess the level of consciousness with AVPU and the Glasgow Coma Scale.
2. Manage the first minutes of the patient with reduced consciousness: ABCDE, glucose, naloxone, thiamine, seizure control, and recognition of raised intracranial pressure and herniation.
3. Use the bedside examination (pupils, eye movements, respiratory pattern, motor response, signs of meningism, skin) to distinguish structural from toxic–metabolic causes.
4. Diagnose delirium with a validated tool (CAM, 4AT), identify its causes, and manage it with a non-pharmacological, cause-directed approach.
5. Recognise the major metabolic, toxic and infectious causes of altered mental state (hypoglycaemia, alcohol withdrawal, Wernicke encephalopathy, hepatic encephalopathy, sepsis, encephalitis).
6. Classify seizures, distinguish provoked from unprovoked seizures and epilepsy, and evaluate a first seizure.
7. Treat status epilepticus in a time-based manner, and recognise non-convulsive and psychogenic seizures.
8. Give appropriate safety, driving and medication advice to patients with epilepsy.

# Part A: Altered Consciousness and Confusion

### 33.1 Definitions

**Consciousness** has two components:

- **Arousal (wakefulness):** governed by the ascending reticular activating system in the brainstem and its connections to the thalamus.
- **Awareness (content):** the cerebral cortex and its networks; awareness of self and environment.

Reduced consciousness results from **bilateral cerebral hemisphere dysfunction or brainstem (reticular activating system) dysfunction.** A small lesion in one hemisphere does not by itself cause coma (but may through herniation or mass effect).

| Term | Description |
|---|---|
| **Alert** | Awake, oriented, attentive |
| **Lethargy / drowsiness** | Reduced alertness; falls asleep easily but wakes to voice |
| **Obtundation** | Reduced interest in the environment, slow responses; needs stimulation to remain awake |
| **Stupor** | Rousable only with vigorous or painful stimuli; returns to unresponsiveness when stimulation stops |
| **Coma** | **Unrousable unresponsiveness;** eyes closed, no purposeful response |
| **Delirium** | **Acute, fluctuating disturbance of attention and awareness with additional cognitive disturbance** (see 33.7) |
| **Unresponsive wakefulness syndrome (vegetative state)** | Sleep–wake cycles with eye opening but no awareness |
| **Minimally conscious state** | Fluctuating but reproducible evidence of awareness |
| **Locked-in syndrome** | Awake and aware but quadriplegic and unable to speak (ventral pontine lesion, e.g. basilar artery occlusion); communication by vertical eye movements and blinking |
| **Brain death** | Irreversible cessation of all brain function including the brainstem; diagnosed using formal criteria |

**Confusion** is a lay and clinical term for impaired clear thinking, disorientation, or inability to follow a coherent line of thought; it usually means delirium or dementia and needs precise characterisation.

### 33.2 The first minutes: the unconscious or obtunded patient

**Treat while diagnosing.** Reduced consciousness is a medical emergency; many causes are reversible if treated rapidly (hypoglycaemia, opioid overdose, hypoxia, seizures, meningitis).

#### Step 1: ABCDE (Chapter 37)

- **Airway:** open (head tilt–chin lift or jaw thrust if trauma), suction, adjuncts; **GCS ≤8 or inability to protect the airway** → call anaesthetic help for intubation.
- **Breathing:** respiratory rate and pattern, SpO₂, give oxygen, blood gas.
- **Circulation:** pulse, BP, rhythm (ECG), IV access, bloods, fluids.
- **Disability:** **glucose (immediately),** AVPU/GCS, pupils, lateralising signs, seizures.
- **Exposure:** temperature, skin (rash, injection marks, bruises, medical alert bracelets), injuries (log-roll, assess spine).

#### Step 2: Immediate empirical treatment

| If | Give |
|---|---|
| **Hypoglycaemia** (glucose <4 mmol/L / 70 mg/dL) | **IV glucose** (e.g. 10% or 20% dextrose 150–200 mL or 50% 50 mL per local protocol); IM glucagon if no IV access. Give **thiamine (IV) before or with glucose** in alcohol misuse, malnutrition, hyperemesis, or bariatric surgery to avoid precipitating Wernicke encephalopathy (do not delay glucose in a hypoglycaemic patient) |
| **Opioid toxidrome** (respiratory depression, pinpoint pupils, reduced consciousness) | **Naloxone** titrated (0.4–2 mg IV/IM/intranasal; repeat); support ventilation; observe because naloxone is shorter-acting than many opioids |
| **Suspected seizure activity** | Benzodiazepine (see 33.15) |
| **Suspected meningitis/encephalitis** | **IV antibiotics (cephalosporin ± ampicillin) and aciclovir,** with dexamethasone per protocol, without waiting for CT or LP |
| **Hypothermia / hyperthermia** | Warming / cooling |
| **Hypotension / shock** | Fluids, vasopressors, treat cause |
| **Suspected raised ICP, herniation** (see below) | Head-up 30°, airway and ventilation, osmotic therapy (mannitol or hypertonic saline), urgent neurosurgical input |
| **Poisoning** | Specific antidotes (paracetamol, salicylate, organophosphates, carbon monoxide, etc.), activated charcoal if appropriate and early; call poison centre |
| **Benzodiazepine overdose** | **Flumazenil** only in selected cases (risk of seizures in mixed overdoses or benzodiazepine dependence) |

#### Step 3: Rapid assessment and tests

- **Collateral history** is crucial: onset (sudden vs gradual), preceding events (headache, trauma, illness, fever, seizure), medications, alcohol and drugs, medical history (diabetes, epilepsy, cancer, liver or kidney disease, psychiatric illness, previous suicide attempts), possible overdose (containers, notes), circumstances (found down, exposure to cold, fire, gas).
- **Bedside tests:** glucose, ECG, blood gas (pH, PCO₂, lactate, carboxyhaemoglobin), U&E, calcium, magnesium, LFTs, ammonia (if liver disease), FBC, CRP, coagulation, blood cultures, urinalysis, **toxicology** (paracetamol, salicylate, ethanol level, drug screen as appropriate), CK, TSH/cortisol (if suspected), troponin, pregnancy test.
- **CT head without contrast** as soon as stable, particularly if there are focal signs, signs of trauma, headache, anticoagulation, or no obvious metabolic cause; **CT before LP** if there is focal deficit, papilloedema, reduced GCS, seizures, or immunosuppression (while treating for meningitis); **MRI** and **EEG** for persistent unexplained coma (nonconvulsive status epilepticus, encephalitis).

### 33.3 Assessment of the comatose patient

#### Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS)

| Component | Response | Score |
|---|---|---|
| **Eye opening** | Spontaneous | 4 |
| | To sound | 3 |
| | To pressure (pain) | 2 |
| | None | 1 |
| **Verbal response** | Orientated | 5 |
| | Confused | 4 |
| | Words (inappropriate) | 3 |
| | Sounds (incomprehensible) | 2 |
| | None | 1 |
| **Motor response** | Obeys commands | 6 |
| | Localises to pain | 5 |
| | Withdraws from pain | 4 |
| | Abnormal flexion (decorticate) | 3 |
| | Extension (decerebrate) | 2 |
| | None | 1 |

**Total 3–15.** Always **record the components** (e.g. E2 V3 M4 = 9), not only the sum. **GCS ≤8 = severe impairment** (airway at risk). Limitations: intubated patients (verbal not testable: "V-T"), orbital swelling, aphasia, sedation. The **FOUR score** (Eye, Motor, Brainstem, Respiration) adds brainstem reflexes and respiration and is useful in intubated patients.

**AVPU:** Alert, responds to Voice, responds to Pain, Unresponsive. Quick triage tool (Chapter 11).

#### Examination points that localise the cause

| Finding | Interpretation |
|---|---|
| **Respiratory pattern** | **Cheyne–Stokes** (bihemispheric or diencephalic dysfunction, heart failure, metabolic); **central neurogenic hyperventilation** (midbrain/pons); **apneustic** (pontine); **ataxic (Biot)** (medullary, ominous); **Kussmaul** (metabolic acidosis); **slow shallow** (opioids, hypothyroidism, sedatives) |
| **Pupils** | **Pinpoint, reactive:** opioids, pontine haemorrhage (very small), organophosphates, cholinergic drugs, clonidine. **Small and reactive:** metabolic encephalopathy, thalamic lesions. **Unilateral dilated and fixed:** third nerve compression from uncal herniation (emergency), topical mydriatics, previous injury, aneurysm. **Bilateral fixed, midposition:** midbrain lesion, severe anoxia. **Bilateral fixed dilated:** anticholinergics, anoxia, deep barbiturate toxicity, brain death. **Preserved pupillary reflexes with coma** points toward a metabolic cause (except opioids, which also preserve it) |
| **Eye position and movements** | **Conjugate deviation toward the lesion** in hemispheric stroke (away from the paralysed side); **toward the paralysed side** in seizures and pontine lesions; **dysconjugate:** brainstem or cranial nerve palsies; **downward and inward (setting sun):** hydrocephalus/midbrain; **roving eye movements:** intact brainstem, metabolic coma; **ocular bobbing:** pontine lesion; **oculocephalic (doll's eyes) and oculovestibular (caloric) reflexes** test brainstem integrity (do not perform the oculocephalic test if cervical spine injury is possible) |
| **Corneal and gag reflexes** | Brainstem function |
| **Motor** | **Asymmetric response, hemiparesis, unequal tone or reflexes:** structural lesion; **symmetrical:** metabolic or toxic (but may be asymmetric in hypoglycaemia, hepatic and hyperosmolar states); **decorticate (flexion):** hemispheric/diencephalic; **decerebrate (extension):** midbrain/pons; **myoclonus, asterixis:** metabolic (uraemia, liver, CO₂), **tremor and clonus:** serotonin syndrome |
| **Neck stiffness** | Meningitis, subarachnoid haemorrhage, but also cervical spine pathology and tonsillar herniation |
| **Fundoscopy** | Papilloedema (raised ICP, but absent in the early phase), subhyaloid haemorrhage (SAH), hypertensive retinopathy |
| **Skin and mucosae** | Petechial rash (meningococcaemia), needle marks, jaundice, cyanosis, cherry-red (CO), pallor, myxoedema (dry, coarse skin), sweating, bruising, scars, Medic-Alert |
| **Temperature** | Fever (infection, heat stroke, neuroleptic malignant syndrome, serotonin syndrome, thyroid storm, anticholinergic toxicity), hypothermia (exposure, sepsis, hypothyroidism, hypoglycaemia, alcohol) |
| **Breath** | Alcohol, ketones (fruity), uraemic, hepatic fetor, garlic (organophosphates) |
| **Blood pressure and pulse** | **Cushing reflex:** hypertension, bradycardia, irregular respirations (raised ICP); hypotension (shock, sepsis, overdose); hypertension (hypertensive encephalopathy, intracranial haemorrhage) |

#### Herniation syndromes (emergency)

- **Uncal (transtentorial) herniation:** ipsilateral dilated pupil, then contralateral hemiparesis, decorticate then decerebrate posturing, reduced consciousness, respiratory changes.
- **Central transtentorial herniation:** progressive diencephalic then midbrain and pontine signs.
- **Tonsillar herniation (foramen magnum):** neck stiffness, head tilt, sudden collapse and respiratory arrest.
- **Treatment:** secure airway, elevate head to 30°, hyperventilate briefly to PaCO₂ 30–35 mmHg as a temporising measure, hyperosmolar therapy (mannitol 0.25–1 g/kg or 3% hypertonic saline), treat cause (haematoma evacuation, EVD placement, decompressive craniectomy), avoid hypotonic fluids, hypotension and hypoxia; treat seizures; urgent neurosurgical consultation.

### 33.4 Causes of altered consciousness

A mnemonic that covers the major categories is **AEIOU-TIPS:**

| Letter | Cause |
|---|---|
| **A** | **A**lcohol (intoxication, withdrawal), **A**cidosis |
| **E** | **E**pilepsy (postictal, non-convulsive status), **E**lectrolytes (Na, Ca, Mg), **E**ncephalopathy (hepatic, hypertensive, Wernicke), **E**ndocrine (thyroid, adrenal) |
| **I** | **I**nsulin (hypoglycaemia, DKA, hyperosmolar state), **I**nfection (sepsis, meningitis, encephalitis) |
| **O** | **O**verdose/**O**piates, **O**xygen deficiency (hypoxia, CO poisoning) |
| **U** | **U**raemia |
| **T** | **T**rauma, **T**emperature (hypo- and hyperthermia), **T**umour, **T**hiamine |
| **I** | **I**nfarction/ischaemia (stroke), **I**ntracranial haemorrhage |
| **P** | **P**sychiatric (catatonia, conversion), **P**oisoning, **P**orphyria |
| **S** | **S**troke, **S**ubarachnoid haemorrhage, **S**eizure, **S**hock, **S**yncope, **S**ubdural |

#### Structural vs toxic-metabolic

| | Structural | Toxic–metabolic |
|---|---|---|
| **Onset** | Sudden (stroke, haemorrhage) or progressive (tumour, abscess) | Gradual or fluctuating |
| **Focal signs** | Present, asymmetric | Usually absent, symmetrical (exceptions: hypoglycaemia, hepatic, hyperosmolar) |
| **Pupils** | Often asymmetric or abnormal | Usually symmetrical, reactive |
| **Respiratory pattern** | May indicate level | Often hyperventilation or hypoventilation |
| **Myoclonus, asterixis, tremor** | Uncommon | Common |
| **Structural lesion on CT/MRI** | Yes | No (unless secondary) |

**Causes by category:**

- **Structural (primary CNS):** ischaemic stroke (brainstem, bilateral thalamic, basilar artery occlusion, large hemispheric stroke with oedema), intracerebral haemorrhage, subarachnoid haemorrhage, subdural or extradural haematoma, tumour, abscess, hydrocephalus, cerebral venous thrombosis, traumatic brain injury, posterior reversible encephalopathy syndrome.
- **Seizures and postictal states:** including non-convulsive status epilepticus.
- **Infection:** meningitis, encephalitis (HSV, VZV, enteroviruses, arboviruses, autoimmune), brain abscess, cerebral malaria, sepsis, neurosyphilis, HIV-related.
- **Metabolic and endocrine:** hypoglycaemia, hyperglycaemic states (DKA, HHS), hyponatraemia, hypernatraemia, hypercalcaemia, hypo- and hypermagnesaemia, uraemia, hepatic encephalopathy, hypercapnia, hypoxia, myxoedema coma, thyroid storm, adrenal crisis, hypopituitarism, Wernicke encephalopathy, vitamin B12 deficiency, porphyria, hypothermia, hyperthermia.
- **Toxins and drugs:** alcohol, opioids, benzodiazepines, barbiturates, sedatives, antipsychotics, antidepressants (TCA overdose: arrhythmias, seizures), lithium, salicylates, paracetamol (late), carbon monoxide, cyanide, organophosphates, anticholinergics, serotonin syndrome, neuroleptic malignant syndrome, stimulants, toxic alcohols (methanol, ethylene glycol), GHB.
- **Cardiovascular:** cardiac arrest, severe hypotension, arrhythmia, massive PE, hypertensive encephalopathy.
- **Psychiatric:** catatonia, conversion disorder (psychogenic unresponsiveness; eyelids resist opening, eyes deviate away from the examiner, normal reflexes, preserved normal EEG).

### 33.5 Specific causes of reduced consciousness

#### Hypoglycaemia

- **Causes:** insulin and sulfonylureas (most common), alcohol, liver failure, sepsis, adrenal insufficiency, insulinoma, malnutrition, critical illness, medications (quinine, beta-blockers masking symptoms).
- **Features:** sweating, tremor, hunger, confusion, drowsiness, seizures, focal deficits (can mimic stroke), coma.
- **Action:** capillary glucose in every patient with altered consciousness; treat immediately; identify cause; observe long-acting sulfonylurea or insulin effects for 24 hours.

#### Opioid and sedative overdose

- Respiratory depression, miosis, hypothermia, reduced bowel sounds, needle marks. Naloxone; airway support; look for co-ingestants (paracetamol, benzodiazepines, alcohol).

#### Poisoning

- **Paracetamol:** often asymptomatic initially; level at 4 hours; N-acetylcysteine.
- **Salicylates:** tinnitus, hyperventilation, fever, metabolic acidosis, respiratory alkalosis.
- **Tricyclic antidepressants:** anticholinergic features, wide QRS, seizures, arrhythmia; sodium bicarbonate.
- **Carbon monoxide:** headache, confusion, cherry-red skin (rare), raised carboxyhaemoglobin; high-flow oxygen, consider hyperbaric oxygen.
- **Organophosphates:** cholinergic toxidrome (salivation, lacrimation, urination, defecation, bradycardia, bronchorrhoea, miosis); atropine, pralidoxime.
- **Toxic alcohols:** high anion gap and osmolar gap acidosis; fomepizole.
- **Serotonin syndrome:** agitation, tremor, clonus, hyperreflexia, diaphoresis, hyperthermia; cyproheptadine.
- **Neuroleptic malignant syndrome:** rigidity, fever, autonomic instability, raised CK; dantrolene, bromocriptine.

#### Hepatic encephalopathy

- Neuropsychiatric syndrome in liver disease: grade I (altered sleep, mild confusion, asterixis) to grade IV (coma). **Precipitants:** infection (including SBP), GI bleeding, constipation, dehydration, hypokalaemia, diuretics, sedatives, TIPS, renal failure, non-adherence to lactulose. Treat precipitants, **lactulose** (aim 2–3 soft stools/day), **rifaximin** for recurrence prevention, correct electrolytes; ammonia is not required for diagnosis. Consider other causes (subdural, Wernicke, hypoglycaemia).

#### Alcohol-related: intoxication, withdrawal, Wernicke–Korsakoff

- **Intoxication:** do not assume alcohol explains reduced consciousness; check glucose and CT head if head injury is possible.
- **Withdrawal:** begins 6–12 hours after the last drink with tremor, sweating, anxiety, tachycardia; **hallucinosis** 12–24 hours; **seizures** 24–48 hours; **delirium tremens** at 48–96 hours (confusion, agitation, hallucinations, autonomic instability, fever; mortality can be significant). Monitor with **CIWA-Ar;** treat with benzodiazepines (chlordiazepoxide, diazepam; lorazepam in severe liver disease), high-dose **IV thiamine,** correct electrolytes (magnesium, potassium, phosphate), fluids, glucose after thiamine. Identify and treat infections and other causes.
- **Wernicke encephalopathy:** triad of **confusion, ataxia and ophthalmoplegia/nystagmus** (complete triad in a minority); due to thiamine deficiency in alcohol use, malnutrition, hyperemesis, bariatric surgery, cancer; **treat on suspicion with IV thiamine** (e.g. 500 mg three times daily for 2–3 days, then continue); risk of Korsakoff syndrome (anterograde amnesia and confabulation) if untreated.

#### Hypo- and hypernatraemia and other electrolytes

- Severe hyponatraemia (<120 mmol/L or rapid fall): headache, nausea, confusion, seizures, coma; treat symptomatic hyponatraemia with hypertonic saline bolus (avoid overcorrection >8–10 mmol/L in 24 hours: osmotic demyelination).
- **Hypernatraemia:** irritability, lethargy, seizures; correct slowly.
- **Hypercalcaemia:** confusion, constipation, polyuria; fluids, bisphosphonates.
- **Hypomagnesaemia, hypocalcaemia, hypophosphataemia:** seizures, tetany, arrhythmias.

#### Diabetic emergencies

- **DKA:** abdominal pain, Kussmaul respiration, ketones, acidosis; **hyperosmolar hyperglycaemic state:** very high glucose (>30 mmol/L), severe dehydration, older adults, altered consciousness, seizures, focal signs.

#### Endocrine emergencies

- **Myxoedema coma:** hypothermia, bradycardia, hypoventilation, hyponatraemia, hypoglycaemia; give IV thyroid hormone and hydrocortisone.
- **Adrenal crisis:** hypotension, hypoglycaemia, hyponatraemia, hyperkalaemia; IV hydrocortisone.
- **Thyroid storm:** fever, tachyarrhythmia, agitation, delirium; beta-blockers, thionamides, steroids, iodine.

#### Meningitis and encephalitis

- **Bacterial meningitis:** fever, headache, neck stiffness, photophobia, confusion; rash in meningococcal disease; start antibiotics immediately (Chapter 41).
- **HSV encephalitis:** fever, headache, behavioural change, memory impairment, aphasia, seizures; temporal lobe involvement on MRI, CSF lymphocytes with red cells, HSV PCR; **aciclovir** IV at once.
- **Autoimmune encephalitis (e.g. anti-NMDA receptor):** psychiatric symptoms, seizures, dyskinesias, autonomic instability, reduced consciousness, young women with teratoma, subacute onset; CSF/serum antibodies, MRI, EEG; immunotherapy.
- **Others:** cerebral malaria (travel), neurosyphilis, HIV, TB meningitis, cryptococcal meningitis, progressive multifocal leukoencephalopathy, Creutzfeldt–Jakob disease (rapidly progressive dementia, myoclonus).

#### Stroke and haemorrhage

- **Basilar artery occlusion:** sudden coma or locked-in syndrome; prodromal vertigo, diplopia, dysarthria; time-critical thrombectomy.
- **Intracerebral and subarachnoid haemorrhage:** headache, vomiting, reduced consciousness (Chapter 41).
- **Subdural haematoma** (older, falls, anticoagulants): fluctuating confusion, headache, hemiparesis; weeks of progression may be subtle.

#### Non-convulsive status epilepticus (NCSE)

- Unexplained altered consciousness (confusion, stupor, coma) with subtle motor signs (eyelid twitching, nystagmus, automatisms); **EEG** is required for diagnosis; treat as status epilepticus.

#### Psychiatric and functional unresponsiveness

- Active resistance to eyelid opening, avoidance of face-hitting by a dropped hand, normal vestibulo-ocular reflexes, rapid fluttering of the eyelids; diagnosis after exclusion of organic causes; often accompanied by stress and psychiatric history. Catatonia (immobility, mutism, posturing, waxy flexibility) may be due to psychiatric or medical causes and responds to lorazepam.

### 33.6 Brain death (brief)

Brain death is the irreversible loss of all brain function. Formal testing (by experienced clinicians, per national protocol) requires **a known irreversible cause of coma, exclusion of reversible factors** (hypothermia, drugs, metabolic and endocrine disturbance, neuromuscular blockade, hypotension), and demonstration of **absent brainstem reflexes** (pupils, corneal, oculocephalic, caloric, gag, cough) and **absent respiratory effort** at a PaCO₂ above a defined threshold (apnoea test). Confirmatory tests (EEG, cerebral angiography, transcranial Doppler) are used when clinical tests cannot be performed. This diagnosis is important for organ donation and for ethical decisions about continuing treatment.

# Part B: Delirium and Confusion

### 33.7 Delirium

**Delirium** is an acute, fluctuating disturbance in attention and awareness accompanied by an additional disturbance in cognition (memory deficit, disorientation, language, visuospatial ability, perception), developing over hours to days, caused by a medical condition, substance or medication. It is common (up to 20–30% of general hospital inpatients, over 50% post-operatively in some groups, and up to 80% of ventilated ICU patients), is **frequently missed** (especially the hypoactive form), and is associated with longer stay, functional decline, institutionalisation, dementia, and death.

#### Core features (DSM-5)

1. **Disturbance in attention** (reduced ability to direct, focus, sustain, shift attention) and **awareness.**
2. Develops over a **short period (hours to days),** represents a change from baseline, and **fluctuates** during the day.
3. An **additional cognitive disturbance** (memory, orientation, language, visuospatial, perception).
4. Not better explained by pre-existing neurocognitive disorder, and not in the context of severely reduced arousal such as coma.
5. Evidence of a **medical cause** (illness, drug, toxin, withdrawal).

#### Subtypes

- **Hyperactive:** agitation, restlessness, hallucinations, sleep-wake reversal, wandering, pulling at lines.
- **Hypoactive:** drowsiness, apathy, withdrawal, slowed speech and movement; **most commonly missed and has a worse prognosis.**
- **Mixed:** fluctuation between the two.

#### Risk factors

| Predisposing | Precipitating |
|---|---|
| Age >65, **dementia or cognitive impairment,** previous delirium, frailty, sensory impairment (vision, hearing), polypharmacy, depression, alcohol misuse, severe illness or comorbidity, malnutrition, functional dependence | **Infection** (urinary, chest, others), **medications** (anticholinergics, benzodiazepines, opioids, sedatives, steroids, antipsychotics, anticonvulsants, H₂ blockers, antiparkinsonians, polypharmacy), **surgery and anaesthesia,** **pain,** **dehydration, electrolyte disturbance,** **urinary retention, constipation,** hypoxia, metabolic disturbance (glucose, uraemia, liver failure), stroke, hospital environment (admission, ICU, restraints, catheters, sleep deprivation, change of surroundings), withdrawal (alcohol, benzodiazepines, nicotine), myocardial infarction, heart failure, severe anaemia |

#### Recognition: screening tools

- **4AT** (rapid assessment test for delirium and cognitive impairment): (1) **A**lertness (normal 0; abnormal 4), (2) **AMT4** (age, date of birth, place, year; 0 errors = 0, 1 error = 1, ≥2 errors or untestable = 2), (3) **A**ttention (months of the year backwards: ≥7 months correct = 0; <7 or refuses = 1; untestable = 2), (4) **A**cute change or fluctuating course (yes = 4). Score ≥4 suggests possible delirium; 1–3 possible cognitive impairment.
- **Confusion Assessment Method (CAM):** delirium is likely when there is **(1) acute onset and fluctuating course, AND (2) inattention, AND either (3) disorganised thinking OR (4) altered level of consciousness.** CAM-ICU for ventilated patients.
- Always ask relatives or carers about the baseline cognition ("Is she usually like this?").

#### Differentiating delirium, dementia, depression and psychosis

| Feature | Delirium | Dementia | Depression | Psychosis (functional) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Onset** | Acute (hours–days) | Insidious (months–years) | Weeks–months | Days–weeks |
| **Course** | Fluctuating, worse at night | Progressive, stable day to day | Diurnal variation | Persistent |
| **Attention** | Impaired | Preserved until late | May be reduced (poor concentration) | Variable |
| **Consciousness** | Altered | Clear until late | Clear | Clear |
| **Hallucinations** | Visual common | Late | Rare (mood-congruent) | Auditory common |
| **Reversibility** | Often reversible | Usually irreversible | Reversible | Variable |

Delirium superimposed on dementia is common (a clue is acute change from baseline).

#### Investigation

Targeted to the history and examination (**"look for the cause" systematically**):

- **Vital signs, glucose, hydration, pain assessment,** urinary retention (bladder scan), faecal impaction (rectal examination).
- **Bloods:** FBC, U&E, calcium, glucose, LFTs, CRP, TSH, B12, folate, blood cultures if febrile, **medication review.**
- **Urine dipstick** (interpret carefully: asymptomatic bacteriuria is common and not a cause of delirium by itself; Chapters 2 and 32).
- **ECG,** troponin, chest X-ray, oxygen saturation, blood gas.
- **CT head** if focal signs, history of falls or head injury, anticoagulation, or no cause found; **LP, EEG, MRI** if suspected CNS infection, seizures or unexplained persistent delirium.

#### Management

1. **Identify and treat the cause(s):** infection, hypoxia, electrolyte and glucose disturbance, pain (analgesia), urinary retention, constipation, dehydration, withdrawal, drugs (**stop or reduce** deliriogenic drugs, especially anticholinergics and benzodiazepines).
2. **Non-pharmacological measures (first line):** re-orientation (clocks, calendars, familiar objects, repeated explanations), **glasses, hearing aids, dentures,** promote sleep (quiet, dark at night; light and activity in the day; avoid unnecessary night-time interventions), **mobilise early,** hydration and nutrition, **involve family** and consistent staff, avoid physical restraints, urinary catheters and tethers where possible, a calm environment, reduce noise, treat pain.
3. **Medications for distress:** only if the patient is **distressed or endangering themselves or others** and non-drug measures have failed: **low-dose haloperidol** (0.5–1 mg oral or IM; ECG for QTc) or an atypical antipsychotic (e.g. olanzapine, quetiapine) for the shortest time; **avoid in Parkinson disease and Lewy body dementia** (use quetiapine, or low-dose lorazepam; **benzodiazepines** are first-line for **alcohol and sedative withdrawal** delirium and in seizures). Routine use of antipsychotics or benzodiazepines is discouraged.
4. **Communicate:** inform patients and family that delirium is common, often reversible, and may take weeks to resolve; follow up after discharge (cognitive screening in 4–6 weeks since persistent cognitive impairment may reveal underlying dementia).
5. **Prevention** (multicomponent programmes, e.g. HELP: orientation, early mobilisation, hydration, sleep, vision and hearing aids) reduces incidence by about a third.

### 33.8 Dementia and subacute confusion (brief)

When confusion is subacute or progressive over weeks to months, consider: dementia (Alzheimer's, vascular, Lewy body, frontotemporal), **reversible causes** (hypothyroidism, B12 deficiency, depression, normal pressure hydrocephalus (gait disturbance, urinary incontinence, cognitive decline), subdural haematoma, medications, alcohol, neurosyphilis, HIV, chronic infections, tumours, autoimmune encephalitis, paraneoplastic limbic encephalitis), and **rapidly progressive dementias** (CJD, autoimmune, vasculitis). See Chapter 51.

# Part C: Seizures

### 33.9 Definitions and classification

- **Seizure:** transient occurrence of signs and/or symptoms due to abnormal excessive or synchronous neuronal activity in the brain.
- **Epilepsy:** a disease of the brain defined by (1) **at least two unprovoked (or reflex) seizures occurring >24 hours apart;** (2) one unprovoked seizure and a probability of further seizures similar to the general recurrence risk (at least 60%) after two unprovoked seizures; or (3) diagnosis of an epilepsy syndrome.
- **Provoked (acute symptomatic) seizure:** seizure occurring in close temporal relation to an acute insult (metabolic derangement, drug or alcohol withdrawal, stroke, CNS infection, head trauma, eclampsia); **does not by itself indicate epilepsy.**
- **Status epilepticus:** see 33.15.

#### ILAE 2017 classification of seizure types

| Onset | Subtypes |
|---|---|
| **Focal onset** (originating in one hemisphere) | **Aware** or **impaired awareness;** motor onset (automatisms, clonic, tonic, hyperkinetic, myoclonic, epileptic spasms) or non-motor onset (autonomic, behaviour arrest, cognitive, emotional, sensory); **focal to bilateral tonic–clonic** (formerly secondary generalised) |
| **Generalised onset** | **Motor:** tonic–clonic, clonic, tonic, myoclonic, myoclonic–tonic–clonic, atonic, epileptic spasms; **non-motor (absence):** typical, atypical, myoclonic, eyelid myoclonia |
| **Unknown onset** | Motor (tonic–clonic), non-motor, unclassified |

**Aura** is a focal aware seizure that can precede evolution to other seizures (rising epigastric sensation, déjà vu, fear, olfactory or gustatory hallucinations, visual phenomena, paraesthesia).

### 33.10 Causes of seizures

| Category | Examples |
|---|---|
| **Metabolic and toxic (provoked)** | Hypoglycaemia, hyperglycaemia (hyperosmolar), hyponatraemia, hypernatraemia, hypocalcaemia, hypomagnesaemia, uraemia, hepatic failure, hypoxia, **alcohol intoxication and withdrawal, benzodiazepine or barbiturate withdrawal,** drug toxicity (tramadol, bupropion, theophylline, tricyclics, isoniazid, penicillins and carbapenems in high dose, clozapine, lithium, cocaine, amphetamines, flumazenil, local anaesthetics) |
| **Structural** | **Stroke** (ischaemic and haemorrhagic, including post-stroke epilepsy), tumour, head trauma, vascular malformation, cortical dysplasia, hippocampal sclerosis, cerebral venous thrombosis, neurocysticercosis (endemic areas), abscess, hypoxic-ischaemic injury, neurodegenerative disease (Alzheimer's) |
| **Infective and inflammatory** | Meningitis, encephalitis, cerebral malaria, TB, HIV-related, neurosyphilis, autoimmune encephalitis, multiple sclerosis (rare), vasculitis |
| **Genetic** | Idiopathic generalised epilepsies (juvenile myoclonic epilepsy, childhood absence epilepsy), channelopathies, tuberous sclerosis, neurofibromatosis, developmental and epileptic encephalopathies (Dravet) |
| **Pregnancy-related** | **Eclampsia** (seizures with hypertension and proteinuria: magnesium sulphate), PRES, cerebral venous thrombosis |
| **Cardiovascular** | Convulsive syncope (brief myoclonic jerks), arrhythmia (can mimic seizures; Chapter 28) |
| **Febrile seizures** | Children 6 months–5 years (Chapter 59) |
| **Unknown (cryptogenic)** | About half of all epilepsy |

### 33.11 Evaluation of the first seizure

The main questions are: **(1) Was it a seizure?** **(2) Was it provoked?** **(3) Is there a structural, metabolic or infectious cause?** **(4) What is the risk of recurrence, and does the patient need treatment, advice, or both?**

#### Was it a seizure? (distinguishing from syncope and other events)

See Chapter 28. Features favouring **seizure:** aura; **prolonged tonic–clonic activity (>1–2 minutes);** cyanosis; **lateral tongue biting;** **postictal confusion lasting minutes to hours,** drowsiness, headache, muscle aches; incontinence (not specific); **Todd paresis** (transient postictal weakness); amnesia for the event; head turning; unresponsiveness with automatisms. Features favouring **syncope:** trigger and prodrome, pallor, brief (seconds) unresponsiveness, rapid recovery. **Mimics:** convulsive syncope, psychogenic non-epileptic seizures, panic attacks, migraine aura, TIA, movement disorders, REM sleep behaviour disorder, hypoglycaemia, cataplexy.

#### History

- **Detailed event description from the patient and witness** (a phone video is very helpful): circumstances, warning, onset, laterality, duration, features (above), recovery, injuries.
- **Precipitants:** sleep deprivation, alcohol, drugs, flashing lights, fever, illness, menses, stress.
- **Medical history:** head injury, stroke, CNS infection, febrile seizures, neurodevelopmental problems, birth complications, family history of epilepsy, diabetes, cardiac disease, cancer, HIV, pregnancy.
- **Medications and substances:** prescribed, over-the-counter, recreational, alcohol (quantity, last drink), withdrawal.
- **Previous events:** déjà vu episodes, jerks on waking (myoclonic seizures), brief staring spells, nocturnal events.

#### Examination

- Vital signs, temperature, glucose, rhythm; **injuries** (tongue, head, shoulder dislocation), signs of alcohol or drug use, neurocutaneous stigmata (café-au-lait spots, ash-leaf macules, facial angiofibromas), neurological examination (focal deficits, papilloedema, meningism), cardiovascular examination (murmurs, orthostatic BP), signs of systemic disease, pregnancy.

#### Investigations

| Test | Purpose |
|---|---|
| **Glucose, electrolytes (Na, Ca, Mg), renal and liver function** | Metabolic cause |
| **FBC, CRP, blood cultures** (if febrile), **toxicology, alcohol level, pregnancy test** | Infection, drugs, pregnancy |
| **ECG** | Arrhythmia, long QT, conduction disease (all patients with TLOC) |
| **CK, lactate, prolactin** (within 10–20 minutes, limited use) | Support seizure; not diagnostic |
| **EEG** (ideally within 24–72 hours, sleep-deprived if routine is normal) | Epileptiform abnormalities support epilepsy and syndrome classification (interictal abnormality in up to 50% on the first routine EEG; a normal EEG does not exclude epilepsy; EEG abnormalities occur in about 2–3% of healthy people) |
| **MRI brain with epilepsy protocol** | Structural lesions (tumour, hippocampal sclerosis, cortical dysplasia, vascular malformation, post-stroke gliosis); preferred over CT |
| **CT head (non-contrast)** | Emergency: trauma, focal deficit, persistent altered consciousness, anticoagulation, immunosuppression, suspected haemorrhage, suspected mass |
| **Lumbar puncture** | Suspected meningitis/encephalitis, fever, immunosuppression, persistent altered mental status |
| **Video-EEG monitoring** | Diagnostic uncertainty (psychogenic seizures), surgical evaluation |
| **Other** | Autoimmune and paraneoplastic antibodies, genetic testing (selected), HIV, neurocysticercosis serology |

#### Risk of recurrence

After a **first unprovoked seizure,** the risk of recurrence is about **30–50% over 2 years,** greatest in the first 6–12 months. Higher with: **abnormal EEG (epileptiform), structural brain lesion,** prior brain insult, nocturnal seizure, focal neurological deficit, and a first seizure presenting as status epilepticus. Lower with a normal MRI and EEG.

### 33.12 Management of epilepsy

#### When to start antiseizure medication (ASM)

- Generally after a **second unprovoked seizure.** After a first seizure, treatment is considered when the **risk of recurrence is high** (epileptiform EEG, structural lesion, nocturnal seizure, neurological deficit) or the **consequences of a recurrence are serious** (employment, driving, social circumstances), after informed discussion with the patient. Immediate treatment after a first seizure reduces early recurrence but does not change long-term remission.
- **Provoked seizures:** treat the cause; no long-term ASM usually.

#### Choice of drug

Selection depends on seizure type or syndrome, sex and childbearing potential, age, comorbidities, drug interactions, and patient preference. Common choices (always follow current local guidelines):

| Seizure type | First-line options |
|---|---|
| **Focal** | Lamotrigine, levetiracetam, carbamazepine, oxcarbazepine, lacosamide, zonisamide |
| **Generalised tonic–clonic (genetic generalised epilepsy)** | Lamotrigine, levetiracetam; **valproate** is most effective but **should be avoided in girls and women who could become pregnant** (high risk of congenital malformations and neurodevelopmental effects) unless other options fail and a pregnancy prevention programme is in place |
| **Absence** | Ethosuximide, valproate, lamotrigine |
| **Myoclonic (e.g. juvenile myoclonic epilepsy)** | Valproate (with the caveat above), levetiracetam, lamotrigine (may worsen myoclonus in some), topiramate |

**Principles:** start with monotherapy, titrate slowly, aim for seizure freedom with the lowest effective dose; check adherence and trigger factors; treat for typically 2 years of seizure freedom before considering withdrawal (individualised); **drug-resistant epilepsy** (failure of two appropriate ASMs) → refer to a specialist centre for evaluation of epilepsy surgery, vagus nerve stimulation, dietary therapy (ketogenic diet).

**Adverse effects and interactions:** enzyme-inducing drugs (carbamazepine, phenytoin, phenobarbital) reduce the effectiveness of hormonal contraception, warfarin, DOACs, and others, and cause osteoporosis; valproate: weight gain, tremor, hair loss, hepatotoxicity, polycystic ovary-like changes, teratogenicity; carbamazepine: hyponatraemia, rash (HLA-B*1502 in some Asian populations), leucopenia; lamotrigine: rash (Stevens–Johnson syndrome; slow titration, valproate interaction); levetiracetam: irritability, depression; topiramate: cognitive slowing, renal stones, glaucoma; phenytoin: gum hyperplasia, ataxia, narrow therapeutic index.

#### Counselling and safety

- **Driving:** follow local legal requirements (typically a seizure-free period before resuming driving; stricter for commercial drivers). Provide written advice and document.
- **Safety measures:** showers rather than baths, supervision for swimming, caution with heights, cooking and machinery; sleep hygiene; avoid excessive alcohol; recognise triggers; **medication adherence**; do not stop ASMs abruptly.
- **Pregnancy and women's health:** pre-conception counselling, **folic acid** (higher dose per national guidance), choose safer drugs (lamotrigine, levetiracetam), monitor levels (lamotrigine levels fall in pregnancy), enzyme inducers and contraception, catamenial epilepsy.
- **SUDEP** (sudden unexpected death in epilepsy): the risk is highest in uncontrolled generalised tonic–clonic seizures, nocturnal seizures, and non-adherence; counsel respectfully.
- **Psychosocial:** depression, anxiety, stigma, cognitive effects, employment, education; epilepsy nurse and specialist support.
- **Seizure first aid for the family:** note the time, protect from injury, place in the recovery position when the convulsions stop, do not restrain or put anything in the mouth; **call emergency services if the seizure lasts >5 minutes, repeated seizures without recovery, injury, difficulty breathing, first seizure, or pregnancy.** Rescue medication (buccal midazolam, intranasal midazolam, rectal diazepam) can be prescribed for prolonged seizures.

### 33.13 Special populations in epilepsy and seizures

- **Older adults:** new-onset epilepsy is common (stroke, neurodegenerative disease, tumours). Seizures may present as transient confusion, falls, or lapses; evaluate carefully, use drugs with fewer interactions (lamotrigine, levetiracetam), and beware of cognitive and bone side effects.
- **Pregnancy:** eclampsia is the commonest cause of seizures in the third trimester or puerperium: treat with **magnesium sulphate** (loading 4–6 g IV, then maintenance) and control hypertension; do not use benzodiazepines as first-line unless magnesium is unavailable or seizures persist.
- **Children:** febrile seizures; infantile spasms (West syndrome: emergency; EEG hypsarrhythmia; ACTH or vigabatrin); absence epilepsy; benign rolandic epilepsy; see Chapter 59.
- **Alcohol-related seizures:** withdrawal seizures (24–48 hours after the last drink) and alcohol-related epilepsy; treat with benzodiazepines and thiamine; chronic antiseizure treatment is generally not required for withdrawal seizures alone.
- **Brain tumour, stroke, head injury:** seizure prophylaxis not routinely given, treat seizures when they occur.

---

### 33.14 Functional (psychogenic non-epileptic) seizures

**Functional or dissociative seizures** (previously psychogenic non-epileptic seizures, PNES) are episodes resembling epileptic seizures, but not caused by abnormal electrical discharges; they are a genuine and common condition (up to 20–30% of patients referred to epilepsy centres) associated with trauma, anxiety, depression, personality factors, and sometimes coexisting epilepsy (about 10–20%).

**Features that favour functional seizures** (none is perfectly discriminating):

- **Long duration (>2 minutes, often >5–10 minutes), waxing and waning intensity,** with **asynchronous or side-to-side movements,** pelvic thrusting, **eyes tightly closed** during the event (eyes open in most epileptic seizures), **ictal crying,** **memory recall of the event,** rapid recovery without postictal confusion, emotional or situational triggers, occurrence in the presence of witnesses, absence of injury (although injuries may occur), resistance to eye opening, absence of tongue biting (lateral), no raised prolactin.
- **Diagnosis:** **video-EEG monitoring** capturing typical events with no epileptiform correlate is the gold standard; refer to specialist services.
- **Management:** clear and positive explanation (real, not "faking," involuntary), psychological therapy (CBT-informed), treat comorbid anxiety and depression, stop unnecessary ASMs if epilepsy is excluded; avoid repeated ED visits and escalation to anaesthetic treatment for non-epileptic status.

### 33.15 Status epilepticus

**Definition (operational):** a seizure lasting **≥5 minutes** (convulsive) or **≥2 seizures without return to baseline between them.** A life-threatening emergency with mortality and morbidity rising with duration. Convulsive status epilepticus requires immediate treatment; refractory status epilepticus is associated with higher mortality.

#### Causes

Non-adherence to or withdrawal of ASMs (common in known epilepsy), alcohol withdrawal, acute stroke or haemorrhage, CNS infection, hypoglycaemia, hyponatraemia, hypocalcaemia, drug toxicity, head trauma, brain tumour, anoxic brain injury, sepsis, eclampsia.

#### Time-based management (adults; verify against local protocol)

| Time | Actions |
|---|---|
| **0–5 minutes (stabilise)** | ABC, oxygen, **check glucose** (treat hypoglycaemia with IV glucose, and thiamine if at risk), IV access, monitoring, bloods (gas, glucose, electrolytes, calcium, magnesium, drug levels, toxicology, pregnancy test), **time the seizure**, protect from injury |
| **5–20 minutes (first-line: benzodiazepine)** | **IV lorazepam 4 mg** (0.1 mg/kg; may repeat once after 5–10 minutes) **or IM midazolam 10 mg** (>40 kg; 5 mg if 13–40 kg) **or IV diazepam 10 mg** (repeat once) **or buccal midazolam 10 mg / intranasal midazolam / rectal diazepam 10–20 mg** if no IV access |
| **20–40 minutes (second-line: IV antiseizure drug)** | **Levetiracetam** 60 mg/kg (max 4,500 mg), or **fosphenytoin** 20 mg PE/kg (max 1,500 mg PE) (cardiac monitoring), or **valproate** 40 mg/kg (max 3,000 mg) (avoid in women of childbearing potential, liver disease, mitochondrial disease), or phenobarbital 15 mg/kg; treat cause (antibiotics/aciclovir, correct metabolic disturbance, naloxone, etc.) |
| **>40 minutes (refractory status epilepticus)** | **ICU admission and general anaesthesia** with propofol, midazolam, or thiopental (barbiturate), with continuous EEG monitoring (burst suppression or seizure suppression), ventilation, and haemodynamic support; consider ketamine, further ASMs, steroids/immunotherapy for autoimmune causes |

- After seizure control: continue ASM, identify the cause, **CT/MRI,** LP if infection suspected, EEG (non-convulsive status may continue after convulsions stop: persistent unresponsiveness warrants EEG).
- **Complications:** hypoxia, aspiration, rhabdomyolysis, hyperthermia, lactic acidosis, raised ICP, cardiac arrhythmia, neuronal injury.
- **Non-convulsive status:** confusion or coma with subtle signs; EEG diagnosis; treat as above, with the aggressiveness depending on the context.

### 33.16 Worked cases

#### Case 1: Reduced consciousness: hypoglycaemia

*A 58-year-old man with type 2 diabetes on glibenclamide is found drowsy and sweaty, with right-sided weakness. GCS 11. Capillary glucose 1.8 mmol/L (32 mg/dL).*

**Reasoning.** Hypoglycaemia (sulfonylurea) can mimic stroke with focal deficits.

**Action.** IV glucose; thiamine if alcohol use; recheck glucose; the focal deficit resolves with correction; admit because sulfonylurea effects last long; infusion of dextrose and monitoring for 24+ hours; review medication, consider octreotide in refractory sulfonylurea hypoglycaemia; investigate cause (infection, renal impairment, drug interaction).

#### Case 2: Opioid overdose

*A 29-year-old woman is found unresponsive on a bathroom floor with a needle beside her. RR 6, SpO₂ 78%, pupils 1 mm and reactive, GCS 6.*

**Reasoning.** **Opioid toxidrome.**

**Action.** Airway and bag-valve-mask ventilation with oxygen, naloxone IV/IM/intranasal titrated to respiratory rate (avoid precipitating severe withdrawal and agitation), monitor for recurrent sedation (observe at least 4–6 hours after naloxone for short-acting opioids, longer for long-acting or fentanyl analogues), check paracetamol and co-ingestants, glucose, ECG; provide harm-reduction support and take-home naloxone.

#### Case 3: HSV encephalitis

*A 41-year-old woman has 4 days of fever, headache and personality change, and then a focal seizure with speech difficulty. GCS 13, temperature 38.5 °C, expressive dysphasia. CT is normal; MRI shows right > left temporal lobe T2/FLAIR hyperintensity. CSF: 120 lymphocytes/µL, 250 red cells/µL, protein 1.2 g/L, glucose normal; HSV-1 PCR positive.*

**Reasoning.** **HSV encephalitis.**

**Action.** Started on IV aciclovir 10 mg/kg every 8 hours at presentation (before the results) for 14–21 days with hydration and renal monitoring; seizure control; ICU if declining; rehabilitation for cognitive sequelae. *Lesson:* treat empirically before the test results.

#### Case 4: Delirium in an older patient

*An 83-year-old woman with mild dementia, 2 days after a hip fracture repair, becomes agitated at night, sees "people in the room," and by day is drowsy and inattentive. 4AT score is 11. Temperature 37.2 °C, SpO₂ 90% on air, creatinine up from 90 to 140 µmol/L, urinary catheter in situ, last bowel movement 6 days ago, on opioids and hyoscine.*

**Reasoning.** **Post-operative delirium** with multiple precipitants: hypoxia (possible atelectasis/pneumonia), acute kidney injury and dehydration, opioids and anticholinergics, constipation, catheter, pain, sleep disruption.

**Action.** Treat each: oxygen and chest assessment, fluids, review opioids (substitute regular paracetamol, nerve block), stop anticholinergics, treat constipation, remove catheter early, glasses and hearing aids, family presence, sleep hygiene, early mobilisation; avoid antipsychotics unless distress or danger; follow-up cognitive assessment.

#### Case 5: Alcohol withdrawal and Wernicke encephalopathy

*A 52-year-old man with heavy alcohol use is admitted with a leg infection. On day 3 he is tremulous, sweating, hallucinating (insects on the walls), HR 118, temperature 37.9 °C, and confused. He is unsteady on walking and has horizontal nystagmus.*

**Reasoning.** **Delirium tremens** at 48–72 hours, plus features of **Wernicke encephalopathy** (confusion, ataxia, nystagmus).

**Action.** **High-dose IV thiamine** (before glucose), benzodiazepines per CIWA-Ar (diazepam/chlordiazepoxide; lorazepam if liver disease), fluids and electrolyte repletion (magnesium, potassium, phosphate), treat infection, monitor in a high-dependency setting, seizure precautions; refer for alcohol dependence treatment.

#### Case 6: Hepatic encephalopathy

*A 60-year-old man with cirrhosis, on furosemide and spironolactone, has had 3 days of vomiting, then becomes drowsy and disoriented with a flapping tremor. Na 123, K 3.0, creatinine 170, WBC 18, ascites with abdominal tenderness.*

**Reasoning.** **Grade 2–3 hepatic encephalopathy,** precipitated by dehydration, electrolyte disturbance, AKI and probable **spontaneous bacterial peritonitis.**

**Action.** Diagnostic paracentesis, antibiotics and albumin, stop diuretics, correct potassium (cautiously with the sodium), lactulose (oral/enema), rifaximin, search for bleeding, avoid sedatives, protect airway if GCS falls.

#### Case 7: First seizure with a structural cause

*A 54-year-old man, a smoker, has a first generalised tonic–clonic seizure at home, with a left-sided headache over the previous 2 weeks and mild left-hand clumsiness afterward. CT: right frontal ring-enhancing lesion with oedema.*

**Reasoning.** New-onset seizure in a middle-aged adult with focal features: structural lesion (**metastasis, glioma, abscess**).

**Action.** Start an ASM (levetiracetam), dexamethasone for oedema, MRI brain with contrast, CT chest/abdomen/pelvis, neuro-oncology and neurosurgical referral; driving advice.

#### Case 8: Status epilepticus

*A 33-year-old man with known epilepsy, who stopped his tablets 3 days ago, has had a generalised tonic–clonic seizure for 8 minutes when the ambulance arrives. Glucose 6.2 mmol/L.*

**Reasoning.** **Convulsive status epilepticus** (>5 minutes).

**Action.** Airway, oxygen, IV access; **IV lorazepam 4 mg** (or IM midazolam 10 mg if no IV access); repeat once if seizures continue; second-line levetiracetam 60 mg/kg or fosphenytoin; check for causes (non-adherence is likely), consider ICU if refractory; resume ASM.

#### Case 9: Functional seizures

*A 24-year-old woman with a history of childhood abuse has been admitted several times with "refractory status epilepticus" lasting 30–60 minutes, with side-to-side head shaking, closed eyes, and thrashing, with preserved responsiveness at times, normal gases, and no postictal confusion. EEG during events shows normal background activity with movement artefact.*

**Reasoning.** **Functional (dissociative) seizures.**

**Action.** Video-EEG confirmation, a clear non-judgemental explanation, psychological therapy, withdrawal of unnecessary ASMs and avoidance of intubation based on semiology alone; coordinate with neurology and mental health services.

#### Case 10: Eclampsia

*A 27-year-old woman at 36 weeks has a headache, visual disturbance and a generalised seizure. BP 178/112, 3+ proteinuria, hyperreflexia.*

**Reasoning.** **Eclampsia.**

**Action.** Airway, left lateral position, **magnesium sulphate** (4 g IV over 5–15 minutes, then 1 g/hour), antihypertensive (labetalol or nifedipine), delivery planning with obstetric team, monitor for magnesium toxicity (loss of reflexes, respiratory depression: calcium gluconate).

### 33.17 Common pitfalls

- **Not checking glucose** in any altered consciousness.
- **Anchoring on alcohol or "psychiatric" explanations** in patients with reduced consciousness or confusion.
- **Giving glucose before thiamine** in at-risk patients, or withholding glucose in hypoglycaemia.
- **Delaying antibiotics and aciclovir** while awaiting CT or LP.
- **Failing to document GCS components** and to reassess regularly.
- **Missing delirium** (especially hypoactive) and attributing changes to dementia or "ageing."
- **Using antipsychotics or benzodiazepines** as first-line for delirium.
- **Overreliance on a urine dipstick** in explaining confusion.
- **Ignoring medication causes** (anticholinergics, opioids, benzodiazepines).
- **Mistaking convulsive syncope for epilepsy** (and vice versa).
- **Stopping at a normal EEG or CT** when epilepsy is suspected; MRI is more sensitive.
- **Prescribing valproate** to women of childbearing potential without a pregnancy prevention programme.
- **Under-treating status epilepticus** (small benzodiazepine doses, delay).
- **Missing non-convulsive status epilepticus** in persistently altered patients.
- **Intubating and escalating treatment for functional seizures** without video-EEG confirmation.
- **Forgetting driving and safety advice.**

### 33.18 Key points

- In reduced consciousness: ABCDE, glucose, naloxone, thiamine, antibiotics and aciclovir, treat seizures; get a collateral history; examine pupils, eye movements, respiratory pattern and motor responses; consider herniation.
- Document GCS by component; GCS ≤8 requires airway protection.
- Structural lesions tend to produce focal signs and asymmetric pupils; metabolic and toxic causes tend to be symmetrical with preserved pupillary reflexes (except opioids, which produce miosis).
- Delirium: acute onset, fluctuating attention and awareness; screen with 4AT/CAM; find and treat causes (infection, drugs, retention, constipation, pain, dehydration); non-drug measures first.
- Alcohol: treat withdrawal (benzodiazepines), give IV thiamine for suspected Wernicke encephalopathy.
- A first seizure needs a decision on whether it was a seizure, provoked, or caused by a structural lesion; investigate with glucose, electrolytes, ECG, EEG and MRI.
- Start ASMs after a second unprovoked seizure (or earlier with high recurrence risk); avoid valproate in women of childbearing potential; give driving, safety and pregnancy advice.
- Status epilepticus: time-based treatment with early adequate benzodiazepine, then a second-line drug, then anaesthesia if refractory.
- Recognise functional seizures and non-convulsive status epilepticus.

### 33.19 Self-assessment

1. Describe the three components of the GCS and the significance of a score of 8.
   *Answer: Eye opening (1–4), verbal response (1–5), motor response (1–6); GCS ≤8 indicates severe impairment where the airway is at risk and intubation should be considered.*

2. Which pupillary finding suggests uncal herniation, and which an opioid or pontine cause?
   *Answer: A unilateral dilated, fixed pupil suggests uncal herniation (third nerve compression); pinpoint pupils suggest opioids, pontine haemorrhage, or cholinergic poisoning.*

3. State the CAM criteria for delirium.
   *Answer: Acute onset and fluctuating course AND inattention, plus either disorganised thinking or altered level of consciousness.*

4. Why must thiamine be given with or before glucose in at-risk patients?
   *Answer: Glucose metabolism consumes thiamine and may precipitate or worsen Wernicke encephalopathy in thiamine-deficient patients.*

5. List features favouring epileptic over psychogenic seizures.
   *Answer: Brief duration (1–2 minutes), stereotyped synchronised movements, eyes open, cyanosis, lateral tongue biting, postictal confusion, injury, raised lactate/prolactin shortly afterward.*

6. What is the first-line treatment for status epilepticus at 5–20 minutes?
   *Answer: A benzodiazepine: IV lorazepam 4 mg (repeat once), or IM midazolam 10 mg, or IV diazepam, or buccal/intranasal/rectal alternatives if no IV access.*
