# Chapter 68. Point-of-Care and Rapid Tests

## Learning objectives

After studying this chapter you should be able to:

1. Define point-of-care testing (POCT), list its advantages and limitations, and explain why quality assurance, operator training and result documentation are essential.
2. Apply the concepts of sensitivity, specificity, predictive value and likelihood ratios to rapid tests, especially the effect of disease prevalence and timing.
3. Use and interpret bedside tests: capillary glucose and ketones, blood gases and lactate, haemoglobin, INR, troponin and D-dimer, urinalysis and pregnancy tests, pulse oximetry and peak flow.
4. Use and interpret rapid infection tests: Group A streptococcus antigen, influenza/RSV/SARS-CoV-2 antigen and PCR, malaria and dengue rapid tests, HIV and hepatitis tests, urinary antigens, *C. difficile*, CRP and procalcitonin at the point of care.
5. Recognise the pitfalls: wrong specimen, timing (window period), interferences, hook effect, poor performance in specific populations, over-reliance on a negative result.
6. Decide when a rapid test is sufficient, when it must be confirmed, and when to proceed directly to treatment without waiting for results.
7. Interpret self-tests and consumer devices (home glucose, pregnancy, COVID/flu tests, wearable ECG) and advise patients on their limits.
8. Communicate results, handle false positives and false negatives, and manage notification duties.

> **Note.** Manufacturer sensitivities and specificities vary by brand and are updated frequently. The figures given are typical ranges and illustrate principles; consult local laboratory guidance and current product inserts.

## 1. Point-of-care testing: concepts

### 1.1 Definition and settings

Point-of-care testing (POCT, near-patient testing) is analysis performed at or near the patient, with results available within minutes, allowing immediate clinical decisions. Settings: emergency department, ICU, wards, ambulance, primary care, pharmacies, remote and low-resource clinics, home (self-testing).

### 1.2 Advantages and disadvantages

| Advantages | Limitations |
|---|---|
| Fast results; shorter length of stay; earlier treatment (sepsis, MI, hypoglycaemia) | Less accurate than central laboratory analysers for some analytes |
| Small sample volumes (capillary blood); useful in children | Interferences (haematocrit, oxygen, drugs, ascorbic acid, maltose) |
| Allows testing when laboratories are unavailable | Operator-dependent (training, technique, reading of lateral flow) |
| Patient engagement (self-monitoring) | Cost per test is higher; risk of over-testing |
| Supports public health (outbreak control) | Documentation, quality control, connectivity, infection control, waste |
| | Missed reporting duties (notifiable diseases) |

### 1.3 Quality framework

- **Accreditation** (ISO 22870/15189) and governance by the laboratory; **training and competency assessment** of users; **internal quality control** with each batch/day; **external quality assessment (EQA)**; **lot checks** and storage (temperature); **result transmission** to the electronic record; **clear thresholds** and escalation rules; **device cleaning**.
- Verify that the **specimen type** matches the test (capillary vs venous vs arterial blood; nasopharyngeal swab vs throat swab vs saliva).
- A POCT result discordant with the clinical picture should be **repeated and confirmed in the laboratory**.

### 1.4 Statistical principles revisited

| Parameter | Meaning for the clinician |
|---|---|
| **Sensitivity** | Chance a person with disease tests positive. Rapid tests are often *less sensitive* than laboratory PCR/culture. A negative result in a symptomatic, high-probability patient does not rule out disease |
| **Specificity** | Chance a person without disease tests negative. High specificity means a positive test is usually true when prevalence is high |
| **Prevalence and PPV/NPV** | In low prevalence, PPV falls and many positives are false; in high prevalence, NPV falls and negatives are less reliable |
| **Likelihood ratios** | LR+ and LR− convert pre-test to post-test probability |
| **Timing** | Antigen tests peak when viral load is highest (first 5–7 days); antibody tests need time (window period) |
| **Spectrum bias** | Performance in symptomatic hospital patients differs from screening asymptomatic people |

**Example**: Rapid influenza antigen test (sensitivity 60%, specificity 98%). During the epidemic with pre-test probability 40%: positive result post-test probability ≈ 92%; negative result ≈ 23% (still high). Out of season (pre-test 2%): positive post-test probability ≈ 38%; negative ≈ 1%. The same test, very different meaning.

## 2. Bedside metabolic and haematological tests

### 2.1 Capillary glucose and ketones

- **Glucose meters** (capillary blood): accuracy ±15% (ISO 15197:2013 requires ≥ 95% of results within ±15% above 5.6 mmol/L, ±0.83 mmol/L below). Interferences: haematocrit (anaemia reads high, polycythaemia low), hypotension/peripheral shock (capillary samples unreliable; use arterial/venous), oxygen therapy (some enzyme systems), high-dose vitamin C, paracetamol, icodextrin (peritoneal dialysis fluid: falsely high with GDH-PQQ strips, risk of unrecognised hypoglycaemia), maltose, galactose, cold, dirty or wet fingers (contamination with sugar).
- **Hypoglycaemia** (< 4.0 mmol/L in treated diabetes; < 3.0 clinically significant; < 2.5 serious): immediate treatment (15–20 g fast carbohydrate orally, or IV 20% glucose 75–100 mL or glucagon 1 mg IM if unable to swallow) and **confirm** with venous plasma glucose only when it won't delay treatment. Find the cause (sulfonylurea or insulin, alcohol, liver failure, adrenal insufficiency, sepsis, insulinoma).
- **Hyperglycaemia**: > 11 mmol/L; if > 13–14 mmol/L, check **ketones** (blood β-hydroxybutyrate): 0.6–1.5 mmol/L (raised, risk), 1.5–3.0 (high risk of DKA), > 3.0 mmol/L (suggests DKA; send venous gas, electrolytes). Urine ketone strips (acetoacetate) lag behind and may miss β-hydroxybutyrate in early DKA. **SGLT2 inhibitor users** can have euglycaemic DKA (glucose < 14 mmol/L with ketones > 3): check ketones in any unwell patient on these drugs, regardless of glucose.
- **Continuous glucose monitors (CGM)** and flash systems: interstitial glucose lags blood glucose by 5–15 minutes; check capillary glucose when symptoms don't match or during rapid change; time in range 3.9–10.0 mmol/L (target > 70%); interference with acetaminophen (some sensors), hydroxyurea, ascorbic acid; MRI, CT and diathermy require removal.

### 2.2 Blood gases, electrolytes and lactate (POCT analysers)

Arterial or venous (or capillary) blood gas analysers give pH, PaCO₂, PaO₂, HCO₃⁻, base excess, lactate, Na, K, Cl, iCa, glucose, haemoglobin and sometimes creatinine within 2 minutes (see chapter 64 for interpretation).

- **Venous gas** is adequate for pH, HCO₃⁻, lactate and K; venous pCO₂ ≈ arterial + 0.8 kPa (6 mmHg) (a normal venous pCO₂ excludes hypercapnia). Not suitable for PaO₂.
- Pre-analytical errors: air bubbles (raise PaO₂, lower PaCO₂), heparin dilution (low pCO₂, low K if liquid heparin), delay in processing (> 15 min: falls pH, glucose, rises lactate), wrong syringe, temperature correction.
- **Lactate** ≥ 2.0 mmol/L (suspected sepsis: hypoperfusion marker), ≥ 4 mmol/L (severe); re-measure within 2–4 hours to guide resuscitation.
- **Whole-blood potassium** on blood gas machines is more accurate than sample delayed in transit (no haemolysis in transport, but check for haemolysed samples).

### 2.3 Haemoglobin and haematocrit

HemoCue-type point-of-care haemoglobin (photometric): within ±5 g/L of laboratory values; capillary samples vary (squeezing the finger dilutes with tissue fluid). Rapid decisions in haemorrhage, pregnancy, anaemia screening in low-resource settings. **Initial Hb may be normal in acute bleeding** (equilibration lag of hours): trend and treat the patient, not the number.

### 2.4 Coagulation: INR and ACT

POCT INR (CoaguChek) calibrated for **warfarin** monitoring in stable patients at home or clinic; accurate within INR 0.8–4.5; reads inaccurately at high INR (> 4.5), in antiphospholipid syndrome, DOAC use, liver disease, DIC, polycythaemia, severe anaemia; confirm high or discordant results with venous INR. **Activated clotting time (ACT)** monitors heparin in cardiac surgery/PCI. **Viscoelastic tests** (TEG/ROTEM) at the bedside guide transfusion in trauma, obstetric haemorrhage and cardiac surgery (clot formation, strength, fibrinolysis).

### 2.5 Cardiac biomarkers

- **Point-of-care high-sensitivity troponin** (some devices: sensitivity and rule-out performance comparable to laboratory hs-cTn for 0/1-hour pathways; cut-offs are assay-specific; use only validated algorithms). Conventional (non-high-sensitivity) POC troponin needs ≥ 6–12 hours from symptom onset to rule out MI.
- **POC BNP/NT-proBNP** for dyspnoea (rule-out thresholds as in chapter 64).
- **POC D-dimer** (quantitative): use only in low or intermediate pre-test probability for VTE (Wells ≤ 4 or "PE unlikely"), age-adjusted cut-off (age × 10 µg/L above 50 years) or YEARS algorithm; invalid in high pre-test probability; semi-quantitative whole-blood agglutination tests are less sensitive. A negative D-dimer in low risk has an NPV > 99%.

### 2.6 Urine tests

- **Dipstick**: leukocyte esterase (LE), nitrite, blood, protein, glucose, ketones, pH, specific gravity, bilirubin, urobilinogen. For UTI: in women with ≥ 2 of dysuria, frequency, no vaginal discharge, dipstick has limited extra value; nitrite + LE positive has PPV ~ 90%; **both negative has NPV ~ 95%** in symptomatic young women. In older adults, dipstick results are unreliable (asymptomatic bacteriuria is present in 15–50% of care-home residents; do not treat without symptoms or signs, chapter 61).
- **Pregnancy test** (urine hCG, sensitivity 20–25 IU/L): detects pregnancy ~10 days after conception; the "hook effect" can give a false negative with very high hCG (molar pregnancy, choriocarcinoma): dilute and repeat; false positives from hCG-secreting tumours, recent miscarriage/termination, hCG injections, rare heterophile antibodies; always perform before imaging, drugs or surgery in women of childbearing age. Serum quantitative β-hCG for ectopic suspicion (chapter 57).
- **Urine drug screens** (immunoassay): presumptive; cross-reactions (e.g., pseudoephedrine, labetalol, bupropion for amphetamines; poppy seeds for opiates; fluoroquinolones for opiates; quetiapine/TCA; ibuprofen/efavirenz/ PPIs for cannabis/benzodiazepines in some assays); positive results do not prove impairment; confirm with mass spectrometry if consequences are significant.
- **Urinary antigens** for *Legionella pneumophila* serogroup 1 (sensitivity 70–80%, specificity > 95%; remains positive for weeks after infection) and *Streptococcus pneumoniae* (sensitivity ~70%, specificity ~95%; false positives in children carriers and recent pneumococcal infection). Positive results guide targeted therapy but do not replace culture/PCR in severe disease.

## 3. Rapid infection tests

### 3.1 Principles and technologies

- **Lateral flow immunoassay (LFA)** detects antigens or antibodies in minutes. **Antigen tests** are best when pathogen load is high (early symptomatic period) and less sensitive than nucleic acid amplification tests (NAAT/PCR) by 20–40% (varying by pathogen). **Antibody tests** show prior exposure and may take days to weeks to turn positive (window period).
- **Near-patient NAAT/PCR** (e.g., GeneXpert, cobas Liat, BioFire, ID NOW, Film Array): high sensitivity (85–99%) with a turnaround of 20–120 minutes; cartridge-based, more costly; reduces unnecessary antibiotics and isolation days.
- **Interpretation**: sensitive PCR may detect non-viable nucleic acid for weeks (SARS-CoV-2) or carriage (e.g., *Streptococcus pneumoniae*, enteroviruses); correlate with symptoms.

### 3.2 Upper respiratory tract

| Test | Performance | Use and caution |
|---|---|---|
| **Group A streptococcus rapid antigen test (RADT)** | Specificity > 95%; sensitivity 80–90% in children (adults similar but lower prevalence) | In children and young adults with high Centor/FeverPAIN score: positive → treat; negative → backup culture/PCR in children (not needed in adults per many guidelines) to avoid missing GAS and rheumatic fever. Carriers exist (up to 10–15% of school-age children) |
| **Influenza A/B rapid antigen** | Sensitivity 50–70%, specificity > 95% | Negative does not exclude; rapid molecular tests (sensitivity 90–95%) preferred in hospitalised patients; **treat high-risk or severe patients with oseltamivir without waiting** (within 48 h ideally, but still benefit in hospitalised patients beyond 48 h) |
| **SARS-CoV-2 antigen** | Sensitivity 60–80% overall (higher with high viral load, symptomatic, days 1–5); specificity > 99% | Repeat after 24–48 h if negative with symptoms and exposure; PCR for hospital admission, immunocompromised, outbreaks; positive antigen in a symptomatic person is reliable |
| **RSV rapid antigen** | Sensitivity 80–90% in infants (low in adults) | Adults: PCR; guides isolation and bronchiolitis care |
| **Multiplex respiratory PCR panels** | Sensitivity 90–99% | Identify viruses and atypical bacteria; detection ≠ causation (co-detections, shedding); beneficial in immunocompromised and ICU; limited effect on outcomes in routine outpatients |

### 3.3 Lower respiratory and sepsis

- **CRP point-of-care** (finger prick, results in 4 minutes): reduces antibiotic prescribing in primary care LRTI (CRP < 20 mg/L → antibiotics unlikely needed; 20–100 → consider delayed prescription; > 100 mg/L → antibiotics likely beneficial, per NICE/GP guidelines). CRP rises after 6–12 hours; early samples may be falsely reassuring.
- **Procalcitonin (PCT)** POC assays: support stopping antibiotics when PCT < 0.25 µg/L or falls by > 80% from peak; do not use to withhold antibiotics in severely ill or unstable patients.
- **Blood cultures** remain the standard. **Rapid molecular blood culture identification** (BioFire BCID2, Verigene, MALDI-TOF on positive bottles) gives species and resistance genes (mecA, vanA, CTX-M, KPC) within 1–3 hours of positivity: faster targeted therapy.
- **Lactate** (see above).

### 3.4 Gastrointestinal

- **Stool antigens/NAAT**: *C. difficile* two-step algorithm (GDH antigen + toxin EIA; discordant results resolved by PCR for toxin gene) to avoid over-diagnosis of colonisation; multiplex GI panels (bacteria, viruses, parasites) reduce time to diagnosis but detect colonisation and low-level carriage; don't test formed stool or patients on laxatives.
- **Norovirus**, **rotavirus** rapid antigen tests: sensitivity moderate; used in outbreaks.
- ***Helicobacter pylori*** stool antigen or urea breath test: sensitivity 90–95%; stop PPIs 2 weeks and antibiotics 4 weeks before; serology is for epidemiological use only (cannot distinguish current from past infection).
- **Faecal immunochemical test (FIT)** for colorectal cancer detection in symptomatic patients (≥ 10 µg Hb/g faeces, NICE): rules out cancer in low-risk patients; quantitative FIT is more informative than qualitative.
- **Faecal calprotectin** (> 250 µg/g suggests IBD; < 50 µg/g makes IBD unlikely); distinguishes IBD from IBS in young patients.
- **Coeliac POC** (tTG): not for diagnosis without laboratory confirmation and normal IgA.

### 3.5 Sexually transmitted and bloodborne infections

- **HIV**: 4th-generation antigen/antibody tests (including POC versions) detect HIV infection 2–4 weeks after exposure; fingerstick rapid antibody-only tests (oral swab and blood) have a window of 3 months and sensitivity ~99% in established infection; a positive screening test requires confirmation (differentiation assay, RNA); **early infection** needs HIV RNA; self-testing kits are available and acceptable; **do not delay PEP** for results; PrEP monitoring includes quarterly testing.
- **Syphilis** (treponemal rapid POC tests: sensitivity 85–95% for treponemal antibodies; cannot differentiate current from past infection; need RPR/VDRL for activity), **hepatitis B** (HBsAg rapid), **hepatitis C** (anti-HCV rapid; reflex HCV RNA), **chlamydia/gonorrhoea NAAT** (self-collected vaginal swab or first-void urine; POC NAAT enables same-visit treatment); **trichomonas POC**; Gram stain of urethral discharge (gonococcal diplococci).
- **Pregnancy and congenital infection screening** in antenatal care (chapter 57): HIV, syphilis, hepatitis B.

### 3.6 Tropical and travel infections

- **Malaria**: any fever after travel to endemic areas is malaria until proven otherwise (within 3 months; up to a year). **Rapid diagnostic tests (RDTs)** detect HRP2 (*P. falciparum*), pLDH/aldolase (all species); sensitivity > 95% at parasitaemia > 100–200/µL for falciparum, lower for non-falciparum and low parasitaemia; HRP2 deletions occur in some regions (Horn of Africa, South America); **a negative RDT must be followed by thick and thin blood films, three samples 12–24 hours apart** (microscopy remains the gold standard for species and parasitaemia, > 2% hyperparasitaemia or signs of severe malaria require IV artesunate). Don't wait for results if severe.
- **Dengue**: NS1 antigen (days 1–5; sensitivity 60–90%), IgM (from day 5), PCR; combined NS1/IgM/IgG RDTs. Warning signs for severe dengue at defervescence (abdominal pain, vomiting, bleeding, rising haematocrit with falling platelets).
- **Typhoid** (blood culture; Typhidot and similar serology are unreliable), **leptospirosis**, **scrub typhus**, **chikungunya**, **Zika**, **Ebola/viral haemorrhagic fever** (isolate; specialist labs), **cholera** rapid tests.
- **Tuberculosis**: **Xpert MTB/RIF Ultra** (sputum; sensitivity ~90% smear-positive, ~70–80% smear-negative, detects rifampicin resistance within 2 hours); urine **LAM** (lateral flow) in advanced HIV (CD4 < 100) improves diagnosis in hospitalised patients; IGRA/TST detect infection (not disease).
- **Rabies, tetanus, diphtheria**: clinical diagnosis; treat on suspicion.

### 3.7 Meningitis and CNS (rapid)

**Do not delay antibiotics** (and dexamethasone) for LP or imaging in suspected bacterial meningitis; POCT adjuncts: CSF Gram stain, latex agglutination (insensitive), CSF lactate > 3.5 mmol/L (supports bacterial), multiplex CSF PCR panel (BioFire ME panel: false positives for *S. pneumoniae*, *Streptococcus agalactiae*, HHV-6; interpret with CSF cell counts), cryptococcal antigen LFA (serum and CSF, sensitivity ~99%).

### 3.8 Skin, wounds, joints

Gram stain of aspirates, rapid MRSA PCR (nasal screening, wound), KOH wet mount for fungi, scabies dermoscopy, Wood's lamp, urine/vaginal wet mount (clue cells, trichomonads, pH > 4.5 for bacterial vaginosis). Synovial fluid analysis includes crystals (polarised microscopy), Gram stain and cell count.

## 4. Physiological and functional bedside tests

- **Pulse oximetry**: SpO₂ accuracy ±2–4% when SaO₂ is 70–100%; reads falsely **high** with carboxyhaemoglobin (smoke, CO poisoning), and in darker skin pigmentation overestimates saturation (occult hypoxaemia roughly 2–3 times more frequent in Black patients; use ABG when decision-critical); falsely low or unreliable with nail polish (blue/black), poor perfusion, motion, methaemoglobinaemia (reads ~85%), cold fingers, severe anaemia, intravenous dyes. A normal SpO₂ does not exclude respiratory failure with hypercapnia or hyperventilation.
- **Peak expiratory flow** (PEF): compare with predicted/best; asthma severity (acute: 50–75% moderate, 33–50% severe, < 33% life-threatening); diary of diurnal variation > 20% supports asthma. Spirometry is the primary test for COPD and restriction (FEV₁/FVC < 0.7 post-bronchodilator; chapter 46).
- **Capnography** (end-tidal CO₂): confirmation of ETT placement, CPR quality (EtCO₂ > 10 mmHg), hypoventilation monitoring.
- **Bladder scanner** (post-void residual).
- **Blood pressure, orthostatic vitals, temperature (tympanic/temporal artery/oral/axillary variability)**.
- **12-lead ECG, single-lead handheld ECG, wearable devices** (see below).
- **Neurological screening scales** (FAST, ROSIER, NIHSS, GCS, 4AT, CAM) and **capillary glucose in all altered consciousness**.
- **Electronic stethoscopes and handheld ultrasound** (chapter 67).

## 5. Self-testing and consumer devices

| Device | Reliability | Advice |
|---|---|---|
| Home blood pressure monitors | Good if validated (BIHS/ESH list), correct cuff size, rested, seated | Take 2 readings morning and evening for 7 days (home average ≥ 135/85 mmHg = hypertension) |
| Home glucose meters/CGM | Good with technique | Confirm unexpected values; send readings to clinic |
| Home pregnancy tests | >99% accurate after missed period | Repeat in 3 days if negative with ongoing amenorrhoea |
| Home COVID-19/flu antigen tests | Sensitivity lower than PCR; best used serially | A negative result does not clear infectivity; follow symptoms and current public-health guidance |
| Smartwatch ECG/PPG for AF | Good sensitivity and specificity for AF in single-lead ECG (≈ 95–98%) but PPG alerts have false positives; false negatives in other arrhythmias | Confirm with 12-lead ECG/Holter; do not treat on a watch alert alone; symptoms like chest pain still require evaluation |
| Home pulse oximeters | Wide error; unreliable in darker skin tones; poor with cold hands | Use trends and symptoms, not a single value |
| Home HIV/hepatitis tests | Window period issues | Confirm positives; ensure linkage to care |
| Direct-to-consumer genetic tests | Not diagnostic; variable validity | Refer for genetic counselling; confirm in a clinical laboratory |
| Apps/AI symptom checkers | Variable | Not a substitute for assessment; red flags override |

Counsel patients on **what a negative result can and cannot mean**, **how to repeat**, **when to seek care regardless of results**, and **how to report** (notifiable diseases).

## 6. Practical decision rules for rapid tests

1. **Treat first when the delay is dangerous**: sepsis, bacterial meningitis, hypoglycaemia, STEMI, suspected severe malaria, tension pneumothorax, necrotising fasciitis, neutropenic fever. Rapid tests refine but do not gate treatment.
2. **A negative rapid test is least reliable when pre-test probability is high.** Retest with a more sensitive method (PCR, culture, repeat in 24–48 h).
3. **A positive rapid test in a low-probability setting needs confirmation** (screening HIV, hCG in a post-menopausal woman, urinary antigens in an asymptomatic person, positive drug screen).
4. **Timing matters**: antigen tests early; antibody tests later; troponin serial; cultures before antibiotics; sputum before treatment where possible.
5. **Match the specimen** (nasopharyngeal vs throat; lower respiratory sample in pneumonia; first-void urine for chlamydia).
6. **Know the notification duties** and isolation implications (TB, measles, meningococcus, HIV partner notification).
7. **Document** the test, lot (if required), result and action.
8. **Avoid test cascades**: do not order multiplex panels reflexively when the result will not change management; detection of a virus does not exclude bacterial co-infection; detection of colonisation does not justify treatment.
9. **Communicate uncertainty**: "This test rules in/out most but not all cases; if you worsen, come back."

## Worked cases

### Case 1: "Confusion" with a glucose of 2.1

**Presentation.** Mr A., 76, type 2 diabetes on gliclazide and insulin, found drowsy and sweating by his daughter. Capillary glucose 2.1 mmol/L. Paramedics gave 150 mL of 10% glucose IV; he awakens.

**Reasoning.** Hypoglycaemia (sulfonylurea and insulin, CKD, poor intake). Sulfonylurea hypoglycaemia is prolonged and recurrent. A single correction is not enough.

**Action.** Admit/observe for at least 12–24 hours (longer if long-acting insulin or renal impairment), regular capillary glucose every 1–2 hours initially, IV 10% glucose infusion if recurrent; consider octreotide for refractory sulfonylurea hypoglycaemia; review diabetes medication (stop gliclazide, reduce insulin, relax HbA1c target in frailty); find the trigger (infection, reduced intake, new drug interaction such as clarithromycin or co-trimoxazole); educate.

**Lessons.** Treat first, confirm later, and anticipate recurrence; reduce overtreatment in older adults.

### Case 2: Sore throat in an adolescent

**Presentation.** A 14-year-old, fever 38.9 °C, tonsillar exudate, tender anterior cervical nodes, no cough (Centor 4). Rapid antigen test (RADT) positive.

**Reasoning.** High pre-test probability + positive test with specificity > 95%: GAS pharyngitis. Antibiotics reduce symptom duration modestly and prevent suppurative complications and rheumatic fever.

**Action.** Phenoxymethylpenicillin 500 mg bd (or amoxicillin) for 5–10 days per local guideline (10 days in many guidelines); analgesia; return if unable to swallow fluids, drooling, trismus (peritonsillar abscess), rash, or no improvement in 48–72 hours. Had the RADT been negative in this child, send a throat culture or PCR to confirm.

**Lessons.** Use clinical scores to decide whom to test; interpret by pre-test probability.

### Case 3: Fever after travel with a "negative malaria test"

**Presentation.** Ms B., 33, returned from Nigeria 12 days ago, fever 39.5 °C, headache, myalgia, vomiting. Malaria RDT in a clinic is negative.

**Reasoning.** Pre-test probability of malaria is high (travel to West Africa, no chemoprophylaxis). A single negative RDT, especially if HRP2-based or at low parasitaemia, does not exclude malaria. Other causes: typhoid, dengue, viral haemorrhagic fever, hepatitis, UTI.

**Action.** Urgent thick and thin blood films repeated at 12–24 hour intervals (three sets) in a laboratory, FBC (thrombocytopenia), CRP, LFT, U&E, glucose, lactate; blood cultures; consider hospital admission and ID advice; start treatment as soon as malaria is confirmed (artemisinin-based combination therapy for uncomplicated; IV artesunate for severe disease: parasitaemia > 2%, impaired consciousness, shock, jaundice, acidosis, renal impairment, hypoglycaemia, respiratory distress). Film shows *P. falciparum* 6% parasitaemia → severe malaria; IV artesunate 2.4 mg/kg at 0, 12, 24 h then daily; HDU monitoring, notification.

**Lessons.** A negative RDT is not enough in a high-risk traveller; microscopy is needed.

### Case 4: A positive pregnancy test and a negative one

**Presentation.** Ms C., 24, 6 weeks amenorrhoea, urine pregnancy test negative twice at home, vaginal spotting, firm uterus enlarged to 14 weeks on examination, with severe vomiting. Laboratory serum β-hCG is 900,000 IU/L.

**Reasoning.** The hook effect: very high β-hCG saturates antibodies in the urine test, producing a falsely negative result. Findings suggest molar pregnancy (hydatidiform mole).

**Action.** Pelvic ultrasound ("snowstorm" or vesicular pattern), check TSH/free T4 (hCG-induced hyperthyroidism), FBC, group and screen; gynaecology for suction evacuation, histology; follow-up of hCG (gestational trophoblastic disease registry).

**Lessons.** If the clinical picture is discordant with a negative pregnancy test, dilute the urine or measure serum hCG.

### Case 5: Chest pain, normal first troponin on a POC device

**Presentation.** Mr D., 59, smoker, 2 hours of central chest pressure, normal ECG. Conventional point-of-care troponin I negative at 2 hours from onset.

**Reasoning.** Conventional POC troponin requires ≥ 6–12 hours from symptom onset to exclude MI; a negative result at 2 hours does not rule out NSTEMI. If hs-troponin is available, use the validated 0/1-hour or 0/2-hour algorithm.

**Action.** Repeat troponin at the appropriate interval (or high-sensitivity test at 0 and 1–3 hours), serial ECGs, risk stratify (HEART score), consider early invasive strategy if high-risk features or dynamic changes. Troponin at 6 hours rises to 120 ng/L → NSTEMI.

**Lessons.** Know the assay generation and its rule-out algorithm; do not discharge on one early negative result.

### Case 6: Pneumonia, urinary antigen and the wrong inference

**Presentation.** Mrs E., 70, community-acquired pneumonia with confusion, RR 28, CURB-65 score 3. Urinary *Legionella* antigen negative; *pneumococcal* urinary antigen positive. Blood cultures pending.

**Reasoning.** Positive pneumococcal antigen supports pneumococcal pneumonia (specificity ~95%), while a negative *Legionella* antigen does not exclude non-serogroup-1 Legionella infection (sensitivity 70–80%). Severe pneumonia warrants atypical cover per guidelines until the diagnosis is secure.

**Action.** Empirical IV beta-lactam plus macrolide (or doxycycline) per severity pathway; continue atypical cover until Legionella excluded by PCR on lower respiratory sample if clinical suspicion (hyponatraemia, diarrhoea, high CRP, travel/hotel/water exposure); narrow to benzylpenicillin/amoxicillin if only pneumococcus confirmed and improving; review at 48 hours; CXR follow-up; vaccination after recovery.

**Lessons.** Rapid antigen tests are adjuncts; they change therapy when positive and rarely exclude when negative.

### Case 7: A smartwatch alert

**Presentation.** Mr F., 67, hypertensive, smartwatch notifies "irregular rhythm" three times in a week; occasional palpitations. 12-lead ECG at the clinic: sinus rhythm with frequent PACs. 7-day patch monitor: paroxysmal AF burden 3% with episodes of 40 minutes.

**Reasoning.** The watch alert is a screening signal with a moderate PPV; confirmation with a 12-lead ECG or a patch recorded the arrhythmia. Paroxysmal AF carries stroke risk like persistent AF; anticoagulation depends on CHA₂DS₂-VASc (age 65–74 = 1, hypertension = 1, total 2).

**Action.** Shared decision about oral anticoagulation (DOAC) given score 2 (guidelines recommend in men with ≥ 2), assess bleeding risk, address modifiable factors (BP, alcohol, sleep apnoea, obesity), TFTs, echocardiography; rhythm control discussion if symptomatic.

**Lessons.** Consumer devices raise suspicion; diagnosis requires a clinical-grade recording and structured risk assessment.

## Common pitfalls

1. **Treating the number**: acting on a point-of-care result discordant with the clinical picture without confirmation.
2. **Relying on a negative rapid test** in high pre-test probability (influenza, malaria, strep in a child, COVID-19).
3. **Ignoring the time course** (antigen tests too early or late; troponin too early; antibody window).
4. **Wrong specimen or poor technique** (capillary sample in shock, squeezed finger, inadequate swab).
5. **Over-testing** with multiplex panels and detecting colonisation or non-viable nucleic acid.
6. **Not recognising device interferences** (maltose/icodextrin and glucose meters, carboxyhaemoglobin and SpO₂, haematocrit effects).
7. **Delaying treatment** for results in sepsis, meningitis, hypoglycaemia, severe malaria.
8. **Treating asymptomatic bacteriuria** or positive urinary antigens without clinical correlation.
9. **Failing the hook effect** (hCG, prolactin, ferritin).
10. **Not documenting or following up** POCT results; missing notifiable disease reporting.
11. **Using unvalidated devices** or expired reagents; poor quality control.
12. **Overreliance on consumer devices** or symptom checkers.
13. **Ignoring skin pigmentation bias** in pulse oximetry.
14. **Poor communication** of the meaning and limits of results to patients.

## Key points

- Point-of-care tests provide speed but often at the cost of sensitivity; interpret them with pre-test probability, timing and specimen quality.
- A negative rapid test rarely rules out disease when suspicion is high; a positive test in a low-prevalence setting may be false.
- Treat first (hypoglycaemia, sepsis, meningitis, severe malaria, STEMI), test in parallel.
- Know the key interferences: haematocrit and maltose/icodextrin (glucose), carboxyhaemoglobin/skin pigment (oximetry), hook effect (hCG), high INR (POC INR).
- Use rapid molecular tests where they change management (influenza, TB, MRSA, blood culture identification) and avoid detecting colonisation.
- Combine clinical scores (Centor, Wells, CURB-65) with tests to decide who to test and treat.
- Self-tests and wearables are screening tools; confirm and counsel.
- Quality assurance, training, documentation and notification are part of safe POCT.

## Self-assessment

**Questions**

1. Give two advantages and two limitations of point-of-care testing.
2. Why might a rapid influenza antigen test be negative in a patient with influenza, and what should you do?
3. A diabetic on peritoneal dialysis with icodextrin has a meter glucose of 12 mmol/L but is drowsy. What is the risk?
4. How is a negative malaria RDT handled in a febrile traveller?
5. When can a conventional (non-high-sensitivity) POC troponin rule out MI?
6. What is the hook effect and which tests does it affect?
7. List four causes of falsely high and four of falsely low SpO₂ readings.
8. What additional step is recommended after a negative rapid strep test in a child?
9. What are ketone thresholds that signal DKA risk, and which drug class causes euglycaemic DKA?
10. A smartwatch alerts "possible AF". Describe the next steps.

**Answers**

1. Advantages: speed, small samples, allows early treatment, useful remotely; limitations: lower sensitivity/accuracy, operator dependence, interferences, cost, quality assurance and documentation burden.
2. Antigen tests have sensitivity of ~50–70%, lower late in illness or with poor sampling; treat high-risk or severe patients empirically with oseltamivir and confirm with PCR if it will change management.
3. Maltose-derived metabolites from icodextrin can falsely elevate readings on GDH-PQQ strips, hiding hypoglycaemia; use a glucose-specific (glucose oxidase) method or laboratory glucose.
4. Do not exclude malaria: repeat thick/thin films (three sets over 24–48 h) and PCR if available; treat promptly if severe; consider other causes.
5. Only after ≥ 6–12 hours from symptom onset with a negative result (and low-risk assessment); high-sensitivity assays allow 0/1-hour or 0/2-hour rule-out per validated pathways.
6. Very high analyte concentrations saturate both antibodies, giving falsely low or negative results (hCG, prolactin, ferritin, tumour markers); dilute the sample and repeat.
7. Falsely high: carbon monoxide poisoning (carboxyhaemoglobin), darker skin pigmentation (overestimation), methaemoglobinaemia with true saturation below 85% (reading drifts towards ~85%), severe anaemia with hypoxaemia in some devices. Falsely low or unreliable: dark nail polish, poor perfusion, motion artefact, cold fingers, methylene blue or other IV dyes (any four of each).
8. Send a throat culture or molecular test to confirm the negative result in children (not routinely needed in adults).
9. β-hydroxybutyrate 1.5–3.0 mmol/L high risk, > 3.0 consistent with DKA; SGLT2 inhibitors cause euglycaemic DKA.
10. Obtain a 12-lead ECG or ambulatory monitor (patch/Holter) to confirm; assess CHA₂DS₂-VASc and bleeding risk; correct modifiable factors; shared decision on anticoagulation; rule out other causes (thyroid, echocardiography).
