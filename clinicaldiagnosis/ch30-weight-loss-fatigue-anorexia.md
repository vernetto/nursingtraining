## Chapter 30. Weight Loss, Fatigue and Anorexia

### Learning objectives

After studying this chapter you should be able to:

1. Define clinically significant unintentional weight loss, confirm it objectively, and distinguish it from intentional weight loss and from fluid shifts.
2. Build a differential for weight loss and loss of appetite using a systematic framework, and recognise that cancer, depression, and non-malignant gastrointestinal disease account for much of it.
3. Perform a stepwise, proportionate investigation of unexplained weight loss, including when to wait and re-measure.
4. Distinguish fatigue from sleepiness, muscle weakness and dyspnoea, and classify fatigue by duration and context.
5. Take a history and choose a limited, high-yield set of tests for fatigue; avoid indiscriminate testing.
6. Recognise treatable causes of fatigue (anaemia, thyroid disease, sleep apnoea, depression, adrenal insufficiency, hypercalcaemia, drugs) and the clinical features of myalgic encephalomyelitis/chronic fatigue syndrome.
7. Assess weight loss, anorexia and fatigue in older adults, and recognise disordered eating and cachexia when they occur.

---

### 30.1 Why these three symptoms belong together

Weight loss, fatigue and anorexia (loss of appetite) are **non-specific, systemic symptoms.** They overlap in cause and often occur together: a patient with cancer, heart failure, chronic infection, depression, hyperthyroidism or chronic kidney disease may have all three. Because there is no localising feature, these presentations are among the most challenging in diagnosis, and among the most prone to either **over-investigation** (a battery of tests with many false positives) or **under-investigation** (attributing symptoms to "age" or "stress").

Three principles guide the approach (Chapters 3 and 4):

1. **Confirm the symptom** and define it precisely (what exactly does the patient mean?).
2. **Start with the history and examination;** most diagnoses are suggested there.
3. **Use a small, standard first tier of tests,** then target further testing to the clues, and use time and review when the initial work-up is unrevealing and the patient is stable.

# Part A: Weight Loss and Anorexia

### 30.2 Defining and confirming weight loss

**Clinically significant unintentional weight loss** is generally defined as loss of **≥5% of usual body weight over 6–12 months** (or ≥5% in 1 month, or ≥10% in 6 months). Smaller losses can matter in frail or older people. Weight loss of ≥5% in older adults is associated with increased morbidity and mortality.

#### Confirm that it is real and unintentional

- **Objective data:** compare current weight with previous records (clinic, hospital, pharmacy, workplace). Patients' recollections are imprecise. Check whether the same scales were used.
- **Corroboration:** clothes, belts or rings that no longer fit, comments from family; a collateral history is particularly important in older adults and in cognitive impairment.
- **Intentional vs unintentional:** ask about dieting, exercise, bariatric surgery, GLP-1 receptor agonist therapy. Intentional weight loss can still signal an eating disorder or a "healthy-lifestyle" cover.
- **Fluid shifts:** weight loss after diuresis, oedema resolution; weight gain from fluid retention can mask tissue loss (heart failure, nephrotic syndrome, cirrhosis with ascites).
- **Calculate** the percentage loss and the rate; measure BMI.

#### Cachexia, starvation and sarcopenia

- **Starvation:** loss of both fat and lean mass due to inadequate intake; reversed by adequate feeding.
- **Cachexia:** a **metabolic syndrome of ongoing muscle loss (with or without fat loss) driven by systemic inflammation** (cytokines such as TNF-α, IL-6), anorexia and altered metabolism, seen in cancer, heart failure, COPD, CKD, HIV, rheumatoid arthritis. It is **not fully reversed by nutritional support alone.** Definitions: weight loss >5% in 12 months (or BMI <20) with at least three of: decreased muscle strength, fatigue, anorexia, low fat-free mass index, abnormal biochemistry (raised CRP, anaemia, low albumin).
- **Sarcopenia:** age-related loss of muscle mass and strength.
- **Frailty:** multi-system vulnerability; weight loss is a core criterion.

### 30.3 Causes of unintentional weight loss

The mechanisms are **reduced intake** (anorexia, dysphagia, nausea, early satiety, depression, poverty), **malabsorption or loss** (coeliac disease, pancreatic insufficiency, IBD, glycosuria, protein-losing enteropathy, short bowel), and **increased expenditure** (hyperthyroidism, fever, infection, cancer, burns, heart failure, COPD, phaeochromocytoma).

#### Common causes by category

| Category | Examples |
|---|---|
| **Malignancy** (about a quarter of cases in many series) | Lung, gastrointestinal (oesophagus, stomach, pancreas, colon, liver), haematological (lymphoma, leukaemia, myeloma), ovarian, renal, prostate, head and neck, breast |
| **Gastrointestinal (non-malignant)** | Peptic ulcer disease, **coeliac disease**, **inflammatory bowel disease**, chronic pancreatitis, **pancreatic exocrine insufficiency**, dysphagia (oesophageal stricture, achalasia), gastroparesis, chronic mesenteric ischaemia, small intestinal bacterial overgrowth, chronic liver disease, *H. pylori*, dental problems, parasitic infection (*Giardia*) |
| **Endocrine and metabolic** | **Hyperthyroidism**, **diabetes mellitus** (new or poorly controlled: glycosuria), **adrenal insufficiency**, hypercalcaemia, phaeochromocytoma, hypopituitarism |
| **Psychiatric and neurological** | **Depression**, anxiety, **anorexia nervosa**, other eating disorders, psychosis, dementia, delirium, Parkinson disease, stroke (dysphagia), grief, bereavement, substance use disorders (**alcohol**, stimulants, opioids) |
| **Chronic infections** | **Tuberculosis**, **HIV**, infective endocarditis, chronic hepatitis, parasitic diseases, abscess, osteomyelitis |
| **Cardiorespiratory** | Heart failure (cardiac cachexia), COPD (increased work of breathing, hypoxia), interstitial lung disease, pulmonary hypertension |
| **Renal** | Chronic kidney disease (uraemia, nausea, dietary restriction), nephrotic syndrome |
| **Rheumatological** | Rheumatoid arthritis, vasculitis (giant cell arteritis, polymyalgia rheumatica), SLE, systemic sclerosis (dysphagia, malabsorption) |
| **Medications** | **Metformin, GLP-1 receptor agonists, SGLT2 inhibitors,** digoxin, levodopa, SSRIs, stimulants (methylphenidate, amphetamines), chemotherapy, antibiotics, opioids, NSAIDs, thyroxine excess, laxatives, anticholinergics (dry mouth), topiramate, zonisamide, cannabis (variable) |
| **Social and environmental** | Poverty and food insecurity, social isolation, inability to shop or cook, poor dentition or ill-fitting dentures, functional limitations, elder abuse or neglect, institutional factors, difficulty feeding |
| **Idiopathic** | About 15–25% in some series remain unexplained after evaluation; many of these patients stabilise or regain weight without a diagnosis |

**Age matters.** In younger adults (<50), psychiatric disease, substance use, hyperthyroidism, IBD, coeliac disease, diabetes, HIV, and eating disorders predominate; cancer is less frequent. In older adults, cancer, **depression,** dementia, medications, dental problems, social factors, and chronic organ failure are the leading causes, and the cause is often multifactorial.

### 30.4 Approach to the patient with weight loss

#### History

- **Amount and time course:** how much, over how long, intentional or not; current appetite and eating pattern; typical daily intake; changes in taste or smell.
- **Appetite:** loss of appetite (anorexia), early satiety (gastric outlet obstruction, gastric cancer, pancreatic cancer, gastroparesis), aversion to specific foods (meat in cancer), fear of eating (mesenteric ischaemia, post-prandial pain), increased appetite with weight loss (**hyperthyroidism, diabetes, malabsorption, phaeochromocytoma**).
- **Swallowing:** dysphagia, odynophagia, regurgitation, coughing with eating, drooling, dental pain.
- **Gastrointestinal symptoms:** nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, diarrhoea, steatorrhoea, constipation, blood in stool or vomit, change in bowel habit.
- **Systemic symptoms:** fever, night sweats ("B symptoms"), fatigue, cough, haemoptysis, breathlessness, lumps, itching, bone pain, headache.
- **Endocrine features:** heat intolerance, palpitations, tremor, polyuria, polydipsia, postural dizziness, skin pigmentation.
- **Mood and cognition:** low mood, anhedonia, anxiety, hopelessness, sleep, memory problems, confusion, paranoia; screen with PHQ-2/PHQ-9 and cognitive screens (Chapters 20, 56, 61).
- **Social:** living situation, finances, ability to shop and cook, social support, alcohol and drugs, bereavement, abuse.
- **Medications and supplements,** recent changes.
- **Risk factors for specific diseases:** smoking, alcohol, TB contact or travel, HIV risk, family history (cancer, IBD, coeliac, thyroid disease).
- **Eating attitudes:** if suspected, ask sensitively about body image, fear of weight gain, restriction, bingeing, purging, over-exercise (see 30.8).

#### Examination

- **Weight, height, BMI** (compare with previous), mid-upper arm circumference, grip strength; screen with a validated tool such as **MUST** (Malnutrition Universal Screening Tool) (Chapter 10).
- **General:** appearance, muscle wasting (temporal, quadriceps), pallor, jaundice, clubbing, lymph nodes (including supraclavicular), skin (pigmentation, rash, bruising), hydration, oedema.
- **Mouth and dentition:** ulcers, candidiasis, dentures, caries, dry mouth.
- **Neck:** thyroid (goitre, nodules, bruit), lymph nodes.
- **Chest, heart:** signs of lung disease, heart failure.
- **Abdomen:** masses, hepatomegaly, splenomegaly, tenderness, ascites; **rectal examination.**
- **Breast, genital, prostate** examinations as appropriate.
- **Neurological:** features of Parkinson disease, stroke, neuropathy.
- **Mental state:** mood, cognition, thought content.
- **Signs of specific diseases:** exophthalmos and tremor (Graves), hyperpigmentation and postural hypotension (adrenal insufficiency), signs of HIV (oral thrush, hairy leukoplakia, lymphadenopathy), signs of malabsorption (glossitis, angular stomatitis, bruising, oedema).

#### Investigations: a tiered approach

**First tier (almost all patients with confirmed unintentional weight loss):**

- Full blood count and film, **ESR/CRP,** ferritin
- Urea, electrolytes, creatinine, **calcium,** albumin
- Liver function tests
- **Glucose or HbA1c**
- **TSH** (and free T4)
- Urinalysis
- **Chest X-ray**
- **Coeliac serology** (tissue transglutaminase IgA with total IgA)
- **HIV test** (if risk factors or no other explanation)
- Faecal immunochemical test (FIT) or stool studies as indicated
- Age- and sex-appropriate cancer screening that is up to date (mammography, cervical screening, colorectal screening, PSA discussion)
- LDH, protein electrophoresis, vitamin B12 and folate in selected cases

**Interpretation:** if the history, examination and first-tier tests are all normal, the probability of serious organic disease is low (but not zero). In this case, **watchful waiting with objective re-weighing** after 1–3 months is reasonable, accompanied by nutritional support and review.

**Second tier (guided by the clues):**

| Finding | Next step |
|---|---|
| Dysphagia, epigastric symptoms, anaemia | **Upper GI endoscopy** |
| Altered bowel habit, anaemia, positive FIT, family history | **Colonoscopy** |
| Abnormal chest X-ray, haemoptysis, smoking | **CT chest** |
| Systemic features, raised inflammatory markers, unexplained abnormal first-tier tests, high suspicion | **CT chest/abdomen/pelvis** with contrast |
| Lymphadenopathy, cytopenias, raised LDH | Biopsy, bone marrow, **PET-CT** |
| Diarrhoea, steatorrhoea, low albumin | Faecal elastase, calprotectin, breath tests, small bowel imaging, duodenal biopsy |
| Raised calcium | PTH, vitamin D, protein electrophoresis, malignancy search |
| Raised ESR/CRP with age >50 | Temporal artery assessment, PMR/GCA review, PET |
| Weight loss in smoker with normal chest X-ray | Low-dose CT where indicated |
| Persistent fever and weight loss | Work-up for FUO (Chapter 21): blood cultures, echocardiography, TB tests, HIV, PET-CT |
| Low mood, cognitive impairment | Depression and dementia assessment |
| Suspected adrenal insufficiency | 9 a.m. cortisol, short synacthen test |

**Tests with low yield in the absence of clues:** tumour markers (not for screening), extensive autoantibody panels, whole-body CT or MRI "just in case," repeated blood tests without reason.

#### Management of weight loss (general)

- **Treat the cause.**
- **Nutritional support:** dietitian assessment; food fortification, small frequent meals, oral nutritional supplements; address dentition, swallowing (speech and language therapy), shopping, meals-on-wheels; consider enteral feeding (nasogastric tube, gastrostomy) for selected patients with a reversible cause and appropriate goals.
- **Appetite stimulants** (megestrol acetate, corticosteroids, mirtazapine, cannabinoids) have limited and short-lived benefit and carry risks; use judiciously, particularly in palliative contexts.
- **Review medications.**
- **Exercise** (resistance training) to maintain muscle.
- **Follow-up:** re-weigh at intervals; continue to investigate if weight loss continues without a diagnosis.

### 30.5 Anorexia (loss of appetite)

**Anorexia** (not to be confused with anorexia nervosa) is a lack or loss of desire to eat. It often accompanies weight loss and is one of the commonest symptoms of serious illness.

| Cause | Examples |
|---|---|
| **Malignancy** | Cytokine-mediated; tumour-related hormonal changes; early satiety from gastric or pancreatic tumours |
| **Acute infection and inflammation** | Fever, viral illness, hepatitis (aversion to food and smoking) |
| **Chronic organ disease** | Heart failure (gut oedema), liver failure, CKD (uraemia), COPD |
| **Gastrointestinal** | Gastritis, ulcer, gastroparesis, constipation, malabsorption, gallbladder disease |
| **Endocrine** | Hypothyroidism, adrenal insufficiency, hypercalcaemia, diabetic ketoacidosis, hypopituitarism |
| **Neuropsychiatric** | **Depression,** anxiety, dementia, delirium, psychosis |
| **Drugs** | Digoxin, opioids, chemotherapy, antibiotics, SSRIs, metformin, stimulants, theophylline |
| **Substance use** | Alcohol, opioids, stimulants, cannabis withdrawal |
| **Pregnancy** (first trimester) | |
| **Social and sensory** | Loss of taste or smell (COVID-19, zinc deficiency, medication), poor dentition, isolation |

**Approach:** identify and treat reversible causes (drugs, depression, constipation, dental problems, oral candidiasis, nausea), use the same first-tier tests, and focus on function and nutrition.

#### Loss of appetite and early satiety: red flags

- Early satiety plus weight loss, anaemia, or dysphagia → upper GI endoscopy (gastric or pancreatic cancer).
- Anorexia with jaundice or dark urine → hepatobiliary disease or pancreatic cancer.
- Anorexia, fatigue, nausea, hypotension, hyperpigmentation, hyponatraemia → adrenal insufficiency.
- Anorexia with polyuria, constipation, confusion → hypercalcaemia.

### 30.6 Weight loss and fatigue in older adults ("failure to thrive")

Weight loss in older adults is often multifactorial, with biological, psychological and social contributors. A structured screen avoids missing reversible causes. The classic mnemonic **MEALS ON WHEELS** covers the common ones:

| Letter | Cause |
|---|---|
| **M** | **Medications** (digoxin, theophylline, SSRIs, anticholinergics, metformin, etc.) |
| **E** | **Emotional problems,** especially **depression** |
| **A** | **Anorexia nervosa** (rare), **alcoholism,** tardive dyskinesia |
| **L** | **Late-life paranoia** |
| **S** | **Swallowing problems** |
| **O** | **Oral factors** (dentition, dry mouth, ill-fitting dentures) |
| **N** | **No money** (poverty, food insecurity) |
| **O** | **Other:** hyperthyroidism, hypercalcaemia, hypoadrenalism, **cancer** |
| **N** | **Nosocomial infections** |
| **W** | **Wandering and dementia-related behaviours** |
| **H** | **Hyperthyroidism, hypercalcaemia, hypoadrenalism** |
| **E** | **Enteric problems** (malabsorption, gluten, chronic diarrhoea) |
| **E** | **Eating problems** (inability to feed oneself) |
| **L** | **Low-salt, low-cholesterol diets** (excessive restriction) |
| **S** | **Stones** (cholelithiasis), **social problems** (isolation, inability to obtain food) |

(Mnemonics vary; the point is the breadth.)

**Important points in older people:**

- **Depression is a leading treatable cause;** screen actively (PHQ-9, Geriatric Depression Scale), and treat.
- **Do not attribute weight loss to ageing.**
- **Apathetic hyperthyroidism** can present with weight loss, fatigue, depression and atrial fibrillation, without heat intolerance or tremor.
- **Review polypharmacy.**
- **Assess function:** ability to shop, cook and feed; vision; cognition; mobility; social isolation.
- **Dysphagia screening** after strokes and in neurodegenerative diseases.
- **Weight loss is a marker of frailty and mortality risk;** set realistic goals aligned with the patient's preferences. Burdensome investigations should be weighed against goals of care.

### 30.7 Cancer cachexia and palliative aspects

- Weight loss, anorexia and fatigue are frequent in advanced cancer. **Early nutritional screening and dietitian input,** exercise, and treatment of symptoms (nausea, constipation, pain, mouth problems) are important.
- **Pharmacological options:** corticosteroids (short-term improvement in appetite and well-being), progestins (megestrol acetate; risk of thromboembolism), olanzapine, anamorelin (in some countries); evidence for benefit is modest.
- **Communication:** explain that cachexia is part of the disease, not caused by failure to eat; avoid forcing food; support family distress.
- **Goals of care** guide the decision about artificial feeding.

### 30.8 Eating disorders: recognition and medical assessment

Eating disorders (anorexia nervosa, bulimia nervosa, binge-eating disorder, avoidant/restrictive food intake disorder, other specified) can present with weight loss, fatigue, amenorrhoea, gastrointestinal symptoms, dizziness, palpitations, or complications. They affect all genders and ages, and are often hidden.

#### Clues in the history and examination

- Preoccupation with weight, shape or food; intense fear of weight gain; denial of seriousness; restrictive eating, rituals, avoidance of eating in front of others; excessive exercise; self-induced vomiting; misuse of laxatives, diuretics, diet pills, or insulin omission (in diabetes); amenorrhoea or irregular periods; cold intolerance; light-headedness; constipation; family history; perfectionism.
- **Examination:** low BMI (or normal weight in bulimia), bradycardia, hypotension (postural), hypothermia, lanugo hair, dry skin, peripheral oedema, muscle wasting, parotid swelling, Russell sign (knuckle calluses), dental erosion, **signs of purging.** **Note:** a normal BMI does not exclude a serious eating disorder or medical instability.

#### Medical risk assessment (outline)

- **Vital signs:** heart rate (<40–50 bpm), blood pressure, orthostatic changes, temperature, **ECG** (bradycardia, prolonged QTc, arrhythmia).
- **Blood tests:** electrolytes (**hypokalaemia, hypomagnesaemia, hypophosphataemia,** hyponatraemia, metabolic alkalosis in purging), glucose (hypoglycaemia), liver function, renal function, FBC (leucopenia), creatine kinase, calcium.
- **Muscle strength** (SUSS test: sit up, squat-stand).
- **Bone health** (DEXA if amenorrhoea >6 months).
- **Mental health and suicide risk assessment** (high co-morbidity with depression, anxiety, OCD, self-harm).

#### Management principles

- **Non-judgemental, collaborative approach;** urgent referral to specialist eating-disorder services; **admission** criteria include severe bradycardia or hypotension, electrolyte disturbance, syncope, rapid weight loss, very low BMI, suicidality, or failure of outpatient treatment.
- **Refeeding syndrome** is a life-threatening risk when nutrition is restarted in a malnourished patient: hypophosphataemia, hypokalaemia, hypomagnesaemia, fluid shifts, thiamine deficiency, arrhythmias, heart failure, delirium. Nutritional rehabilitation in at-risk patients must be supervised, with gradual increase, electrolyte monitoring and supplementation, and **thiamine before feeding.**
- **Psychological treatment** (family-based therapy for adolescents, CBT-E, MANTRA, SSCM) is the mainstay.
- **Do not provide specific weight, calorie or exercise targets** in a general consultation to a patient with a suspected eating disorder; involve the specialist team (Chapter 56).

# Part B: Fatigue

### 30.9 What is fatigue?

**Fatigue** is a subjective feeling of tiredness, lack of energy or exhaustion, disproportionate to activity, and not relieved by rest. It is one of the commonest symptoms in primary care (up to a quarter of patients report it), and **most cases do not have a serious organic cause;** in the majority, fatigue relates to **psychosocial factors, sleep, lifestyle, or recovery from an acute illness.** However, fatigue can be the first symptom of serious disease.

#### Clarify what the patient means

Ask: "What do you mean by tired?" Distinguish:

| Complaint | Meaning | Think of |
|---|---|---|
| **Fatigue** (lack of energy, exhaustion) | Generalised | Depression, anaemia, thyroid, chronic disease, sleep disorders, medications, ME/CFS |
| **Sleepiness** (tendency to fall asleep) | Inappropriate sleep episodes | Sleep apnoea, narcolepsy, insufficient sleep, sedating drugs, hypothyroidism |
| **Muscle weakness** (reduced power) | Objectively reduced strength | Myopathy, neuropathy, myasthenia gravis, hypokalaemia, steroid excess, PMR |
| **Exertional dyspnoea or exercise intolerance** | Breathlessness or limb pain on exertion | Cardiac, pulmonary, anaemia, deconditioning |
| **Lack of motivation, anhedonia** | Mood | Depression |
| **Post-exertional malaise** | Prolonged worsening of symptoms after minimal activity (delayed by 24–72 hours) | ME/CFS, long COVID |

#### Duration

- **Acute** (<1 month): post-infectious, sleep loss, medication, acute illness, stress, pregnancy.
- **Prolonged** (1–6 months).
- **Chronic** (>6 months): chronic disease, depression, sleep disorders, ME/CFS, fibromyalgia, long COVID.

### 30.10 Causes of fatigue

| Category | Examples |
|---|---|
| **Psychological** | **Depression** (the most common organic-looking cause), **anxiety,** stress, burnout, grief, trauma, adjustment disorder, bipolar disorder, somatic symptom disorder |
| **Sleep disorders** | **Obstructive sleep apnoea,** insomnia, restless legs syndrome and periodic limb movements, shift-work disorder, circadian rhythm disorder, narcolepsy, insufficient sleep, poor sleep hygiene, night-time symptoms (pain, nocturia, orthopnoea), parenting and caring |
| **Haematological** | **Anaemia** (iron deficiency, B12/folate deficiency, anaemia of chronic disease, haemolysis), iron deficiency without anaemia (controversial), leukaemia, lymphoma, myeloma, myelodysplasia, haemochromatosis |
| **Endocrine and metabolic** | **Hypothyroidism,** hyperthyroidism (including apathetic), **diabetes mellitus** (hyperglycaemia, hypoglycaemia), **adrenal insufficiency,** Cushing syndrome, hypogonadism, hypopituitarism, hypercalcaemia, hyponatraemia, hypokalaemia, vitamin D deficiency, uraemia, hepatic failure |
| **Infection** | **Post-viral fatigue** (influenza, EBV, COVID-19), chronic hepatitis B/C, **HIV,** TB, endocarditis, Lyme disease, brucellosis, parasites, cytomegalovirus, abscess, chronic urinary infection, dental sepsis |
| **Cardiopulmonary** | Heart failure, valvular disease, arrhythmia, ischaemic heart disease, COPD, interstitial lung disease, pulmonary hypertension, asthma |
| **Gastrointestinal** | **Coeliac disease,** inflammatory bowel disease, chronic liver disease, malabsorption, chronic pancreatitis |
| **Renal** | Chronic kidney disease (anaemia, uraemia, acidosis) |
| **Cancer** | Any cancer, treatment effects (chemotherapy, radiotherapy, immunotherapy), paraneoplastic |
| **Autoimmune and rheumatological** | SLE, rheumatoid arthritis, Sjögren syndrome, polymyalgia rheumatica, vasculitis, sarcoidosis, multiple sclerosis |
| **Neurological** | Multiple sclerosis, Parkinson disease, myasthenia gravis, post-stroke fatigue, traumatic brain injury, myopathies |
| **Medications** | **Beta-blockers,** antihypertensives, sedatives and hypnotics, antihistamines, opioids, anticonvulsants, antidepressants, antipsychotics, statins (myalgia), steroids (withdrawal), chemotherapy, interferon |
| **Substance use** | **Alcohol** (acute and chronic), cannabis, opioids, stimulants (crash), caffeine overuse or withdrawal, nicotine withdrawal |
| **Lifestyle and physical** | Physical inactivity and deconditioning, obesity, poor nutrition, dehydration, overwork, long commute, pregnancy and postpartum (including postpartum thyroiditis, anaemia), menopause |
| **Syndromes** | **ME/CFS,** fibromyalgia, long COVID, postural orthostatic tachycardia syndrome (POTS), chronic Lyme-attributed syndromes |

### 30.11 Approach to fatigue

#### History

- **Onset, course and pattern:** sudden vs gradual; after an illness, event, or medication; constant or fluctuating; worse in the morning (depression: diurnal variation) or after exertion (**post-exertional malaise**).
- **Sleep:** hours, quality, snoring, witnessed apnoeas, waking unrefreshed, restless legs, nocturia, shift work, caffeine use, screen use, napping; screen with **STOP-BANG** (Snoring, Tired, Observed apnoea, Pressure (hypertension), BMI >35, Age >50, Neck circumference >40 cm, Gender male) and the **Epworth Sleepiness Scale.**
- **Mood and stress:** low mood, anhedonia, anxiety, bereavement, work or relationship stress, trauma; use **PHQ-9, GAD-7.**
- **Functional impact:** work, home, social life, exercise.
- **Associated symptoms (review of systems):** weight change, appetite, fever, night sweats, cough, breathlessness, orthopnoea, chest pain, palpitations, heavy periods, GI bleeding, cold intolerance, constipation, dry skin, hair loss (hypothyroidism), tremor, heat intolerance, polyuria and polydipsia, joint pains, rashes, dry eyes and mouth, headaches, muscle weakness, numbness, snoring, itching.
- **Medications, supplements, alcohol, caffeine, and recreational drugs.**
- **Past history:** chronic illness, recent infection (including COVID-19), cancer, surgery, pregnancy and postpartum.
- **Diet, exercise, weight.**
- **Family history:** autoimmune and thyroid disease, anaemia, coeliac, mood disorders.
- **Travel and exposures.**
- **Patient's own explanation and concerns** ("What do you think is causing it?"); fear of cancer or other serious illness is common.

#### Red flags

- Unexplained weight loss, fever, night sweats
- Lymphadenopathy, hepatosplenomegaly
- Progressive course, focal neurological symptoms or signs
- Breathlessness, chest pain, syncope, oedema (cardiac disease)
- Gastrointestinal bleeding, iron deficiency
- Haematological abnormalities on testing
- Features of adrenal insufficiency, hypercalcaemia
- Suicidal ideation (depression)
- New fatigue after age 50 with other symptoms
- Known HIV risk or immunosuppression

#### Examination

- **General:** BMI, vital signs including **postural BP and heart rate,** pallor, jaundice, hydration, signs of chronic disease.
- **Thyroid, lymph nodes, abdomen** (spleen, liver, masses), **heart, chest, skin** (pigmentation, rash), **joints, muscles** (strength), **neurological examination,** **mental state** (mood, affect, cognition).
- **Breast and pelvic examination** or rectal examination as indicated.
- **Oral cavity and neck circumference** (sleep apnoea).

#### Investigations: limited and purposeful

For most patients with persistent fatigue (>1 month) and a normal history and examination, a **basic panel** is reasonable:

| Test | Looks for |
|---|---|
| **FBC** (with indices), **ferritin** | Anaemia, iron deficiency, haematological disease |
| **ESR/CRP** | Inflammation, infection, malignancy, PMR |
| **U&E, creatinine, calcium, glucose/HbA1c, LFTs** | Renal, metabolic, hepatic disease, diabetes, hypercalcaemia |
| **TSH** | Thyroid disease |
| **Coeliac serology** (tTG-IgA with total IgA) | Coeliac disease |
| **Urinalysis** | Diabetes, kidney disease, infection |
| **Vitamin B12 and folate** (if risk or macrocytosis); **vitamin D** (selected) | Deficiency |
| **Pregnancy test** | As appropriate |
| **HIV, hepatitis B/C serology** | If risk factors |
| **ECG, chest X-ray** | If cardiorespiratory symptoms or signs |

Further tests only if the clues indicate: 9 a.m. cortisol or synacthen test (adrenal insufficiency), creatine kinase (myopathy), autoantibodies (if features of connective tissue disease), sleep study (if suspected OSA), echocardiography, CT, bone marrow biopsy, etc.

**What not to do:** order large "fatigue panels," multiple vitamin levels, hormone profiles, food intolerance tests or "adrenal fatigue" saliva tests. They are generally unhelpful, produce false positives, and reinforce illness beliefs. A **normal basic work-up is itself valuable** and should be explained positively, while acknowledging that the fatigue is real.

#### Follow-up

- **Review in 2–4 weeks** and again as needed; many patients improve spontaneously with sleep, stress management, treatment of low mood, and graded activity.
- If fatigue persists >6 months, consider ME/CFS, fibromyalgia, or ongoing medical or psychiatric causes; re-evaluate, and avoid repeating the same tests without new clinical reason.

### 30.12 Specific conditions to know

#### Depression and anxiety

- The most common cause of persistent fatigue in primary care; present with low mood, anhedonia, sleep and appetite change, poor concentration, guilt, psychomotor changes, and suicidal thoughts; many patients present with somatic symptoms only. Screen routinely (PHQ-9); treat with psychological therapy and/or antidepressants (Chapter 56).

#### Obstructive sleep apnoea (OSA)

- Obesity, male sex, large neck, snoring, witnessed apnoeas, unrefreshing sleep, morning headache, daytime sleepiness, hypertension, nocturia. **Diagnosis:** home sleep apnoea test or polysomnography. **Treatment:** CPAP, weight loss, mandibular advancement devices, positional therapy, surgery in selected cases; advise about driving safety.

#### Anaemia and iron deficiency

- Fatigue, breathlessness, pallor, palpitations, headache; investigate the cause (Chapter 35). Iron deficiency without anaemia can cause fatigue; replacement may help in selected patients with low ferritin; always seek the source in men and postmenopausal women.

#### Hypothyroidism and hyperthyroidism

- **Hypothyroidism:** fatigue, weight gain, cold intolerance, constipation, dry skin, hair loss, bradycardia, slow reflexes, depression, menstrual disturbance; raised TSH with low free T4.
- **Hyperthyroidism:** weight loss, anxiety, tremor, palpitations, heat intolerance, diarrhoea; in older adults, **apathetic** presentation with fatigue and AF; low TSH.

#### Diabetes mellitus

- Fatigue with polyuria, polydipsia, weight loss; hypoglycaemia in treated patients. Check glucose and HbA1c.

#### Adrenal insufficiency (Addison disease)

- Fatigue, weakness, weight loss, anorexia, nausea, abdominal pain, **postural hypotension,** **hyperpigmentation** (skin creases, buccal mucosa, scars), salt craving, **hyponatraemia, hyperkalaemia,** hypoglycaemia, eosinophilia. Causes: autoimmune adrenalitis, TB, adrenal haemorrhage, metastases, **long-term steroid use with sudden withdrawal.**
- **Diagnosis:** morning cortisol (low) and **ACTH (high),** short synacthen test; adrenal antibodies; imaging. **Adrenal crisis is an emergency** (vomiting, hypotension, shock, abdominal pain, hypoglycaemia): IV hydrocortisone and fluids without waiting for results.

#### Hypercalcaemia

- "**Stones, bones, abdominal groans, psychic moans:**" fatigue, polyuria, polydipsia, constipation, nausea, abdominal pain, confusion, renal stones. Causes: primary hyperparathyroidism (common), malignancy (bone metastases, myeloma, PTHrP), vitamin D excess, sarcoidosis, drugs (thiazides, lithium). Check calcium, PTH, vitamin D, protein electrophoresis.

#### Infections

- **Post-viral fatigue** is common and typically resolves over weeks to months; **EBV, CMV, hepatitis, HIV, TB** and others should be considered with systemic features. **Long COVID** (post-COVID-19 condition) includes fatigue, post-exertional malaise, cognitive symptoms ("brain fog"), breathlessness and autonomic features; manage with symptom-based care and pacing.

#### Medication-related fatigue

- Beta-blockers, sedatives, antihistamines, opioids, anticonvulsants, antidepressants, antipsychotics, statins: review timing and dose; consider alternatives.

#### Myalgic encephalomyelitis / chronic fatigue syndrome (ME/CFS)

- **Core features (IOM 2015 criteria):** (1) a substantial reduction or impairment in the ability to engage in pre-illness levels of occupational, educational, social or personal activities, persisting for **>6 months** (≥3 months in some guidelines), accompanied by **profound fatigue,** which is new, not the result of ongoing excessive exertion, and not substantially alleviated by rest; (2) **post-exertional malaise** (PEM: worsening of symptoms after minimal physical, cognitive or emotional exertion, typically delayed by hours to days and lasting for days or weeks); (3) **unrefreshing sleep;** and (4) at least one of **cognitive impairment** or **orthostatic intolerance.** Other common features: muscle and joint pain, headaches, sore throat, tender lymph nodes, sensory sensitivity.
- **Diagnosis:** clinical, after history, examination and basic tests have excluded other causes (anaemia, thyroid disease, sleep disorders, depression, coeliac disease, adrenal insufficiency, etc.). Many patients have a viral trigger.
- **Management:** acknowledge the illness as real; **energy management (pacing)** to avoid post-exertional crashes; sleep management; symptom-directed treatment (pain, orthostatic intolerance, mood); support for work and education; psychological support for coping (not as a cure); specialist ME/CFS services. **Graded exercise therapy** is not recommended (current UK guidance) because it can worsen PEM; follow current local guidelines.

#### Fibromyalgia

- See Chapter 29: widespread pain, fatigue, non-restorative sleep.

#### Postural orthostatic tachycardia syndrome (POTS)

- Heart rate increase ≥30 beats/min within 10 minutes of standing (≥40 in adolescents) without orthostatic hypotension, with symptoms (light-headedness, palpitations, fatigue, "brain fog"), >3 months; young women; treat with fluids, salt, compression garments, graded exercise, and sometimes medications.

### 30.13 Special populations

- **Older adults:** fatigue and weight loss may signal depression, heart failure, anaemia, hypothyroidism, polypharmacy, malignancy, B12 deficiency, or frailty. Atypical presentations are common; reduce polypharmacy; evaluate function and social supports.
- **Children and adolescents:** fatigue with weight loss, pallor, bruising or bone pain: leukaemia; also depression, anorexia nervosa, EBV, diabetes, coeliac disease, thyroid disease, IBD, sleep deprivation, school stress.
- **Pregnancy and postpartum:** physiological fatigue, anaemia, thyroid dysfunction (postpartum thyroiditis), depression, sleep deprivation, gestational diabetes.
- **Cancer survivors:** cancer-related fatigue; exclude recurrence, hypothyroidism after neck radiation, anaemia, depression; exercise is the best-supported treatment.
- **Healthcare workers and carers:** burnout.

### 30.14 Worked cases

#### Case 1: Weight loss and clubbing

*A 64-year-old man, 40 pack-years, has lost 9 kg in 4 months with anorexia and a cough. He has finger clubbing and a hoarse voice. Hb 105 g/L, calcium 2.9 mmol/L, ALP raised. Chest X-ray shows a hilar mass.*

**Reasoning.** Weight loss, anorexia, clubbing, hoarseness, hypercalcaemia, anaemia and a hilar mass: **lung cancer** (probably with hypercalcaemia of malignancy or bone metastases).

**Action.** CT chest/abdomen with contrast, bronchoscopy/biopsy, staging, MDT; treat hypercalcaemia (IV fluids, bisphosphonate); nutritional and palliative support.

#### Case 2: Apathetic hyperthyroidism

*A 78-year-old woman is brought by her daughter because of 6 months of weight loss (7 kg), fatigue, low mood and "slowing down." She has no tremor or heat intolerance. Pulse 108 irregularly irregular (AF). TSH <0.01, free T4 high.*

**Reasoning.** **Hyperthyroidism** (apathetic type, possibly multinodular goitre or Graves) with AF, presenting as depression and failure to thrive.

**Action.** Anti-thyroid drug or radioiodine, beta-blocker for rate control, anticoagulation for AF, thyroid uptake scan, monitor for cardiac failure. *Lesson:* check TSH in all older patients with weight loss, AF or depression.

#### Case 3: Weight loss and iron deficiency in a young woman

*A 29-year-old woman has lost 5 kg over 8 months, with bloating, loose stools and tiredness. Ferritin 8, Hb 98, MCV 74. Mouth ulcers. tTG-IgA strongly positive; duodenal biopsy: villous atrophy.*

**Reasoning.** **Coeliac disease** (presenting with malabsorption, iron deficiency).

**Action.** Gluten-free diet with dietitian, replace iron, folate, vitamin D, B12 as needed, bone density, family screening, regular follow-up.

#### Case 4: Failure to thrive in an older woman

*A woman aged 84, widowed 6 months ago and living alone, has lost 6 kg, eats little, is tired, and has stopped going out. She has ill-fitting dentures, tearful at interview; PHQ-9 is 17. Blood tests (first tier) are normal. Cognition is normal (MoCA 27).*

**Reasoning.** Multifactorial weight loss: **depression (bereavement)** plus oral problems and social isolation; no organic cause found.

**Action.** Treat depression (psychological support, consider antidepressant, e.g. mirtazapine), dental referral, dietitian, home meal service, social support, review weight monthly; reassess for organic disease if weight loss continues.

#### Case 5: HIV

*A 35-year-old man has lost 8 kg over 5 months, has oral thrush, night sweats, diarrhoea and fatigue. He has had unprotected sex with multiple partners. Lymphadenopathy in neck and axillae.*

**Reasoning.** **HIV infection** with opportunistic infections (oral candidiasis) and wasting; consider TB, MAC, cryptosporidiosis, lymphoma.

**Action.** HIV test (4th generation) with CD4 and viral load, TB screening, stool studies, CXR, start antiretroviral therapy with specialist team, prophylaxis according to CD4 count, counselling, partner notification.

#### Case 6: Pancreatic cancer

*A 67-year-old man has lost 8 kg, has early satiety, dull epigastric pain radiating to the back, and recently diagnosed diabetes. He is mildly jaundiced. CA 19-9 is high; CT: pancreatic head mass with liver metastases.*

**Reasoning.** New-onset diabetes with weight loss and epigastric pain in an older adult: **pancreatic cancer** until proven otherwise.

**Action.** CT staging, EUS biopsy, biliary stent for jaundice, oncology and palliative care involvement, nutrition support (pancreatic enzyme replacement), pain control.

#### Case 7: Fatigue and iron deficiency

*A 33-year-old woman has 3 months of tiredness, pallor and breathlessness on stairs. Her periods are heavy. Hb 88 g/L, MCV 72, ferritin 4. Coeliac serology negative.*

**Reasoning.** **Iron-deficiency anaemia from menorrhagia** (most likely); consider dietary factors and rarely GI loss.

**Action.** Oral iron (alternate-day dosing improves absorption) or IV iron if intolerance or inadequate response; treat menorrhagia (tranexamic acid, hormonal therapy, intrauterine system); review response in 4 weeks (Hb should rise by about 10–20 g/L), continue iron for 3 months after normalising to replenish stores. If no clear gynaecological explanation or inadequate response, investigate GI sources.

#### Case 8: Obstructive sleep apnoea

*A 52-year-old man, BMI 35, has 2 years of daytime sleepiness, morning headaches and difficulty concentrating; his wife reports loud snoring and pauses in breathing. Hypertension is poorly controlled. Epworth score 16. A home sleep study shows an apnoea–hypopnoea index of 38/hour.*

**Reasoning.** Severe **OSA** causing fatigue and hypertension.

**Action.** CPAP, weight loss, avoid alcohol and sedatives at night, driving advice, treat hypertension, follow-up for adherence.

#### Case 9: Addison disease

*A 31-year-old woman has 4 months of increasing fatigue, nausea, weight loss of 6 kg, salt craving, and dizziness on standing. BP 92/58 lying, 76/50 standing. Tanned skin with darkened palmar creases. Na 128, K 5.7, glucose 3.6. 9 a.m. cortisol low, ACTH high; adrenal antibodies positive.*

**Reasoning.** **Autoimmune adrenal insufficiency (Addison disease).**

**Action.** Hydrocortisone and fludrocortisone replacement; steroid card and sick-day rules (double or triple the dose during illness; IM/IV hydrocortisone in vomiting); educate on adrenal crisis; screen for other autoimmune diseases (thyroid, type 1 diabetes, pernicious anaemia).

#### Case 10: ME/CFS

*A 28-year-old woman has had severe fatigue for 9 months after glandular fever. After a short walk she is bedbound for 3 days. She wakes unrefreshed, has poor concentration, and feels dizzy on standing. Basic blood tests, TSH, coeliac serology, ferritin, and morning cortisol are normal. PHQ-9 is 8.*

**Reasoning.** Meets criteria for **ME/CFS** (fatigue >6 months with post-exertional malaise, unrefreshing sleep, cognitive impairment and orthostatic intolerance), after exclusion of other causes.

**Action.** Validate; explain; pacing and energy management; sleep strategies; manage orthostatic symptoms (fluids, salt, compression); support for education and work; referral to specialist service; avoid graded exercise; monitor mood.

#### Case 11: Fatigue with normal tests: time and review

*A 38-year-old teacher with two young children has felt exhausted for 6 weeks after a viral illness. She sleeps 5–6 hours with interruptions, is stressed at work, and mood is low but without suicidal thoughts. Examination and basic tests are normal.*

**Reasoning.** **Fatigue related to sleep deprivation, post-viral recovery and stress,** with mild depressive symptoms; no red flags.

**Action.** Explain the normal results positively; address sleep, activity, workload and support; screen and monitor mood (PHQ-9); review in 4 weeks; avoid further tests unless new features. The explanation and follow-up are as important as the tests.

### 30.15 Common pitfalls

- **Accepting reported weight loss without objective confirmation,** or dismissing it as "age."
- **Not recognising depression** as a cause of weight loss and fatigue, particularly in older adults and men.
- **Starting with imaging** before history, examination and basic tests.
- **Ordering a battery of tests** for fatigue without clues (false positives, anxiety).
- **Missing treatable endocrine causes** (hyperthyroidism, adrenal insufficiency, hypercalcaemia, diabetes).
- **Missing coeliac disease and HIV.**
- **Overlooking medications,** alcohol and substance use.
- **Missing sleep apnoea** in tired, overweight, hypertensive patients.
- **Forgetting the rectal examination and the oral cavity.**
- **Not following up** unexplained weight loss; cancers may declare themselves later.
- **Appetite stimulants** in place of diagnosis.
- **Refeeding syndrome** when feeding a severely malnourished patient.
- **Dismissing ME/CFS** or prescribing graded exercise to patients with post-exertional malaise.
- **Missing eating disorders** in men, older patients, and patients with normal BMI.

---

### 30.16 Key points

- Confirm weight loss objectively; ≥5% unintentional loss in 6–12 months is significant.
- Main categories: cancer, non-malignant GI disease, endocrine/metabolic, psychiatric (especially depression), infection (TB, HIV), chronic organ failure, drugs, social factors; a quarter remain unexplained.
- Use a first-tier panel (FBC, ESR/CRP, U&E, calcium, LFTs, glucose, TSH, coeliac serology, urinalysis, chest X-ray, HIV where indicated) and targeted second-tier tests; watch and re-measure if all tests are normal.
- Older adults: screen for depression, dentition, swallowing, medications, cognition and social factors.
- Cachexia is inflammation-driven and not reversed by feeding alone.
- Fatigue: clarify the meaning (fatigue vs sleepiness vs weakness vs dyspnoea), duration, sleep, mood and drugs; use a limited basic panel; avoid test panels.
- Common treatable causes: depression, sleep disorders (OSA), anaemia, hypothyroidism, diabetes, adrenal insufficiency, hypercalcaemia, drugs.
- ME/CFS is a clinical diagnosis with post-exertional malaise as a defining feature; pacing is recommended and graded exercise is not.
- Provide follow-up, explain normal results positively, and reassess if new features arise.

### 30.17 Self-assessment

1. What degree of unintentional weight loss is considered clinically significant?
   *Answer: ≥5% of usual body weight over 6–12 months (or ≥5% in 1 month or ≥10% in 6 months).*

2. List the first-tier tests for unexplained weight loss.
   *Answer: FBC, ESR/CRP, U&E, creatinine, calcium, albumin, LFTs, glucose/HbA1c, TSH, urinalysis, chest X-ray, coeliac serology, HIV (if indicated), FIT/stool tests, age-appropriate cancer screening.*

3. How does cachexia differ from simple starvation?
   *Answer: Cachexia is inflammation-driven muscle (± fat) loss with anorexia and altered metabolism, not fully reversed by feeding; starvation is due to inadequate intake and reverses with adequate nutrition.*

4. Name five treatable causes of fatigue that should be considered in the basic work-up.
   *Answer (any five): anaemia/iron deficiency, hypothyroidism, diabetes, depression, sleep apnoea, adrenal insufficiency, hypercalcaemia, coeliac disease, medications, CKD, liver disease, HIV.*

5. What are the defining features of ME/CFS?
   *Answer: Substantial functional reduction with profound new fatigue for >6 months, post-exertional malaise, unrefreshing sleep, plus cognitive impairment and/or orthostatic intolerance, after exclusion of other causes.*

6. Which electrolyte disturbances characterise refeeding syndrome, and what should be given before feeding?
   *Answer: Hypophosphataemia, hypokalaemia, hypomagnesaemia (with fluid shifts); thiamine before and during feeding.*
