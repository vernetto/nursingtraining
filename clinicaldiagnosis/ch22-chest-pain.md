## Chapter 22. Chest Pain

### Learning objectives

After studying this chapter you should be able to:

1. Identify the life-threatening causes of chest pain and exclude them systematically.
2. Take a focused history that discriminates cardiac, pulmonary, vascular, gastrointestinal, musculoskeletal and psychological causes.
3. Distinguish typical from atypical anginal pain and recognise atypical presentations of acute coronary syndrome in women, older adults and people with diabetes.
4. Perform and interpret the essential bedside examination and the first-line investigations (ECG, troponin, chest X-ray).
5. Use validated tools (HEART score, Wells score, PERC, aortic dissection detection risk score) to stratify risk.
6. Recognise the classic ECG patterns of ischaemia, pericarditis and pulmonary embolism.
7. Decide who needs immediate treatment, who can be investigated further, and who can be safely discharged.

---

### 22.1 Why chest pain is a diagnostic challenge

Chest pain is one of the most common reasons for attending an emergency department and for calling an ambulance. The challenge is that:

- The **commonest causes** are benign (musculoskeletal, reflux, anxiety).
- The **commonest dangerous cause** (acute coronary syndrome) can look benign, and the dangerous causes can all look alike.
- A small percentage of patients discharged from emergency departments with chest pain subsequently turn out to have had a myocardial infarction. Most of these were missed because the clinician anchored on a benign diagnosis (Chapter 2).
- Conversely, over-investigating every patient is costly and exposes people to harm (radiation, invasive tests, anxiety).

The approach is therefore a two-step process:

1. **Exclude (or treat) the life-threatening causes**, using history, examination, ECG and biomarkers.
2. **Diagnose the cause** among the remaining possibilities, and treat or reassure appropriately, with safety-netting.

---

### 22.2 The "can't miss" list

| Diagnosis | Why it matters | Key discriminators |
|---|---|---|
| **Acute coronary syndrome** (unstable angina, NSTEMI, STEMI) | Most common life-threatening cause; time-critical reperfusion | Pressure-like retrosternal pain, radiation, exertion, sweating; ECG, troponin |
| **Pulmonary embolism** | Can cause sudden death; treatable | Pleuritic pain, breathlessness, tachycardia, hypoxia, risk factors |
| **Aortic dissection** | Mortality increases by the hour; thrombolysis or anticoagulation is fatal | Abrupt, severe, tearing pain to back; pulse/BP differential; hypertension |
| **Tension pneumothorax** | Rapidly fatal; clinical diagnosis | Sudden pain, severe dyspnoea, hypotension, absent breath sounds, tracheal deviation |
| **Cardiac tamponade** | Fatal if untreated | Hypotension, raised JVP, muffled sounds, pulsus paradoxus |
| **Oesophageal rupture (Boerhaave)** | High mortality if delayed | Severe pain after forceful vomiting; surgical emphysema; pleural effusion |
| **Myocarditis / pericarditis with tamponade** | May deteriorate | Preceding viral illness; ECG changes; raised troponin |
| **Pneumonia with sepsis** | Treatable | Fever, cough, focal signs |
| **Takotsubo cardiomyopathy and spontaneous coronary artery dissection (SCAD)** | Mimic ACS; SCAD affects young women | Emotional stress, peripartum; angiographic diagnosis |

Other conditions that can be dangerous: acute cholecystitis and pancreatitis presenting with lower chest pain; ruptured oesophageal varices; sickle cell chest syndrome; cocaine-related ischaemia; severe aortic stenosis.

### 22.3 History: reading the pain

The history is the most powerful tool; it often points to the diagnosis before any test. Characterise the pain using SOCRATES (Chapter 6), but with a cardiac focus.

#### Site and radiation

- **Central retrosternal** with radiation to the left arm, both arms, jaw, neck or back: ischaemic.
- **Sharp and localised**, with a finger-point location, especially over the left lower chest: more often musculoskeletal, pleural or functional, but not exclusively.
- **Interscapular or tearing** to the back: aortic dissection (also ACS involving the inferior wall or pancreatitis).
- **Epigastric**: ACS (inferior), peptic ulcer, pancreatitis, biliary disease.
- **Unilateral and dermatomal**: herpes zoster (may precede rash by days), radiculopathy.

#### Onset and time course

- **Sudden, maximal at onset** (within seconds to minutes): aortic dissection, pulmonary embolism, pneumothorax, oesophageal rupture.
- **Crescendo over minutes** and persistent: myocardial infarction.
- **Exertional** with a predictable pattern, lasting 2–10 minutes and relieved by rest or nitrate: stable angina.
- **Rest pain lasting >20 minutes, new onset angina, crescendo angina**: unstable angina or NSTEMI.
- **Very brief** (seconds), stabbing: rarely cardiac (precordial catch syndrome, musculoskeletal).
- **Hours to days of constant pain**: pericarditis, pleuritis, musculoskeletal, GORD; consider also myocarditis.
- **Meals-related, nocturnal, bending forward**: GORD, peptic ulcer.

#### Character

| Description | Common association |
|---|---|
| Pressure, tightness, heaviness, squeezing ("elephant on the chest"), a band | Myocardial ischaemia |
| Burning | Reflux, oesophagitis, peptic ulcer, also ischaemia |
| Sharp, stabbing, worse on inspiration (pleuritic) | Pleural and pericardial disease, PE, pneumothorax, chest wall |
| Tearing, ripping | Aortic dissection |
| Dull, constant ache | Musculoskeletal, cancer, pericarditis |
| Colicky | Biliary, oesophageal spasm |
| Tingling/numbness with anxiety | Panic, hyperventilation |

#### Aggravating and relieving factors

- **Exertion** brings on angina; **rest** eases it within minutes. **Glyceryl trinitrate (GTN)** relief within a few minutes supports angina but is **not diagnostic** (oesophageal spasm and other conditions respond too).
- **Inspiration, coughing, movement, position:** pleural, pericardial or musculoskeletal pain. **Leaning forward** relieves pericarditis; **lying flat** worsens it.
- **Food, alcohol, lying flat, bending:** GORD. **Swallowing pain** (odynophagia): oesophagitis, candidiasis.
- **Palpation reproducing the pain:** suggests a chest wall cause, but **reproducible tenderness does not exclude ACS** (it is present in a notable minority of confirmed infarcts).
- **Emotional stress, hyperventilation, panic:** functional chest pain.

#### Associated symptoms

Sweating, nausea, vomiting, breathlessness, faintness, palpitations, anxiety ("sense of impending doom"), syncope (consider massive PE, dissection, aortic stenosis, arrhythmia), cough, fever, haemoptysis, leg swelling, dysphagia, regurgitation, weight loss.

#### Typical and atypical angina (clinical classification)

The traditional categorisation uses three features:

1. **Substernal chest discomfort** of characteristic quality and duration;
2. **Provoked by exertion or emotional stress;**
3. **Relieved by rest or nitroglycerin.**

- **Typical angina:** all three.
- **Atypical angina:** two of three.
- **Non-anginal chest pain:** one or none.

This classification stratifies the pre-test probability of coronary artery disease but should never be used alone to rule out ACS.

#### Atypical presentations of ACS

Atypical or "equivalent" presentations are common, especially in:

- **Women:** nausea, fatigue, breathlessness, jaw or back pain, epigastric discomfort.
- **Older adults:** breathlessness, syncope, confusion, falls, weakness, sometimes **no pain**.
- **People with diabetes:** painless ("silent") ischaemia owing to autonomic neuropathy.
- **Chronic kidney disease**, **dementia**, **postoperative patients**.

#### Risk factors and background

- **Cardiovascular:** age, male sex (until menopause), hypertension, diabetes, smoking, hyperlipidaemia, family history of **premature** coronary disease (male relative <55 years, female relative <65), obesity, previous CAD, stroke, peripheral vascular disease, chronic kidney disease, cocaine or amphetamine use, inflammatory disease, HIV.
- **Venous thromboembolism:** immobility, recent surgery or travel, cancer, previous VTE, oestrogen, pregnancy and puerperium, thrombophilia.
- **Aortic disease:** hypertension, connective tissue disease (Marfan, Ehlers–Danlos), bicuspid valve, family history, cocaine, recent cardiac surgery or catheterisation, pregnancy, giant cell arteritis.
- **Pneumothorax:** tall, thin young male, smoker, COPD, Marfan, catamenial pneumothorax, previous pneumothorax.
- **Gastro-oesophageal:** hiatus hernia, NSAIDs, alcohol, forceful vomiting.

### 22.4 Examination

Aim to answer: **How ill is the patient?** (airway, breathing, circulation), **is there evidence of heart failure, shock or a murmur?** and **is there a non-cardiac cause?**

#### Vital signs and general observation

- **Heart rate, BP in both arms, respiratory rate, SpO₂, temperature.**
- **Appearance:** pale, sweaty, distressed, lying still (ischaemia), leaning forward (pericarditis).
- **Perfusion:** capillary refill, extremities.

#### Cardiovascular

- JVP (raised in RV infarction, PE, tamponade, heart failure).
- Pulses: asymmetry (dissection), tachycardia, irregular rhythm.
- Heart sounds: S3/S4, new murmurs (mitral regurgitation from papillary muscle dysfunction; ventricular septal rupture after MI; aortic regurgitation in dissection; aortic stenosis), friction rub.
- Peripheral oedema, signs of DVT.

#### Respiratory

- Breath sounds (reduced in pneumothorax, effusion), crackles (pulmonary oedema, pneumonia), wheeze, rub, tracheal position.

#### Chest wall and abdomen

- Tenderness, rash (zoster), swelling, crepitus; epigastric tenderness; right upper quadrant tenderness (biliary).

#### Limbs and neck

- Calf tenderness and swelling (VTE), xanthomata, neck swelling.

#### The examination can be normal in ACS

A normal examination does not exclude ACS, and abnormal findings can point to complications (mitral regurgitation murmur, S3, crackles, hypotension).

### 22.5 Investigations

#### The 12-lead ECG

- **Obtain within 10 minutes** of arrival in any patient with suspected cardiac chest pain; repeat if symptoms change, or at intervals (e.g. every 15–30 minutes in ongoing pain with a non-diagnostic ECG).
- Compare with previous ECGs.

**STEMI (ST-elevation myocardial infarction):** new ST elevation at the J point in at least two contiguous leads, ≥1 mm (0.1 mV) in all leads other than V2–V3; in V2–V3: ≥2 mm in men aged ≥40, ≥2.5 mm in men <40, and ≥1.5 mm in women. Reciprocal ST depression supports the diagnosis. **Posterior MI** shows ST depression and tall R waves in V1–V3 (record posterior leads V7–V9). A **new left bundle branch block** with a compatible clinical picture is treated as a possible STEMI equivalent (use the Sgarbossa criteria), as are hyperacute T waves, de Winter pattern (up-sloping ST depression with tall T waves in the precordial leads), and Wellens pattern (biphasic or deeply inverted T waves in V2–V3 indicating critical LAD stenosis).

**NSTE-ACS:** ST depression, T-wave inversion, or a normal ECG. A normal ECG does not exclude ACS.

**Pericarditis:** diffuse concave (saddle-shaped) ST elevation, PR depression (and PR elevation in aVR), without reciprocal changes.

**Pulmonary embolism:** sinus tachycardia (commonest), right axis deviation, new right bundle branch block, T-wave inversion in V1–V4, S1Q3T3 (classic but uncommon).

**Aortic dissection:** may show ischaemic changes if a coronary ostium is involved; the ECG can be normal.

**Pneumothorax, pneumonia, GORD:** typically normal or non-specific.

**Pitfalls:** early repolarisation, left ventricular hypertrophy, bundle branch block, paced rhythms, hyperkalaemia, Brugada pattern and other mimics can cause ST changes; clinical context and serial ECGs are essential.

#### Cardiac troponin

- **High-sensitivity cardiac troponin (hs-cTn)** is the standard biomarker for myocardial injury. It rises 1–3 hours after myocardial injury and remains elevated for days.
- Use a **rapid rule-out/rule-in pathway** (e.g. 0/1-hour or 0/2-hour algorithms) that incorporates baseline and change values, validated for your assay and combined with ECG and clinical assessment. A single very low value (below the assay's rule-out threshold) taken more than 3 hours after symptom onset can rule out MI in selected patients.
- **Elevated troponin is not synonymous with MI.** Myocardial injury occurs in sepsis, heart failure, myocarditis, pulmonary embolism, renal failure, tachyarrhythmia, Takotsubo, stroke, aortic dissection, pericarditis, vigorous exercise, and others. **MI requires a rise and/or fall with symptoms of ischaemia, ischaemic ECG changes, imaging evidence, or coronary thrombus** (Universal Definition of MI). Interpret in context.
- Troponin is **time-dependent**: a negative initial value shortly after pain onset needs repeating.

#### Chest X-ray

- Pneumothorax, pneumonia, effusion, pulmonary oedema, widened mediastinum (dissection), free air under the diaphragm (perforation), pneumomediastinum, rib fractures, cardiomegaly.
- It is often normal in PE, ACS and aortic dissection.

#### D-dimer

- Useful for **excluding** PE or dissection when pre-test probability is **low**. Not useful when pre-test probability is high or in many inpatients. For dissection, the ADvISED pathway combines a low ADD-RS with a negative D-dimer.

#### CT pulmonary angiography (CTPA), CT aortography, echocardiography

- **CTPA** for suspected PE with intermediate or high pre-test probability, or a positive D-dimer (Chapter 70).
- **CT aortography** for suspected aortic dissection.
- **Echocardiography:** regional wall motion abnormalities, ventricular function, valve disease, effusion and tamponade, right ventricular strain in PE, aortic root dilatation or flap. Bedside echo is often vital in the unstable patient.
- **CT coronary angiography** for low-to-intermediate probability chest pain with a negative troponin to exclude significant coronary artery disease; **stress testing** (exercise ECG, stress echo, myocardial perfusion imaging, stress MRI) for stable patients; **invasive coronary angiography** for high-risk ACS and symptomatic patients.

#### Other tests

- Full blood count (anaemia, infection), U&E, glucose, lipids, CRP, **lipase** (pancreatitis), **liver function tests**, **blood gases**, **BNP**, **lactate** if unwell, **pregnancy test**, **toxicology screen** if cocaine suspected.

### 22.6 Risk stratification

#### HEART score (undifferentiated chest pain in the emergency department)

| Component | 0 | 1 | 2 |
|---|---|---|---|
| **H**istory | Slightly suspicious | Moderately suspicious | Highly suspicious |
| **E**CG | Normal | Non-specific repolarisation changes | Significant ST deviation |
| **A**ge | <45 | 45–64 | ≥65 |
| **R**isk factors | None | 1–2 | ≥3 or known atherosclerotic disease |
| **T**roponin | ≤ normal limit | 1–3 × normal | >3 × normal |

- **0–3: low risk** (30-day major adverse cardiac event rate of about 1–2%): consider early discharge with follow-up, according to local policy.
- **4–6: intermediate risk:** observation, serial troponin, and further testing.
- **7–10: high risk:** early invasive strategy.

Use as a **decision aid**, not a substitute for judgement; always integrate other clinical features, especially a high-risk history.

#### Other scores

- **GRACE** and **TIMI** scores for risk of death and recurrent ischaemic events in confirmed NSTE-ACS.
- **Wells score for PE** and **PERC rule** (Chapter 70): PERC (age <50, HR <100, SpO₂ ≥95%, no haemoptysis, no oestrogen use, no previous VTE, no unilateral leg swelling, no recent surgery or trauma) allows exclusion of PE without D-dimer when the pre-test probability is already low (<15%).
- **Aortic Dissection Detection Risk Score (ADD-RS):** one point each for (1) high-risk condition (Marfan, family history of aortic disease, known aortic valve disease, recent aortic manipulation, known thoracic aortic aneurysm), (2) high-risk pain features (abrupt onset, severe intensity, ripping or tearing), (3) high-risk examination features (pulse deficit, systolic BP difference, focal neurological deficit with pain, new aortic regurgitation murmur with pain, hypotension or shock). A score of 0 is low risk (with D-dimer to exclude); ≥2 is high risk (proceed directly to imaging).

### 22.7 Cause-by-cause diagnostic features

#### Cardiac causes

| Diagnosis | Features |
|---|---|
| **Stable angina** | Exertional or stress-related pressure, relieved by rest or GTN within minutes, reproducible, stable pattern over weeks |
| **Unstable angina / NSTEMI** | Rest pain, new-onset severe angina, crescendo; ECG changes or troponin rise (NSTEMI) |
| **STEMI** | Prolonged severe pain, sweating, nausea; ST elevation; time-critical reperfusion |
| **Pericarditis** | Sharp pleuritic pain, worse lying flat, better leaning forward, preceding viral illness, friction rub, diffuse ST elevation and PR depression |
| **Myocarditis** | Chest pain with preceding viral illness; raised troponin; arrhythmias; heart failure; young patients |
| **Aortic stenosis** | Exertional chest pain, syncope, breathlessness; ejection systolic murmur |
| **Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy** | Exertional chest pain, syncope, family history of sudden death |
| **Takotsubo cardiomyopathy** | Emotional or physical stress, usually postmenopausal women; ST elevation and apical ballooning |
| **SCAD** | Young women, peripartum, no atherosclerotic risk factors |
| **Coronary vasospasm (Prinzmetal angina)** | Rest pain at night/early morning, transient ST elevation, smokers, cocaine |

#### Vascular and pulmonary causes

| Diagnosis | Features |
|---|---|
| **Pulmonary embolism** | Pleuritic pain, dyspnoea, tachycardia, hypoxia, risk factors; may have syncope; clear chest; unilateral leg swelling |
| **Aortic dissection** | Abrupt severe tearing pain, back radiation, pulse or BP differential, neurological deficits, new AR murmur, hypertension |
| **Pneumothorax** | Sudden pleuritic pain and breathlessness; tall thin young male or COPD; hyper-resonance, reduced breath sounds |
| **Pneumonia/pleurisy** | Fever, cough, pleuritic pain, focal signs |
| **Pulmonary hypertension** | Exertional pain, breathlessness, syncope, loud P₂, right heart signs |
| **Lung cancer / mesothelioma** | Persistent pain, weight loss, haemoptysis, clubbing, asbestos exposure |
| **Sickle cell acute chest syndrome** | Fever, chest pain, hypoxia in a patient with sickle cell disease |

#### Gastrointestinal causes

| Diagnosis | Features |
|---|---|
| **GORD / oesophagitis** | Burning retrosternal pain after meals or lying flat, regurgitation, relief with antacids |
| **Oesophageal spasm** | Severe squeezing pain, can mimic angina, may respond to GTN, associated with dysphagia |
| **Peptic ulcer, gastritis** | Epigastric pain, relation to meals, NSAID use |
| **Biliary colic / cholecystitis** | Right upper quadrant or epigastric pain, fatty meals, Murphy sign |
| **Pancreatitis** | Epigastric pain radiating to back, relieved leaning forward, vomiting, alcohol or gallstones |
| **Oesophageal rupture (Boerhaave)** | Severe pain after vomiting, subcutaneous emphysema (Hamman crunch), pneumomediastinum, shock |
| **Oesophageal candidiasis / pill oesophagitis** | Painful swallowing; immunosuppression, doxycycline, bisphosphonates |

#### Musculoskeletal and chest wall causes

| Diagnosis | Features |
|---|---|
| **Costochondritis / Tietze syndrome** | Localised tenderness over costochondral junctions, worse with movement or deep breath; benign |
| **Muscle strain, rib fracture** | Trauma, coughing, localised tenderness, pain on movement |
| **Cervical or thoracic radiculopathy** | Positional pain, neck movements, neurological features |
| **Herpes zoster** | Burning dermatomal pain before the rash appears, then vesicles |
| **Fibromyalgia** | Widespread pain, tender points |
| **Bone metastases, myeloma** | Persistent, nocturnal pain, known cancer |

#### Psychogenic and other

- **Panic attacks and anxiety:** chest tightness, hyperventilation, paraesthesia, palpitations, fear; diagnosis of exclusion in high-risk patients, but a common true diagnosis in young patients with typical features.
- **Cocaine and stimulants:** coronary vasospasm, thrombosis, aortic dissection.
- **Anaemia, thyrotoxicosis, severe hypertension:** can provoke angina.
- **Medications:** bisphosphonates (oesophagitis), 5-fluorouracil (vasospasm).

### 22.8 Management principles (brief)

(The full treatment of each condition is beyond the scope of this chapter; the aim is to identify and act.)

#### Suspected acute coronary syndrome

- **Immediate:** monitor, IV access, ECG, oxygen only if SpO₂ <90–94% (routine oxygen may be harmful), analgesia (titrated IV opioid for severe pain; note that opioids may delay absorption of oral antiplatelet drugs), **aspirin** (a loading dose, e.g. 150–300 mg, unless contraindicated), and sublingual or IV nitrates if BP permits (avoid in hypotension, suspected RV infarction, recent phosphodiesterase-5 inhibitors).
- **STEMI:** immediate reperfusion, primary PCI (door-to-balloon target typically within 60–90 minutes) or fibrinolysis if PCI not available within about 120 minutes, plus dual antiplatelet therapy, anticoagulation, and beta-blocker as appropriate.
- **NSTE-ACS:** antiplatelet and anticoagulant therapy, risk stratification (GRACE), early or immediate invasive strategy depending on risk.

#### Suspected pulmonary embolism

- Anticoagulation started if suspicion is high while awaiting imaging; massive PE with shock requires thrombolysis or embolectomy.

#### Suspected aortic dissection

- Analgesia, strict blood pressure and heart rate control (IV beta-blocker first; target systolic around 100–120 mmHg and HR 60 bpm), urgent CT aortography, urgent surgical referral for type A; avoid anticoagulants and thrombolytics.

#### Tension pneumothorax

- Immediate needle decompression, then chest drain.

#### Low-risk chest pain

- Explain the findings and the probable cause; provide specific safety-net advice; ensure follow-up; treat the identified cause (PPI for GORD, analgesia and reassurance for musculoskeletal pain, CBT for panic).

### 22.9 Special populations

- **Women:** More likely to present atypically; historically under-investigated; MINOCA (MI with non-obstructive coronary arteries), SCAD, Takotsubo, and microvascular disease are relatively more common. Apply the same diagnostic rigour.
- **Older adults:** Dyspnoea, syncope or confusion may be the presenting feature; baseline ECG abnormalities are common; troponin is frequently chronically raised (use change).
- **Diabetes and CKD:** Painless ischaemia; chronic troponin elevation in CKD requires dynamic change.
- **Pregnancy:** Consider PE, aortic dissection (third trimester, peripartum), peripartum cardiomyopathy, SCAD, and GORD; use ultrasound and MRI when possible; D-dimer is less specific.
- **Children and adolescents:** Chest pain is rarely cardiac (costochondritis, asthma, pneumonia, anxiety, GORD are most common); look for syncope, exertional pain, family history of sudden death (HCM, long QT, anomalous coronary artery).
- **Cocaine users:** treat as ACS; benzodiazepines first-line; avoid beta-blockers in the acute setting.
- **Post-surgery or cardiac intervention:** consider pericarditis (Dressler), graft failure, sternal infection, PE.

### 22.10 Worked cases

#### Case 1: Typical ACS

*A 62-year-old man with hypertension, diabetes and a 40 pack-year smoking history has 45 minutes of crushing central chest pain radiating to his left arm and jaw, with sweating and nausea. He is pale and clammy. Pulse 98, BP 146/88, SpO₂ 96%. ECG: ST elevation of 3 mm in leads II, III and aVF with reciprocal ST depression in I and aVL.*

**Diagnosis.** Inferior **STEMI.** Check V4R for right ventricular involvement (hypotension and a clear chest should prompt this; avoid nitrates and give fluids if present).

**Action.** Aspirin, antiplatelet, anticoagulation per protocol, analgesia, activate the cath lab for **primary PCI**. Time is myocardium.

#### Case 2: Atypical presentation in a woman with diabetes

*A 71-year-old woman with type 2 diabetes has two days of "indigestion," nausea and mild breathlessness; she thought it was a stomach bug. Pain is minimal. Pulse 92, BP 130/80, SpO₂ 95%. ECG: 1 mm ST depression in V4–V6 and T-wave inversion. Initial hs-troponin is raised and rises further at 2 hours.*

**Diagnosis.** **NSTEMI.** The presentation was atypical (epigastric discomfort, nausea, dyspnoea), typical of older women and diabetic patients.

**Action.** Antiplatelet and anticoagulant therapy, risk stratification (GRACE), early invasive angiography. Do not discharge on a "gastritis" label.

#### Case 3: Pleuritic chest pain in a young woman

*A 29-year-old woman taking the combined oral contraceptive pill has sudden-onset right-sided pleuritic chest pain and breathlessness after a 12-hour flight. HR 112, RR 24, SpO₂ 93%, BP 118/72, temperature 37.4 °C. Chest clear. Calf tenderness on the right. ECG: sinus tachycardia only. Chest X-ray normal.*

**Reasoning.** Pleuritic pain, dyspnoea, tachycardia, hypoxia and risk factors (oestrogen, long flight, leg symptoms). Wells score for PE ≥ moderate (HR >100 +1.5, clinical signs of DVT +3, PE as likely or more likely than alternatives +3). **PE is likely.**

**Action.** Start therapeutic anticoagulation while awaiting CTPA (if no contraindications), stop oestrogen, CTPA, consider leg Doppler, echocardiography if haemodynamically compromised, assess for thrombophilia where appropriate.

#### Case 4: Tearing pain and unequal pulses

*A 55-year-old man with long-standing hypertension has abrupt, severe pain at the front of the chest, spreading between the shoulder blades, "like being ripped apart." BP 188/104 in the right arm, 142/84 in the left. Early diastolic murmur at the left sternal edge. Pulse 100. ECG shows left ventricular hypertrophy only.*

**Reasoning.** ADD-RS = 3 (high-risk pain features, pulse/BP differential and new AR murmur). **Aortic dissection** (probably type A). Immediate imaging, no D-dimer.

**Action.** Resuscitation area, IV beta-blocker, analgesia, **urgent CT aortography**, cardiothoracic surgery. **No anticoagulation or thrombolysis.**

#### Case 5: Low-risk chest pain in a young man

*A 24-year-old man, a non-smoker with no family history of heart disease, has had three days of sharp left-sided chest pain worse on deep breathing and with certain movements, reproducible when pressing on his costal margin. He had a mild viral illness last week. Vital signs normal. ECG normal; hs-troponin normal at presentation and at 3 hours. HEART score 1.*

**Reasoning.** The pain is pleuritic and positional, with reproducible tenderness, a normal ECG and troponin: low risk. The likely diagnosis is **costochondritis or musculoskeletal pain (or a post-viral pleurisy/pericarditis; check for rub and diffuse ST elevation).**

**Action.** Reassure, analgesia (NSAID if no contraindication), safety-net advice (return if exertional pain, breathlessness, fainting, palpitations), review if not improving in a week or two.

#### Case 6: Severe pain after vomiting

*A 48-year-old man with a heavy alcohol binge vomits repeatedly and then develops severe left-sided chest pain, breathlessness and shock. HR 128, BP 88/50, temperature 38.1 °C. There is crepitus in the neck and a left pleural effusion on chest X-ray with mediastinal air.*

**Reasoning.** Forceful vomiting followed by severe chest pain, subcutaneous emphysema and pneumomediastinum: **spontaneous oesophageal rupture (Boerhaave syndrome).**

**Action.** Aggressive resuscitation, broad-spectrum antibiotics and antifungal, nil by mouth, water-soluble contrast study or CT with oral contrast, urgent surgical/thoracic involvement. Mortality rises steeply with delay beyond 24 hours.

#### Case 7: Chest pain with a negative work-up and persistent concern

*A 58-year-old woman has had three emergency visits in two months for chest pain. Each time ECGs and single troponins were normal and she was discharged with a diagnosis of "anxiety." She now describes exertional chest tightness while walking uphill, which eases with rest, and her sister has had stents at 50.*

**Reasoning.** The history is now typical of **stable angina**, with a family history of premature coronary disease. Previous attempts to rule out ACS during pain-free periods do not exclude chronic coronary disease. **A diagnostic label of anxiety should not be applied to a patient with an unexplored typical history** (Chapter 2: psych-out error and posterior probability error).

**Action.** Risk assessment and functional or anatomical testing (CT coronary angiography or stress imaging), trial of anti-anginal therapy and secondary prevention if confirmed.

### 22.11 Common pitfalls

- **Anchoring on a benign cause** because of age, sex, or a previous diagnosis.
- **Relying on a single negative troponin** too early after symptom onset, or without a validated pathway.
- **Assuming that reproducible chest wall tenderness** excludes cardiac disease.
- **Taking GTN response as diagnostic.**
- **Ignoring atypical presentations** in women, older adults and diabetic patients.
- **Missing PE and dissection** because the chest X-ray and ECG are non-specific.
- **Giving anticoagulants or thrombolytics** to a patient with undiagnosed dissection.
- **Calling every chest pain "reflux"** without considering ACS.
- **Treating raised troponin as MI** without considering alternative causes of myocardial injury.
- **Labelling as anxiety** without full assessment.
- **Failing to give clear safety-net advice** on discharge.

---

### 22.12 Key points

- First exclude the life-threatening causes: ACS, PE, aortic dissection, tension pneumothorax, tamponade, oesophageal rupture.
- The history is the key: character, radiation, exertion, onset, associated symptoms and risk factors.
- ECG within 10 minutes; repeat if pain continues; use serial high-sensitivity troponin in a validated pathway.
- Women, older adults and people with diabetes often present atypically.
- Use risk scores (HEART, Wells, PERC, ADD-RS) to structure decisions but not to replace judgement.
- Reproducible tenderness, GTN response and a normal examination do not exclude serious disease.
- Troponin elevation means myocardial injury, not necessarily MI.
- Provide explicit safety-netting and follow-up when discharging.

### 22.13 Self-assessment

1. Define typical angina.
   *Answer: Substernal chest discomfort of characteristic quality and duration, provoked by exertion or emotional stress, and relieved by rest or nitroglycerin (all three features).*

2. List five life-threatening causes of chest pain.
   *Answer (any five): ACS, pulmonary embolism, aortic dissection, tension pneumothorax, cardiac tamponade, oesophageal rupture, myocarditis/pericarditis with complications, pneumonia with sepsis.*

3. Describe the ECG of acute pericarditis.
   *Answer: Diffuse concave ST elevation with PR depression (PR elevation in aVR), without reciprocal changes.*

4. Why may a single normal troponin taken at arrival not exclude MI?
   *Answer: Troponin rises 1–3 hours after myocardial injury; a very early sample may be below the threshold and requires repeat measurement in a validated pathway.*

5. Which three clinical features give a point on the ADD-RS "pain" and "examination" categories?
   *Answer: Abrupt, severe or ripping/tearing pain; pulse deficit or systolic BP differential; new aortic regurgitation murmur with pain, focal neurological deficit with pain, hypotension.*

6. Why should anticoagulants not be given before aortic dissection is excluded in a patient with tearing chest pain?
   *Answer: They can increase bleeding into the false lumen and rupture; they are harmful in dissection.*
