# Chapter 52. Musculoskeletal and Rheumatology

## Learning objectives

After studying this chapter you should be able to:

1. Apply a structured approach to the patient with joint pain: inflammatory or mechanical, monoarticular or polyarticular, acute or chronic.
2. Recognise the septic joint and other musculoskeletal emergencies and act within hours.
3. Diagnose and manage osteoarthritis, gout and pseudogout, rheumatoid arthritis, spondyloarthritis and psoriatic arthritis.
4. Recognise systemic autoimmune disease: lupus, systemic sclerosis, Sjögren syndrome, myositis, and the vasculitides including giant cell arteritis.
5. Interpret autoantibody tests, inflammatory markers, synovial fluid and imaging with attention to pre-test probability.
6. Diagnose and treat osteoporosis, assess fracture risk, and recognise other metabolic bone disease.
7. Evaluate common regional and soft-tissue syndromes, fibromyalgia, and polymyalgia rheumatica.
8. Use immunosuppressive drugs safely: screening, monitoring and infection risk.

> **Note on thresholds and doses.** Classification criteria, treatment targets and doses follow commonly used guidance (ACR, EULAR, NICE, BHSF, Endocrine Society, NOGG) at the time of writing. Criteria are classification tools for research, not diagnostic rules; use clinical judgement. Verify drug doses and monitoring schedules against your national formulary.

## 1. The approach to joint and musculoskeletal symptoms

### 1.1 Five questions

1. **Is it articular, peri-articular or non-articular?** True joint disease causes pain and limitation on both active and passive movement in all directions (capsular pattern), with swelling or effusion. Tendon, bursa and enthesis problems hurt on specific movements and resisted testing. Muscle and referred pain follow different patterns.
2. **Inflammatory or mechanical?**
3. **How many joints, and which?** Mono-, oligo- (2–4) or polyarticular (5 or more); symmetrical or asymmetrical; small or large; axial involvement.
4. **What is the tempo?** Acute (hours to days), subacute, chronic.
5. **Is there a systemic disease?** Fever, rash, eye disease, mucosal ulcers, weight loss, renal, lung or neurological involvement.

### 1.2 Inflammatory versus mechanical pain

| Feature | Inflammatory | Mechanical (degenerative) |
|---|---|---|
| Morning stiffness | > 30–60 min | < 30 min |
| Effect of activity | Improves | Worsens |
| Rest | Worsens ("gelling") | Improves |
| Night pain | Common, second half of night (spondyloarthritis) | With position, late stage |
| Swelling | Soft, warm, boggy synovitis | Hard bony swelling (osteophytes) |
| Systemic features | Fatigue, fever, weight loss | None |
| CRP/ESR | Often raised | Normal |

### 1.3 The patterns

| Pattern | Think of |
|---|---|
| Acute monoarthritis | Septic arthritis, gout, pseudogout, trauma/haemarthrosis, reactive arthritis, early inflammatory arthritis |
| Symmetrical small-joint polyarthritis (hands) | Rheumatoid arthritis, viral arthritis (parvovirus B19, hepatitis B/C, chikungunya), lupus, early psoriatic |
| Asymmetric oligoarthritis, lower limb, dactylitis, enthesitis | Spondyloarthritis (reactive, psoriatic, enteropathic) |
| DIP joints, Heberden/Bouchard nodes | Osteoarthritis, psoriatic arthritis |
| Thumb base, 2nd–3rd MCP hooks | OA of CMC; haemochromatosis (2nd–3rd MCP) |
| Axial pain, inflammatory back pain | Axial spondyloarthritis |
| Proximal girdle pain and stiffness, age > 50 | Polymyalgia rheumatica, myositis, hypothyroidism, statin myopathy |
| Widespread pain, fatigue, poor sleep, normal exam | Fibromyalgia (a diagnosis of positive criteria, not exclusion) |
| Arthralgia with rash, cytopenias, renal disease | Lupus |

### 1.4 History

- Onset and tempo; trigger (trauma, infection, new drug, travel, sexual exposure, tick bites).
- Morning stiffness (duration), night pain, response to NSAIDs (a dramatic response is typical of spondyloarthritis).
- Distribution and symmetry; ability to make a fist; difficulty with fine tasks.
- Extra-articular features: psoriasis, nail pitting, uveitis (red painful eye), diarrhoea, urethritis, oral ulcers, Raynaud, photosensitive rash, sicca, dysphagia, dyspnoea, haematuria.
- Family history (psoriasis, IBD, ankylosing spondylitis, gout), medications (diuretics, aspirin, ciclosporin, statins, quinolones, checkpoint inhibitors), alcohol, diet, occupation, falls.

### 1.5 Examination: look, feel, move

Use the screening approach "GALS" (gait, arms, legs, spine) and then examine the affected joints (chapter 16). Distinguish:

- **Synovitis**: warm, boggy swelling over the joint line, tenderness along the joint, painful end-range movement, limited extension, effusion (bulge or ballotable patella).
- **Bony swelling**: hard, non-tender or mildly tender, crepitus.
- **Enthesitis**: tenderness at tendon insertion (Achilles, plantar fascia).
- **Tenosynovitis**: sausage swelling along tendons, crepitus; **dactylitis**: whole digit swollen.
- Examine skin, nails, eyes, mouth, nodes, heart, lungs, and urine.

### 1.6 Investigations: use with a purpose

- **Not all patients need blood tests.** Typical osteoarthritis needs none.
- **FBC, CRP/ESR, creatinine, liver function, urate** in most inflammatory presentations; urinalysis for any suspected connective tissue disease.
- **Rheumatoid factor (RF) and anti-CCP antibodies** only if synovitis is present (specificity of RF is about 85% with many false positives in older adults, infections, and other diseases; anti-CCP is about 95% specific for rheumatoid arthritis and predicts erosions).
- **ANA** (antinuclear antibodies) for suspected connective tissue disease: sensitive (above 95% for lupus at 1:160 on HEp-2 cells) but not specific (positive in up to 20–30% of healthy people at 1:40, and in 5% at 1:160). Do not order as a screen in widespread pain. If positive, test for **ENA** (anti-Ro/SSA, La/SSB, Sm, RNP, Scl-70, Jo-1, centromere) and **anti-dsDNA**, complement C3/C4.
- **HLA-B27**: supports but does not make the diagnosis of axial spondyloarthritis (present in about 8% of Europeans, and in 80–90% of those with ankylosing spondylitis).
- **Uric acid** is often normal during an acute attack of gout. Do not use it to exclude gout.
- **Imaging**: X-ray (erosions, joint-space narrowing, osteophytes, chondrocalcinosis, sacroiliitis); ultrasound (synovitis, effusion, erosions, double contour sign in gout, enthesitis, guided injection); MRI (sacroiliac bone marrow oedema, early erosions, soft-tissue, osteomyelitis); DEXA (bone density); dual-energy CT (urate crystal deposits).
- **Synovial fluid analysis**: the single most useful test in an acute swollen joint (section 2).

## 2. Acute monoarthritis and the septic joint

### 2.1 Septic arthritis is an emergency

Mortality is 5–15% and a delay of even a few days risks permanent cartilage destruction. Suspect it in any acutely red, hot, swollen, painful joint with restricted movement, with or without fever. Fever is absent in about 40% of adults, particularly older adults, diabetic or immunosuppressed patients.

Risk factors: pre-existing joint disease (especially rheumatoid arthritis), prosthetic joints, diabetes, immunosuppression, intravenous drug use, recent joint injection or surgery, skin infection, age over 80, bacteraemia, and HIV. The knee is affected in about half of cases. *Staphylococcus aureus* is the commonest organism, then streptococci; *Neisseria gonorrhoeae* in young, sexually active adults (disseminated gonococcal infection: migratory polyarthralgia, tenosynovitis, pustular skin lesions); Gram-negative organisms in intravenous drug users and the elderly; *Kingella kingae* in young children; Lyme disease in endemic areas.

### 2.2 Management

1. **Arthrocentesis before antibiotics** (blood cultures first, then aspirate; do not delay antibiotics more than an hour in a septic patient). Send fluid for Gram stain, culture (inoculate blood culture bottles too), cell count with differential, and crystals by polarised microscopy.
2. Interpretation of synovial fluid:

| | Normal | Non-inflammatory (OA) | Inflammatory (gout, RA) | Septic |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Appearance | Clear | Clear, yellow | Cloudy | Turbid, purulent |
| WBC (/µL) | < 200 | 200–2,000 | 2,000–50,000 | > 50,000 (often > 100,000) |
| Neutrophils | < 25% | < 25% | > 50% | > 75–90% |
| Crystals | None | None | Possible | Possible: crystals do not exclude infection |
| Culture | Negative | Negative | Negative | Positive in ~70–80% |

A low WBC count does not exclude infection (especially early or with gonococcus). **Crystals and infection coexist in a few percent of cases.**

3. **Empirical IV antibiotics** (flucloxacillin or cefazolin; vancomycin if MRSA risk; add a third-generation cephalosporin if Gram-negative or gonococcal risk), narrowed by culture, for 2 weeks IV and then oral to a total of 4–6 weeks (shorter courses of 2–4 weeks are accepted in some native joint infections with prompt drainage).
4. **Drainage**: repeated needle aspiration, arthroscopic washout, or open drainage; the hip and shoulder usually need surgical drainage. Prosthetic joint infection: orthopaedic referral for debridement/implant retention or revision.
5. Immobilise briefly, analgesia, then early mobilisation.
6. Look for the source: blood cultures, endocarditis (new murmur), skin, urine, dental, HIV, and intravenous drug use.

In children, an irritable hip with fever, inability to bear weight, ESR above 40 and white cells above 12,000/µL (Kocher criteria) predicts septic arthritis; transient synovitis is the commonest cause of irritable hip but is a diagnosis of exclusion.

### 2.3 Other causes of acute monoarthritis

- **Gout and pseudogout** (section 4).
- **Haemarthrosis**: trauma (ligament or fracture), anticoagulation, haemophilia, tumour (pigmented villonodular synovitis).
- **Reactive arthritis**: 1–4 weeks after urogenital (*Chlamydia*) or enteric (*Salmonella*, *Shigella*, *Campylobacter*, *Yersinia*) infection; asymmetric lower limb oligoarthritis, enthesitis, conjunctivitis, urethritis, circinate balanitis, keratoderma blennorrhagica.
- **Lyme arthritis**, **viral arthritis**.
- **Early rheumatoid or psoriatic arthritis**, **juvenile idiopathic arthritis** in children.
- **Fracture, avascular necrosis, osteomyelitis, bone tumour**, periarticular conditions mimicking arthritis (bursitis, cellulitis, cellulitis overlying a joint).

## 3. Osteoarthritis

### 3.1 Diagnosis

Osteoarthritis (OA) is a whole-joint disease of cartilage, bone and synovium, and the most common joint disorder. Risk factors: age, female sex, obesity, previous injury or occupational overload, malalignment, genetics. Typical sites: knees, hips, hands (DIP, PIP, first CMC), spine (facet joints), and first MTP.

**Clinical diagnosis** in adults over 45 with activity-related joint pain, no or brief (< 30 min) morning stiffness, and typical distribution. X-ray is not needed to diagnose or to guide treatment in typical cases and correlates poorly with symptoms. Features: crepitus, bony enlargement, restricted movement, joint-line tenderness, small effusion, deformity. Consider other diagnoses if there is prolonged morning stiffness, warm swelling, systemic symptoms, rapid progression, or atypical joint involvement (MCP, wrist, shoulder, elbow: think haemochromatosis, calcium pyrophosphate disease, inflammatory arthritis).

### 3.2 Management

Core treatments, which every patient should receive:

1. **Education and self-management.**
2. **Exercise**: strengthening and aerobic exercise reduce pain and improve function to a degree similar to NSAIDs; tai chi and aquatic exercise are good options.
3. **Weight loss** if overweight: 5–10% weight loss improves symptoms; each kilogram lost removes about 4 kg of load across the knee.

Further options:

- **Topical NSAIDs** first for knee and hand OA (few systemic effects).
- **Oral NSAIDs** at the lowest effective dose for the shortest time (cardiovascular, gastrointestinal and renal risk; co-prescribe a proton pump inhibitor in at-risk patients; avoid in heart failure, CKD, those on anticoagulants).
- **Paracetamol** has little benefit in OA; **opioids** are not recommended (including tramadol, little benefit and notable harm). **Duloxetine** can be used for knee OA in selected patients.
- **Intra-articular corticosteroid injection** (short-term relief of weeks; repeated injections may accelerate cartilage loss; do not inject more than 3–4 times a year into the same joint). Hyaluronic acid has no or small benefit; platelet-rich plasma is under study.
- **Glucosamine and chondroitin** do not work reliably.
- Aids: walking stick (contralateral hand), insoles, knee brace in specific cases, heat packs.
- **Joint replacement** when pain and disability persist despite maximal conservative treatment and quality of life is substantially impaired; outcomes are excellent for hips and good for knees.

## 4. Crystal arthropathies

### 4.1 Gout

Gout is an inflammatory arthritis caused by monosodium urate crystals in people with hyperuricaemia (serum urate > 0.42 mmol/L, 7 mg/dL, saturation point). Prevalence up to 3–4% in adults in high-income countries; more common in men and in postmenopausal women.

**Acute attack**: abrupt, excruciating monoarthritis, peaking within 12–24 hours, with striking erythema and exquisite tenderness (even a sheet is intolerable). The first MTP joint ("podagra") is classic (about half of first attacks), then midfoot, ankle, knee, wrist, fingers. Fever may occur. Triggers: alcohol (beer, spirits), purine-rich food (red meat, organ meats, shellfish), fructose-sweetened drinks, dehydration, surgery, trauma, starting or stopping urate-lowering therapy, diuretics, low-dose aspirin, ciclosporin.

**Chronic tophaceous gout**: tophi (chalky nodules in the pinna, olecranon bursa, fingers, Achilles), erosions with overhanging edges ("punched out"), chronic synovitis, kidney stones, and urate nephropathy.

**Diagnosis**: definitive diagnosis is **negatively birefringent needle-shaped crystals** on polarised light microscopy of synovial fluid or tophus aspirate. In the typical podagra in a man with hyperuricaemia and risk factors, a clinical diagnosis is acceptable. Ultrasound ("double contour sign", tophi, "snowstorm") and dual-energy CT help. Check serum urate (it may be falsely normal in the attack; recheck 2–4 weeks later), creatinine, glucose, lipids, blood pressure. A joint aspiration is required whenever infection is a realistic possibility.

Look for causes and comorbidities: chronic kidney disease, hypertension, diabetes, obesity, metabolic syndrome, cardiovascular disease, alcohol, drugs (thiazides, loop diuretics, low-dose aspirin, ciclosporin, pyrazinamide), myeloproliferative disease, psoriasis, tumour lysis, and in young patients enzyme defects.

**Treatment of an acute attack** (start as early as possible; continue ongoing urate-lowering therapy if already taking):

- **NSAIDs** (naproxen 500 mg twice daily, indomethacin 50 mg three times daily, etoricoxib 120 mg once daily) for 5–7 days, with a PPI if indicated; avoid in CKD, heart failure, peptic ulcer, anticoagulation.
- **Colchicine** 0.5 mg two or three times a day (a low-dose regimen is as effective as high dose with less diarrhoea; maximum 1.5–2 mg on the first day); adjust for renal function; **interacts with clarithromycin, ciclosporin and strong CYP3A4/P-glycoprotein inhibitors: risk of toxicity**.
- **Corticosteroids**: prednisolone 30–40 mg daily for 5 days, or intra-articular injection after infection is excluded. The safest option in CKD and those at risk from NSAIDs.
- IL-1 inhibitors (anakinra, canakinumab) for refractory cases. Rest and ice help.

**Long-term urate-lowering therapy (ULT)** is recommended after a second attack, or at first attack when tophi, urate arthropathy, CKD stage 3 or above, urate above 0.54 mmol/L (9 mg/dL), or urolithiasis exist. Discuss early; an informed patient is more adherent. 

- **Allopurinol** is first-line. Start at 50–100 mg daily (lower in CKD) and **titrate every 2–4 weeks** by 50–100 mg to a serum urate target below 0.36 mmol/L (6 mg/dL), and below 0.30 mmol/L (5 mg/dL) in tophaceous gout. Doses of 300 mg or more are often needed, safely, even in CKD if increased slowly. **HLA-B\*58:01 screening** is advised in Han Chinese, Thai, Korean, and patients of African ancestry (risk of severe cutaneous adverse reactions: SJS/TEN, DRESS).
- **Febuxostat** if allopurinol is not tolerated (cardiovascular safety warning in patients with established CVD). **Benzbromarone / probenecid**: uricosurics in selected patients. **Pegloticase** for refractory tophaceous gout.
- **Prophylaxis against flares**: when starting ULT, give low-dose colchicine (0.5 mg once or twice daily) or low-dose NSAID for at least 3–6 months, because lowering urate mobilises crystals and may provoke attacks.
- Lifestyle: weight loss, less alcohol (particularly beer and spirits), fewer sugar-sweetened drinks and purine-rich foods; diet alone rarely reduces urate enough. Review diuretics (switch hypertension treatment to losartan or a calcium-channel blocker if appropriate).
- Treat comorbidities.

**Asymptomatic hyperuricaemia** generally should not be treated.

### 4.2 Calcium pyrophosphate deposition disease (pseudogout)

Calcium pyrophosphate (CPP) crystals cause acute arthritis (usually in knees or wrists), chronic arthritis resembling OA in atypical joints, or are asymptomatic (chondrocalcinosis on X-ray). Peaks in people over 65. Triggers: intercurrent illness, surgery, trauma. Search for metabolic causes in young patients (under 55) or polyarticular disease: **haemochromatosis, hyperparathyroidism, hypomagnesaemia, hypophosphatasia, hypothyroidism**. Diagnosis: **positively birefringent rhomboid crystals** on microscopy; chondrocalcinosis on X-ray or ultrasound. Treatment: as for acute gout (NSAIDs, colchicine, corticosteroids, intra-articular steroids); no effective crystal-dissolving therapy.

## 5. Rheumatoid arthritis

### 5.1 Clinical features

Chronic symmetric inflammatory polyarthritis of small joints (MCP, PIP, wrists, MTP) with morning stiffness of an hour or more, fatigue, and weight loss. Peak onset 40–60, women about 3 times more often than men. DIP joints are spared. Smoking is the strongest environmental risk factor.

Examination: soft, tender swelling of MCPs and PIPs, squeeze (compression) test tenderness of MCP/MTP, reduced grip, wrist swelling, tenosynovitis. Late deformities (ulnar deviation, swan-neck and boutonnière, Z-thumb, subluxations).

Extra-articular features: subcutaneous nodules (extensor surfaces, seropositive disease), interstitial lung disease and pleural effusion, pericarditis, scleritis/episcleritis, Sjögren syndrome, vasculitis, anaemia of chronic disease, amyloidosis, Felty syndrome (RA with splenomegaly and neutropenia), osteoporosis, cardiovascular disease (risk equivalent to diabetes). **Cervical spine instability** (atlantoaxial subluxation): warn anaesthetists; neck pain, neurological signs.

### 5.2 Diagnosis

Early diagnosis matters: a **window of opportunity** exists in the first 3–6 months in which treatment achieves remission and prevents erosions. Refer any patient with persistent synovitis in more than one joint, particularly small joints, for more than 6 weeks (many pathways say refer at once, within 3 weeks).

The 2010 ACR/EULAR classification criteria use a score of at least 6 across four domains: joint involvement (number and size), serology (RF/anti-CCP; high titres score more), acute phase reactants, and symptom duration (≥ 6 weeks). Tests: RF, anti-CCP, CRP/ESR, FBC, liver and renal function, hepatitis B/C and HIV serology, TB screening (before biologics), X-rays of hands and feet (erosions often missing early), ultrasound or MRI.

Differential: viral arthritis (parvovirus B19, hepatitis B/C, rubella, chikungunya), psoriatic arthritis, lupus, OA (nodal), polymyalgia rheumatica in the elderly, gout, pseudogout (older adults), reactive arthritis, sarcoidosis, haemochromatosis, fibromyalgia.

### 5.3 Treatment: treat to target

The goal is **remission or low disease activity** (DAS28, CDAI, SDAI) assessed every 1–3 months, with treatment escalated if not reached by 3 months (or no improvement) or 6 months.

1. **Methotrexate** is the anchor drug: 7.5–15 mg once weekly, escalated to 20–25 mg (subcutaneous at higher doses), with **folic acid 5 mg weekly** (on a different day). **Weekly, not daily**: daily dosing errors are fatal. Monitor FBC, creatinine, and liver tests every 2 weeks for the first 6 weeks and then every 1–3 months. Contraindicated in pregnancy (stop 3 months before conception in both sexes), significant renal or hepatic impairment, and heavy alcohol use. Avoid co-trimoxazole and trimethoprim (bone marrow suppression). Pneumonitis is rare but serious.
2. **Short-term glucocorticoids** as bridging (low-dose prednisolone, intramuscular methylprednisolone 120 mg, or intra-articular injection) while the DMARD acts, tapered as soon as possible.
3. **Add or switch**: sulfasalazine, leflunomide, hydroxychloroquine (triple therapy), and then **biological DMARDs** (TNF inhibitors: adalimumab, etanercept, infliximab, certolizumab, golimumab; abatacept; tocilizumab and sarilumab; rituximab) or **targeted synthetic DMARDs** (JAK inhibitors: tofacitinib, baricitinib, upadacitinib: caution in patients with cardiovascular risk factors, older than 65, smokers, with VTE risk or malignancy risk per regulatory warnings).
4. **Before biologics or JAK inhibitors**: screen for latent and active tuberculosis (interferon-gamma release assay, chest X-ray), hepatitis B and C, HIV; update vaccines (inactivated influenza, pneumococcal, COVID-19, zoster recombinant vaccine; **avoid live vaccines** on therapy).
5. **NSAIDs** for symptomatic relief only.
6. Non-drug: occupational therapy, physiotherapy, joint protection, foot care, smoking cessation.
7. Manage comorbidity: cardiovascular risk (statin, blood pressure), osteoporosis, infection, lung disease, depression.
8. Pregnancy: plan ahead; use sulfasalazine, hydroxychloroquine, azathioprine, TNF inhibitors (certolizumab and etanercept with minimal placental transfer) as compatible; stop methotrexate and leflunomide (washout with colestyramine) before conception.

## 6. Spondyloarthritis and psoriatic arthritis

### 6.1 Axial spondyloarthritis (axSpA, including ankylosing spondylitis)

Inflammatory back pain in a young person (onset before 45, insidious, duration > 3 months, improving with exercise but not rest, night pain in the second half, alternating buttock pain, morning stiffness > 30 min, dramatic response to NSAIDs) with: HLA-B27, sacroiliitis on MRI (bone marrow oedema) or radiographs (erosions, sclerosis, fusion), uveitis (acute anterior, unilateral, recurrent: 25–40% of patients), enthesitis, dactylitis, psoriasis, IBD, family history.

Examination: reduced lumbar flexion (modified Schober test below 5 cm), reduced chest expansion (< 2.5 cm), loss of lateral flexion, occiput-to-wall distance. Late: "question-mark" posture, fusion ("bamboo spine").

Management: education, regular exercise and physiotherapy (cornerstone), continuous NSAIDs (naproxen, etoricoxib), then biologics if the disease remains active (BASDAI ≥ 4) after 2 NSAIDs over 4 weeks: TNF inhibitors or IL-17 inhibitors (secukinumab, ixekizumab) or JAK inhibitors. Methotrexate and sulfasalazine do not help axial disease (sulfasalazine helps peripheral arthritis). Watch: fracture of a stiff spine after minor trauma (a "stable-looking" painful spine in AS may be fractured: CT/MRI), cardiac conduction disease, aortic regurgitation, osteoporosis.

### 6.2 Psoriatic arthritis (PsA)

Affects up to 30% of people with psoriasis, often years after skin disease (in 10–15% the arthritis comes first). Patterns: oligoarthritis, polyarthritis resembling RA (RF-negative), DIP disease (with nail pitting and onycholysis), spondylitis, and arthritis mutilans. Characteristics: **dactylitis, enthesitis, nail changes, asymmetry**. X-ray: erosions with adjacent bone proliferation, "pencil-in-cup", periostitis. Screen with PEST or CASPAR criteria. Treatment: NSAIDs, intra-articular steroid, csDMARDs (methotrexate, leflunomide, sulfasalazine) for peripheral disease, then biologics selected according to dominant domains (TNF, IL-17, IL-12/23, IL-23 inhibitors, JAK inhibitors, apremilast), and treat skin and eye disease together.

### 6.3 Enteropathic and reactive arthritis

Inflammatory bowel disease-associated arthritis (peripheral type 1 mirrors gut activity; type 2 and axial disease are independent). Reactive arthritis: see section 2.3; treat with NSAIDs, steroid injection, sulfasalazine for persistent disease; antibiotics for chlamydial infection (tetracycline) but not shown to alter arthritis in general.

## 7. Connective tissue diseases and vasculitis

### 7.1 Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE)

A multisystem autoimmune disease with a strong female predominance (about 9:1), onset at 15–45, higher prevalence and severity in people of African, Asian and Hispanic ancestry.

**Manifestations**: constitutional (fatigue, fever, weight loss), mucocutaneous (malar rash sparing nasolabial folds, discoid lesions, photosensitivity, oral/nasal ulcers, alopecia), musculoskeletal (non-erosive arthritis, tendon involvement), haematological (haemolytic anaemia, leukopenia/lymphopenia, thrombocytopenia), renal (lupus nephritis), neuropsychiatric (seizures, psychosis, headache, stroke, neuropathy), serositis (pleuritis, pericarditis), and antiphospholipid syndrome (thrombosis, pregnancy morbidity).

**Diagnosis**: clinical judgement with the 2019 EULAR/ACR classification criteria (ANA ≥ 1:80 as entry criterion, then weighted clinical and immunological criteria). Tests: ANA (nearly all), anti-dsDNA (specific, correlates with nephritis), anti-Sm (very specific), complement C3 and C4 (low in active disease), antiphospholipid antibodies (lupus anticoagulant, anticardiolipin, anti-β2-glycoprotein I), FBC with differential, direct Coombs test, ESR (CRP is often normal unless serositis or infection), urinalysis, urine protein:creatinine ratio, renal function.

**Management**: hydroxychloroquine for all patients (5 mg/kg/day maximum actual body weight; annual eye screening after 5 years), sun protection, low-dose glucocorticoids, and immunosuppressants for organ-threatening disease: mycophenolate or cyclophosphamide for proliferative lupus nephritis, azathioprine, methotrexate, belimumab, anifrolumab, voclosporin, rituximab. Vaccinate, manage cardiovascular risk and bone health, plan pregnancy during quiescent disease. **Check anti-Ro/SSA in pregnancy**: congenital heart block risk about 2% (monitor fetal heart; hydroxychloroquine reduces recurrence). Drug-induced lupus (hydralazine, procainamide, isoniazid, minocycline, TNF inhibitors): anti-histone antibodies, kidneys and CNS spared; resolves after stopping the drug.

### 7.2 Antiphospholipid syndrome

Arterial or venous thrombosis, or pregnancy morbidity (recurrent early miscarriages, fetal death, severe pre-eclampsia, placental insufficiency) plus persistent (≥ 12 weeks apart) antiphospholipid antibodies. Treat thrombosis with long-term warfarin (INR 2–3); DOACs are inferior in high-risk (triple-positive) arthropathy and arterial events. In pregnancy: low-dose aspirin plus prophylactic heparin.

### 7.3 Systemic sclerosis

**Raynaud phenomenon** (often the first symptom), skin thickening (starting in fingers, "sclerodactyly"; limited cutaneous disease ends at elbows/knees; diffuse involves proximal limbs and trunk), puffy fingers, digital ulcers, telangiectasia, calcinosis, GI involvement (reflux, dysphagia, bowel dysmotility), interstitial lung disease, pulmonary arterial hypertension, and **scleroderma renal crisis** (abrupt hypertension and AKI, most often in early diffuse disease on steroids: treat urgently with an ACE inhibitor). Antibodies: anti-centromere (limited disease, PAH), anti-Scl-70 (topoisomerase; diffuse disease, ILD), anti-RNA polymerase III (diffuse, renal crisis, cancer association). Annual echocardiography and lung function tests; treatment: vasodilators (calcium-channel blockers, phosphodiesterase-5 inhibitors, iloprost, bosentan), proton pump inhibitors, immunosuppression (mycophenolate, tocilizumab, stem cell transplant for selected severe diffuse disease), and organ-specific care.

### 7.4 Sjögren syndrome

Dry eyes and mouth (sicca), parotid swelling, fatigue, arthralgia; extraglandular disease (neuropathy, interstitial lung disease, renal tubular acidosis, vasculitis); lymphoma risk (about 5% over time). Anti-Ro/SSA, anti-La/SSB, Schirmer test, salivary gland biopsy. Treat with artificial tears and saliva substitutes, pilocarpine, hydroxychloroquine for arthralgia, immunosuppressants for organ involvement; screen for lymphoma (persistent parotid swelling, cryoglobulinaemia, low C4).

### 7.5 Inflammatory myopathies

Proximal symmetric muscle weakness over weeks to months (difficulty rising from a chair, combing hair), with raised CK. Dermatomyositis: heliotrope rash, Gottron papules, shawl sign, periungual changes, with cancer risk (screen). Polymyositis and immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (statins: anti-HMGCR). Inclusion body myositis: older patients, finger flexor and quadriceps weakness, slowly progressive and poorly responsive to treatment. Antisynthetase syndrome (anti-Jo-1): myositis, interstitial lung disease, mechanic's hands, arthritis, Raynaud. Diagnosis: CK, aldolase, myositis-specific antibodies, MRI, EMG, muscle biopsy. Treatment: glucocorticoids plus methotrexate/azathioprine, IVIG, rituximab for resistant cases.

**Differential for raised CK with weakness**: statin myopathy/myositis, hypothyroidism, alcohol, drugs (colchicine, fibrates, daptomycin), electrolyte disorders (hypokalaemia, hypophosphataemia), muscular dystrophy, metabolic myopathy (McArdle), rhabdomyolysis, infection (viral), and exercise. Check TSH, calcium, potassium, creatinine, urine myoglobin.

### 7.6 Giant cell arteritis (GCA) and polymyalgia rheumatica (PMR)

**GCA** is an emergency because it causes irreversible blindness. Age over 50 (usually > 70), new headache, scalp tenderness, jaw claudication (most specific), visual disturbance (amaurosis fugax, diplopia), fever, malaise, weight loss, ESR often > 50 mm/h and raised CRP, thrombocytosis, anaemia; 20–50% have PMR features. Examination: tender, nodular, pulseless temporal arteries; fundoscopy (pale swollen disc: arteritic anterior ischaemic optic neuropathy). Large-vessel GCA presents with arm claudication, bruits, absent pulses and fever of unknown origin.

**Management**: if suspected, start **prednisolone immediately** (40–60 mg/day; IV methylprednisolone 500–1,000 mg daily for 3 days if visual symptoms) without waiting for a biopsy, and arrange urgent specialist review. Biopsy (temporal artery) within 1–2 weeks of steroids is still often positive (up to 6 weeks); ultrasound of temporal and axillary arteries ("halo sign") is increasingly used as the first-line test. Taper steroids slowly guided by symptoms and CRP over 1–2 years; add tocilizumab (reduces steroid exposure) or methotrexate. Give bone protection (calcium, vitamin D, bisphosphonate) and aspirin if indicated. Monitor for aortic aneurysm.

**PMR**: age over 50, bilateral shoulder and hip girdle pain and stiffness lasting more than 45 minutes, raised ESR/CRP, and a dramatic response to low-dose prednisolone (12.5–25 mg daily, 15 mg typical; tapering over 12–24 months). Exclude mimics: RA, myositis, hypothyroidism, statin myopathy, malignancy, myeloma, infection. Ask about GCA symptoms at each review.

### 7.7 Other vasculitides

ANCA-associated vasculitis (see chapter 49), IgA vasculitis, polyarteritis nodosa (hepatitis B, mononeuritis multiplex, hypertension, abdominal pain, livedo reticularis), Takayasu arteritis (young women, absent pulses), Behçet disease (oral and genital ulcers, uveitis, pathergy), Kawasaki disease in children (fever ≥ 5 days, conjunctivitis, rash, extremity changes, adenopathy; give IVIG and aspirin within 10 days to prevent coronary aneurysms). A unifying clue: a patient with fever, weight loss, multisystem disease, high inflammatory markers and a bland infectious work-up.

## 8. Osteoporosis and metabolic bone disease

### 8.1 Osteoporosis: the problem

A skeletal disorder of reduced bone strength causing fragility fractures: fractures from a fall at standing height or less. One in two women and one in five men over 50 suffer an osteoporotic fracture. **Vertebral** fractures (often unrecognised: height loss, kyphosis, back pain), **hip** fractures (one-year mortality 20–30%), distal radius, proximal humerus.

Secondary causes (found in up to 30% of women and 50–60% of men): glucocorticoids (≥ 5 mg prednisolone daily for ≥ 3 months), hypogonadism and androgen deprivation therapy, aromatase inhibitors, hyperthyroidism, hyperparathyroidism, vitamin D deficiency, malabsorption (coeliac, IBD, bariatric surgery), CKD, chronic liver disease, myeloma, anorexia, alcohol, smoking, anticonvulsants, PPIs, SSRIs, thiazolidinediones, long-term heparin, rheumatoid arthritis, diabetes, low BMI, early menopause, immobility.

### 8.2 Assessment

- Risk factors and a **fracture risk calculator** (FRAX, QFracture, Garvan) for adults over 50 (or younger with risk factors).
- **DEXA** at hip and lumbar spine: T-score ≤ −2.5 defines osteoporosis; −1 to −2.5 osteopenia. Fragility fracture of the spine or hip diagnoses osteoporosis irrespective of the T-score. In younger adults and children use Z-scores.
- Vertebral imaging: lateral spine X-ray or DEXA vertebral fracture assessment when height loss > 4 cm, back pain, kyphosis.
- Laboratory work-up: calcium, albumin, phosphate, creatinine/eGFR, 25-OH vitamin D, PTH, alkaline phosphatase, TSH, FBC, ESR, liver function, coeliac serology, protein electrophoresis; testosterone in men; consider urinary calcium and 24-hour cortisol if suspected.
- Falls assessment (see chapter 61).

### 8.3 Treatment

1. **Lifestyle**: weight-bearing and resistance exercise, balance training, adequate protein, calcium intake of 1,000–1,200 mg/day (diet first, supplements to fill gaps), vitamin D 800–2,000 IU/day (maintain 25-OH D above 50 nmol/L, 20 ng/mL), stop smoking, limit alcohol, fall prevention (vision, footwear, medicines).
2. **Pharmacological treatment** for patients with prior hip or vertebral fracture, T-score ≤ −2.5, or high FRAX risk (e.g., major osteoporotic fracture risk ≥ 20%, hip fracture risk ≥ 3% in the US; country-specific thresholds elsewhere):
   - **Bisphosphonates** (alendronate 70 mg weekly, risedronate 35 mg weekly, ibandronate, zoledronic acid 5 mg IV yearly): first-line. Oral dosing: fasting, with a full glass of plain water, upright for 30 minutes (oesophagitis risk); contraindicated in oesophageal disease, eGFR < 30–35, hypocalcaemia. Correct vitamin D deficiency first. Rare adverse events: osteonecrosis of the jaw (dental review before starting; risk about 1 in 10,000–100,000 at osteoporosis doses) and atypical femoral fracture (prodromal thigh pain; risk rises with duration over 5 years). A "drug holiday" after 5 years oral or 3 years IV in lower-risk patients.
   - **Denosumab** 60 mg subcutaneously every 6 months (potent; **must not be stopped abruptly**, since rebound vertebral fractures occur: transition to a bisphosphonate); suitable for renal impairment.
   - **Anabolic agents** (teriparatide, abaloparatide, romosozumab) for very high risk (multiple vertebral fractures, very low T-score), followed by antiresorptive therapy. Romosozumab has a cardiovascular risk warning.
   - Raloxifene, hormone replacement therapy, and calcitonin have limited, specific roles.
3. **Glucocorticoid-induced osteoporosis**: assess fracture risk when starting a prolonged course (≥ 3 months, ≥ 7.5 mg prednisolone daily); offer a bisphosphonate to those at moderate-high risk (older than 70, prior fracture, high doses).
4. **Men**: same drugs; look for hypogonadism.
5. Review at 3–5 years; monitor adherence (poor), bone turnover markers when available, and repeat DEXA every 2–3 years.

### 8.4 Other metabolic bone disease

- **Osteomalacia (and rickets in children)**: defective mineralisation from vitamin D deficiency, malabsorption, CKD, hypophosphataemia, or drugs. Diffuse bone pain, proximal weakness, waddling gait, fractures; low 25-OH vitamin D, high alkaline phosphatase and PTH, low or normal calcium and phosphate; Looser zones. Replace vitamin D (loading regimens, e.g., 50,000 IU weekly for 6–8 weeks, then maintenance) plus calcium.
- **Paget disease of bone**: focal disordered remodelling in older adults; bone pain, deformity, deafness, fractures, high alkaline phosphatase with normal calcium; treat with zoledronic acid for symptomatic or risky sites.
- **Primary hyperparathyroidism**: hypercalcaemia ("stones, bones, groans, moans"), high or inappropriately normal PTH; surgery for symptomatic disease, osteoporosis, kidney stones or calcium > 0.25 mmol/L above normal.
- **Renal osteodystrophy / CKD-MBD**: see chapter 49.

## 9. Regional pain syndromes and soft-tissue conditions

### 9.1 Shoulder

Subacromial (rotator cuff) pain: painful arc 60–120°, positive Hawkins–Kennedy and Neer tests. Rotator cuff tear: weakness of external rotation or abduction, positive drop-arm test, trauma in younger patients or degeneration in older. **Frozen shoulder (adhesive capsulitis)**: painful, then stiff, then resolving over 1–3 years; loss of passive external rotation is the key sign; associated with diabetes and thyroid disease. Acromioclavicular pain at the top of the shoulder with cross-body adduction. Cervical radiculopathy and cardiac or diaphragmatic pain may refer to the shoulder. Treatment: analgesia, physiotherapy, subacromial or intra-articular steroid injections for short-term relief, and surgery for failure or full-thickness tears in younger patients.

### 9.2 Elbow, wrist and hand

Lateral epicondylitis (tennis elbow: pain on resisted wrist extension), medial epicondylitis, olecranon bursitis (infected until proven otherwise if hot and febrile), **De Quervain tenosynovitis** (radial wrist pain, positive Finkelstein test), trigger finger, Dupuytren contracture, ganglion, and carpal tunnel syndrome (chapter 51). Most settle with relief of overuse, splinting, physiotherapy and, if required, a steroid injection.

### 9.3 Hip, knee, and foot

- **Hip pain**: groin pain with reduced internal rotation indicates hip joint disease (OA, avascular necrosis, femoroacetabular impingement, fracture, septic arthritis); lateral hip pain with tenderness over the greater trochanter is **greater trochanteric pain syndrome** (gluteal tendinopathy); referred pain from the lumbar spine is posterior. Suspect avascular necrosis with steroid use, alcohol, sickle cell disease, and trauma.
- **Knee**: locking and giving way (meniscal tear, ACL), anterior pain (patellofemoral), pes anserine bursitis, Baker cyst (posterior knee swelling; rupture mimics DVT: ultrasound), Osgood–Schlatter disease in adolescents.
- **Foot**: plantar fasciitis (heel pain on first steps), Achilles tendinopathy, Morton neuroma, hallux valgus, gout, stress fracture. Rupture of the Achilles tendon: sudden "kick" or "snap", positive Thompson (calf squeeze) test; associated with fluoroquinolones and steroids.

### 9.4 Low back and neck pain

Covered in chapter 29. For the rheumatologist, think inflammatory back pain (section 6.1), osteoporotic vertebral fracture, infection (spinal epidural abscess, discitis), and malignancy (age > 50, previous cancer, unexplained weight loss, pain unrelieved by rest).

### 9.5 Fibromyalgia

Chronic widespread pain (> 3 months) with fatigue, unrefreshing sleep, cognitive symptoms ("fibro-fog"), and often irritable bowel, headaches, and mood disorders; no objective abnormalities on examination apart from diffuse tenderness; normal blood tests and imaging. A **positive clinical diagnosis**: use the 2016 ACR criteria (widespread pain index and symptom severity score). Do not order autoantibodies as a screen. Management: validate the symptoms, explain the central sensitisation mechanism, graded aerobic exercise (best evidence), sleep hygiene, cognitive behavioural therapy, and, selectively, amitriptyline (10–50 mg at night), duloxetine or pregabalin; avoid opioids and repeated investigations. Coexistent inflammatory disease can be missed: do not stop looking when new objective signs appear.

### 9.6 Hypermobility

Joint hypermobility (Beighton score ≥ 5/9 in adults) is common and often asymptomatic. Hypermobile Ehlers–Danlos syndrome is a clinical diagnosis; vascular EDS (thin translucent skin, easy bruising, arterial or organ rupture) and Marfan syndrome (tall, arachnodactyly, lens subluxation, aortic root dilatation) need exclusion by specialist evaluation; they carry life-threatening risks.

## 10. Safe prescribing in rheumatology

### 10.1 Glucocorticoids

Use the lowest dose for the shortest time; document the indication. Adverse effects: hyperglycaemia, hypertension, weight gain, osteoporosis, myopathy, avascular necrosis, infection, cataracts, glaucoma, mood disorder, adrenal suppression. Never stop abruptly after more than 3 weeks of therapy at ≥ 7.5 mg prednisolone equivalent; teach "sick day" rules (increase dose during acute illness, steroid card). Give bone protection and gastric protection if combined with NSAIDs. Consider PJP prophylaxis with prolonged higher doses combined with other immunosuppressants.

### 10.2 DMARD monitoring (typical)

| Drug | Main risks | Monitoring |
|---|---|---|
| Methotrexate | Myelosuppression, hepatotoxicity, pneumonitis, teratogenic | FBC, creatinine, ALT every 2 weeks until stable, then monthly for 3 months and every 3 months |
| Leflunomide | Hepatotoxicity, hypertension, neuropathy, teratogenic | FBC, ALT, BP monthly for 6 months then every 8–12 weeks |
| Sulfasalazine | Rash, cytopenias, hepatitis | FBC, ALT monthly for 3 months, then every 3 months |
| Azathioprine | Myelosuppression, hepatitis, infection, skin cancer; check TPMT/NUDT15 | FBC, LFT; avoid allopurinol (profound neutropenia) |
| Hydroxychloroquine | Retinopathy (dose and duration dependent) | Baseline and annual eye examination after 5 years |
| Mycophenolate | Myelosuppression, GI upset, teratogenic | FBC, creatinine |
| Biologics / JAK inhibitors | Serious infection, TB and hepatitis B reactivation, malignancy, VTE and MACE (JAK) | Screen before; infection vigilance; lipid and cardiovascular risk review |

### 10.3 Infection and immunisation

All patients on immunosuppression: counselled to report fever promptly, avoid live vaccines, receive inactivated vaccines (ideally before starting), and be assessed for opportunistic infections (see chapter 62). Hold the drug during serious infection. **Pregnancy and conception**: review drug compatibility before conception.

## Worked cases

### Case 1: The hot knee in a diabetic

**Presentation.** Mr D., 66, has diabetes (HbA1c 74 mmol/mol), and a right knee that became swollen, red and painful over 24 hours. Temperature 37.9 °C. He cannot bear weight and flexion is limited to 30°. Previously he had gout in the big toe.

**Reasoning.** Gout is likely, but septic arthritis cannot be excluded on clinical grounds in a diabetic with a hot swollen joint. The cost of missing sepsis is a destroyed joint. Gout and infection can coexist.

**Action.** Blood cultures, CRP, FBC, urate, glucose. Aspirate the knee: turbid fluid, WBC 62,000/µL (90% neutrophils), **negatively birefringent crystals** present, Gram stain negative. Start empirical IV flucloxacillin while the culture is pending, plus treatment for gout (prednisolone 30 mg for 5 days, not NSAID because of CKD stage 3). Culture is negative at 48 hours; antibiotics are stopped and the gout managed. Allopurinol started at 100 mg after the flare (with colchicine prophylaxis), titrated to urate < 0.36 mmol/L. His thiazide is replaced.

**Lessons.** Crystals do not exclude infection: send the fluid for culture and treat until the result. A diabetic hot joint is septic until proven otherwise.

### Case 2: Persistent "arthralgia" in a 45-year-old smoker

**Presentation.** Rachel, 45, has had bilateral hand pain and stiffness for 10 weeks, lasting two hours in the morning, with difficulty opening jars and fatigue. Tender, swollen MCPs 2–3 and PIPs bilaterally; positive squeeze test. CRP 28 mg/L, RF 80 IU/mL, anti-CCP strongly positive. X-rays normal. Ultrasound: synovitis with power Doppler signal.

**Reasoning.** Early seropositive rheumatoid arthritis with a high risk of erosions. The window of opportunity is open.

**Action.** Screen for hepatitis B/C, HIV, TB (baseline), check FBC, creatinine, ALT. Start methotrexate 15 mg weekly with folic acid 5 mg (different day), plus prednisolone 15 mg with rapid taper over 6–8 weeks; counsel on contraception, alcohol, and the weekly dosing; smoking cessation. At 3 months DAS28 is 3.8: escalate methotrexate to 20 mg (subcutaneous) and add hydroxychloroquine and sulfasalazine. At 6 months in remission. Annual cardiovascular risk assessment and bone health.

**Lessons.** Positive anti-CCP at presentation identifies patients who need rapid, aggressive treatment. Treat to target; reassess at every visit.

### Case 3: Headache, jaw pain and a scalp that hurts when combed

**Presentation.** Mrs F., 76, has a new right temporal headache for 3 weeks, jaw pain on chewing, shoulder stiffness, weight loss, and one episode of dim vision in the right eye lasting 5 minutes. The right temporal artery is thick and tender with a weak pulse. ESR 92 mm/h, CRP 110 mg/L, platelets 520 × 10⁹/L.

**Reasoning.** Giant cell arteritis with visual symptoms (transient monocular visual loss): high risk of permanent blindness in the next days.

**Action.** The same day: IV methylprednisolone 500 mg daily for 3 days (or oral prednisolone 60 mg daily in centres where IV is not accessible), then oral prednisolone 60 mg. Urgent ophthalmology and rheumatology review; temporal artery ultrasound shows a "halo" and biopsy confirms giant-cell arteritis. Start aspirin 75 mg, PPI, calcium/vitamin D, alendronate; tocilizumab added as a steroid-sparing agent. Taper steroids over about a year guided by symptoms and CRP.

**Lessons.** GCA is treated on suspicion; do not wait for the biopsy. Visual symptoms mean urgency measured in hours.

### Case 4: A woman with fatigue, a rash and foamy urine

**Presentation.** Aisha, 24, has 3 months of fatigue, painful swollen hands, mouth ulcers, a facial rash worse in sunlight, and hair thinning. BP 150/95, leg oedema. Urine dipstick: protein +++, blood ++. ANA 1:1280, anti-dsDNA positive, low C3 and C4, Hb 98 g/L with positive direct Coombs, lymphocytes 0.7 × 10⁹/L, creatinine 128 µmol/L, urine PCR 380 mg/mmol.

**Reasoning.** SLE with probable lupus nephritis (nephritic–nephrotic picture, hypertension, rising creatinine, low complement). Haematological involvement (autoimmune haemolytic anaemia, lymphopenia).

**Action.** Urgent rheumatology and nephrology review; kidney biopsy (class IV lupus nephritis); start IV methylprednisolone followed by oral steroids, mycophenolate mofetil (or low-dose cyclophosphamide), add hydroxychloroquine, ACE inhibitor for proteinuria and BP, and consider belimumab or voclosporin. Contraception counselling (mycophenolate is teratogenic; avoid oestrogen-containing contraceptives in active disease or with antiphospholipid antibodies), vaccination, bone protection, sun protection, and monitoring of complement, anti-dsDNA, urine protein, and creatinine.

**Lessons.** In any young woman with unexplained multisystem illness test ANA and urine. Lupus nephritis is the main driver of mortality and needs early biopsy.

### Case 5: The patient with "sciatica" who is 28 and has never had back pain before

**Presentation.** Luka, 28, has had low back and buttock pain for 8 months, waking him at 4 a.m., with two hours of stiffness, improving with a walk and not with rest. Naproxen helped dramatically. He had an episode of a red painful right eye last year. HLA-B27 positive; CRP 18 mg/L. X-ray of the pelvis: normal. MRI of the sacroiliac joints: bone marrow oedema bilaterally.

**Reasoning.** Axial spondyloarthritis (non-radiographic at this stage) with inflammatory back pain, HLA-B27, MRI sacroiliitis, acute anterior uveitis, and a good NSAID response.

**Action.** Education and a structured exercise program; continuous NSAID at the lowest effective dose; assess disease activity (BASDAI 6/10 despite two NSAIDs over 4 weeks); start a TNF inhibitor or IL-17 inhibitor (screen TB/hepatitis); an IL-17 inhibitor is avoided if IBD is present. Ophthalmology for uveitis recurrence plan. Screen for psoriasis and IBD; smoking cessation; bone density at follow-up.

**Lessons.** Average diagnostic delay in axSpA is 5–8 years. Inflammatory back pain in a young person: think SpA. X-rays are normal for years; MRI shows early changes.

### Case 6: "It's just osteoporosis"

**Presentation.** Mrs B., 72, loses 5 cm in height, has a recent low-impact L1 compression fracture and a T-score of −3.1 at the hip. She is on omeprazole and a long course of prednisolone for polymyalgia rheumatica. Calcium 2.78 mmol/L (high), PTH 11 pmol/L (elevated), 25-OH vitamin D 41 nmol/L, creatinine 92 µmol/L, urinary calcium high.

**Reasoning.** Osteoporosis has several contributors (steroids, PPI, age), but the high calcium with high PTH shows **primary hyperparathyroidism**, which is a treatable cause of bone loss and fracture. Treating with a bisphosphonate alone would miss the cause (and could worsen hypocalcaemia if vitamin D were deficient).

**Action.** Localise the adenoma (neck ultrasound and sestamibi scan), refer for parathyroidectomy (indications: osteoporosis, fracture, calcium > 0.25 mmol/L above normal, age under 50, kidney stones or eGFR below 60). Correct vitamin D to a safe level. Bone-protective treatment afterwards if T-score remains low. Review steroid dose.

**Lessons.** Before attributing osteoporosis to age, check calcium, PTH, vitamin D, renal function and TSH. Treat secondary causes.

## Common pitfalls

1. **Failing to aspirate** an acutely swollen joint, or starting antibiotics before obtaining cultures.
2. **Excluding septic arthritis because crystals are seen**, or because the patient is afebrile or the white cell count is not high.
3. **Using serum urate to diagnose or exclude gout** during an acute attack.
4. **Starting allopurinol during an acute flare without prophylaxis**, or stopping it after a flare, or underdosing and not titrating to target.
5. **Ordering ANA, RF or HLA-B27 as broad screening tests** in patients with diffuse pain, producing false positives and anxiety.
6. **Delaying referral** of persistent synovitis; missing the therapeutic window in rheumatoid arthritis.
7. **Prescribing methotrexate daily** instead of weekly, or forgetting that trimethoprim and co-trimoxazole interact.
8. **Waiting for the biopsy before treating suspected giant cell arteritis.**
9. **Missing the diagnosis of axial spondyloarthritis** because the X-ray of the sacroiliac joints is normal or the patient is "too young".
10. **Treating osteoporosis without assessing secondary causes** and vitamin D, or stopping denosumab abruptly.
11. **Chronic steroids without bone protection**, or stopping them abruptly.
12. **Overinvestigating and overtreating fibromyalgia**, or conversely labelling every widespread pain fibromyalgia without a careful examination.
13. **Starting biologics without screening for TB and hepatitis B.**
14. **Using opioids for chronic musculoskeletal pain.**
15. **Missing cervical spine instability** in rheumatoid arthritis before anaesthesia.

## Key points

- Decide whether pain is inflammatory or mechanical, how many joints are involved, and whether there are systemic features.
- A hot swollen joint needs aspiration; septic arthritis is an emergency and crystals do not exclude it.
- Osteoarthritis is a clinical diagnosis; exercise, weight loss and education are the foundation.
- Gout: diagnose with crystals, treat flares quickly, and treat to target with allopurinol titrated to urate below 0.36 mmol/L with prophylaxis.
- Rheumatoid arthritis: refer early, treat to target with methotrexate-based strategies, and screen before biologics.
- Inflammatory back pain in a young person suggests axial spondyloarthritis; MRI detects early disease.
- Lupus and other connective tissue diseases are diagnosed clinically with serology as support; check urine in every case.
- Giant cell arteritis: treat immediately on suspicion to protect vision.
- Osteoporosis: identify risk, exclude secondary causes, treat to reduce fracture, and avoid abrupt denosumab cessation.
- Fibromyalgia is a positive clinical diagnosis; exercise and education are first line.

## Self-assessment

**Questions**

1. A 55-year-old man has an acutely hot, swollen knee. Synovial fluid shows WBC 80,000/µL, needle-shaped negatively birefringent crystals, Gram stain negative. Is septic arthritis excluded? What do you do?
2. Name four features that distinguish inflammatory from mechanical back pain.
3. At what serum urate level do you aim in gout, and why do you give colchicine when starting allopurinol?
4. A 70-year-old woman has new headache and jaw claudication, ESR 85. What do you do first?
5. When should anti-CCP and RF be ordered and when should they not?
6. A patient taking azathioprine develops gout. Why is allopurinol dangerous?
7. List three things to do before starting a TNF inhibitor.
8. What does a T-score of −2.7 at the femoral neck mean, and what else is needed before starting a bisphosphonate?
9. A young woman has widespread pain and fatigue, normal examination and normal blood tests. What is the likely diagnosis, and what is first-line treatment?
10. What is the risk of stopping denosumab abruptly?

**Answers**

1. No. Crystals and infection can coexist. Send fluid for culture, start empirical IV antibiotics if suspicion remains (especially with a very high WBC), and treat gout; stop antibiotics if cultures are negative and the clinical picture is consistent with gout alone.
2. Onset before age 45 and insidious; morning stiffness longer than 30 minutes; improvement with exercise and not with rest; night pain in the second half of the night; alternating buttock pain; dramatic response to NSAIDs. (Any four.)
3. Below 0.36 mmol/L (6 mg/dL), below 0.30 mmol/L (5 mg/dL) in tophaceous gout. Colchicine (or a low-dose NSAID/steroid) prevents flares triggered by urate mobilisation during the first 3–6 months of urate-lowering therapy.
4. Suspect giant cell arteritis: start high-dose prednisolone immediately (IV methylprednisolone if visual symptoms), arrange same-day specialist assessment, temporal artery ultrasound or biopsy, ophthalmology review. Do not wait for the biopsy.
5. Order them when synovitis is present and inflammatory arthritis is suspected. Do not order for non-specific musculoskeletal pain or as a general screen; RF has many false positives.
6. Allopurinol inhibits xanthine oxidase, which metabolises azathioprine (via 6-mercaptopurine); the combination can cause severe, potentially fatal myelosuppression. Use febuxostat with caution, or reduce azathioprine dose substantially under specialist guidance.
7. Screen for latent and active TB (IGRA, chest X-ray), hepatitis B and C, and HIV; update vaccinations (avoid live vaccines); review for infection, malignancy and heart failure; counsel on infection risk. (Any three.)
8. Osteoporosis by WHO criteria (T-score ≤ −2.5). Before starting: calcium, vitamin D, creatinine/eGFR, PTH, TSH and secondary causes, dental review, swallowing and oesophageal history for oral bisphosphonates, and fracture risk assessment (FRAX).
9. Fibromyalgia (positive clinical diagnosis). First-line: education, graded aerobic exercise, sleep hygiene, CBT; consider amitriptyline, duloxetine or pregabalin; avoid opioids.
10. Rebound bone loss with multiple spontaneous vertebral fractures within months; transition to a bisphosphonate (usually zoledronic acid) after the last dose.
