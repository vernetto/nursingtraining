# Chapter 53. Infectious Diseases

## Learning objectives

After studying this chapter you should be able to:

1. Take an infectious-disease-oriented history (exposures, travel, contacts, vaccination, sexual history) and use it to build a differential.
2. Diagnose HIV infection, recognise acute retroviral syndrome and AIDS-defining illnesses, and explain current principles of antiretroviral therapy (ART) and prevention (PrEP, PEP, U=U).
3. Approach sexually transmitted infections (STIs): urethritis, cervicitis, pelvic inflammatory disease, genital ulcers, syphilis, and partner management.
4. Evaluate a febrile returned traveller: prioritise malaria, typhoid, dengue and other time-critical diagnoses, using incubation periods.
5. Recognise and manage malaria, including severe malaria.
6. Distinguish common viral illnesses (influenza, COVID-19, infectious mononucleosis, measles, varicella and zoster, viral hepatitis, hand-foot-and-mouth, viral gastroenteritis) and know when to treat and when to isolate.
7. Recognise infection emergencies: meningococcal disease, necrotising soft tissue infection, toxic shock, severe malaria, rabies exposure, and tetanus.
8. Apply antimicrobial stewardship principles at the bedside.

> **Note.** Regimens and doses are given as examples consistent with major guidelines (WHO, IDSA, BHIVA/EACS, CDC, NICE). Antimicrobial resistance, national protocols and drug availability differ; check local guidance before prescribing.

## 1. The infectious-disease history

Most infectious diagnoses are made or missed in the history. Ask systematically.

**Time and tempo.** When did it start? Abrupt (hours: bacteraemia, influenza, meningococcus, malaria paroxysm) or insidious (weeks: TB, endocarditis, brucellosis, HIV, abscess)?

**Exposures (the "who, where, what, with whom")**
- *Travel*: countries, rural vs urban, dates (incubation periods), malaria prophylaxis taken (and adherence), pre-travel vaccines, freshwater swimming (schistosomiasis, leptospirosis), insect and tick bites, animal contact, unpasteurised dairy, street food, hospital care abroad, sexual contacts abroad.
- *Contacts*: sick household members, children in nursery or school, healthcare workers, prisons and shelters (TB), recent outbreaks.
- *Animals and occupation*: farm animals and abattoirs (Q fever, brucellosis, leptospirosis), birds (psittacosis), cats (bartonellosis, toxoplasmosis), rodents (hantavirus, leptospirosis), farmers and gardeners (tetanus, sporotrichosis), healthcare workers (needlestick).
- *Food and water*: undercooked meat, shellfish, eggs, raw milk, well water, group meals with others ill.
- *Sexual history* (non-judgmental, routine): partners (number, gender), condom use, sites of exposure (genital, rectal, oral), last test, chemsex.
- *Injecting drug use, tattoos, transfusions, dental or surgical procedures.*
- *Immune status and medications*: steroids, biologics (anti-TNF), chemotherapy, transplant, splenectomy, diabetes, alcohol, HIV, recent antibiotics.
- *Vaccination record.* Childhood vaccines, pneumococcal, influenza, hepatitis B, meningococcal, COVID-19.

**Examination clues** that narrow infection: pattern of fever, rash (see chapter 34), lymphadenopathy (localised or generalised), hepatosplenomegaly, eschar, oral thrush or hairy leukoplakia, genital ulcers or discharge, new murmur, spinal tenderness, joint swelling, meningism, conjunctival injection, and signs of immunosuppression.

## 2. HIV infection

### 2.1 Epidemiology and transmission

About 39 million people live with HIV worldwide; the epidemic is concentrated in sub-Saharan Africa but present everywhere. Transmission occurs through unprotected sex (highest per-act risk with receptive anal sex), blood (needle sharing, transfusion in unscreened settings, needlestick), and mother-to-child (pregnancy, delivery, breastfeeding). It is **not** transmitted by casual contact, kissing, sharing utensils, or mosquito bites.

**Undetectable = Untransmittable (U=U).** People with HIV on ART with a sustained undetectable viral load (< 200 copies/mL) do not sexually transmit HIV. This is among the most important messages for patients.

### 2.2 Who to test

Test (opt-out, with a clear explanation) all adults in high-prevalence settings, all patients with STIs, hepatitis B/C, TB, lymphoma, pregnancy, injecting drug use, and people with **indicator conditions**: unexplained lymphadenopathy, mononucleosis-like illness, persistent unexplained fever or weight loss, thrombocytopenia or leukopenia, herpes zoster in young adults, severe or recurrent seborrhoeic dermatitis, oral candidiasis, community-acquired pneumonia, unexplained peripheral neuropathy, cervical or anal dysplasia, cytopenias. Late diagnosis (CD4 < 350 cells/µL or an AIDS-defining illness at presentation) remains common and avoidable.

### 2.3 Diagnosis

- **4th-generation (Ag/Ab) combination test** (HIV-1/2 antibodies plus p24 antigen): detects most infections by 2–4 weeks; **window period ends by about 45 days**. A reactive screen is confirmed with a differentiation assay and HIV-1 RNA.
- **HIV RNA PCR** is used for suspected acute infection (negative antibody but symptoms), infants, and monitoring.
- Point-of-care rapid tests (finger-prick, oral fluid) are useful; reactive results need confirmation. Self-tests are available in many countries.

### 2.4 Clinical stages

**Acute retroviral syndrome (2–4 weeks after exposure, in 50–90%).** Fever, sore throat, maculopapular rash on the trunk, generalised lymphadenopathy, myalgia, headache, oral or genital ulcers, diarrhoea, occasionally aseptic meningitis. Looks like mononucleosis or flu. Viral load is very high and infectiousness is greatest; antibody tests may be negative, so order RNA.

**Asymptomatic phase (years).** Gradual CD4 decline (average 50–100 cells/µL per year without treatment).

**Symptomatic disease and AIDS.** Typical **opportunistic infections** by CD4 count:

| CD4 (cells/µL) | Typical conditions |
|---|---|
| < 500 | Oral candidiasis, herpes zoster, TB (can occur at any CD4), Kaposi sarcoma, seborrhoeic dermatitis, bacterial pneumonia, lymphoma |
| < 200 | *Pneumocystis jirovecii* pneumonia (PJP), oesophageal candidiasis, cryptococcal meningitis (< 100), HIV wasting, HIV-associated dementia, cryptosporidiosis |
| < 100 | Cerebral toxoplasmosis, cryptococcal disease, CNS lymphoma, progressive multifocal leukoencephalopathy (PML) |
| < 50 | Disseminated *Mycobacterium avium* complex (MAC), cytomegalovirus (CMV) retinitis |

AIDS is defined by CD4 < 200 or an AIDS-defining illness.

**Key opportunistic syndromes**

- *PJP*: subacute dry cough, progressive exertional dyspnoea, hypoxaemia that worsens with exertion (desaturation on walking), bilateral interstitial ("ground-glass") infiltrates, raised LDH and β-D-glucan. Diagnosis: induced sputum or BAL with PCR/immunofluorescence. Treatment: high-dose co-trimoxazole (trimethoprim 15–20 mg/kg/day) for 21 days, plus prednisolone if PaO₂ < 9.3 kPa (70 mmHg) or SpO₂ < 92%.
- *Cerebral toxoplasmosis*: headache, fever, focal deficits, seizures; ring-enhancing lesions on MRI/CT; Toxoplasma IgG positive; treat with pyrimethamine–sulfadiazine (or high-dose co-trimoxazole) for 6 weeks and reassess imaging.
- *Cryptococcal meningitis*: headache that can be indolent for weeks, little neck stiffness, raised intracranial pressure; CSF opening pressure often high; cryptococcal antigen in serum and CSF; treat with amphotericin B plus flucytosine, **repeated therapeutic lumbar punctures** if raised pressure. Delay ART 4–6 weeks.
- *TB* in HIV: often extrapulmonary or disseminated, atypical chest X-ray, sputum smear-negative; use Xpert MTB/RIF, urine LAM, and culture. Treat TB and start ART within 2 weeks if CD4 < 50 (except in TB meningitis, where early ART is dangerous).
- *CMV retinitis*: floaters and visual loss, "pizza-pie" retinal haemorrhage. Needs ophthalmology and oral valganciclovir.
- *Kaposi sarcoma* (HHV-8): violaceous skin or mucosal macules and nodules; lung and gut involvement possible.

**Immune reconstitution inflammatory syndrome (IRIS).** Paradoxical worsening of a known infection (e.g., TB, cryptococcus) or unmasking of a subclinical one 2–12 weeks after starting ART. Continue ART, treat the infection, and use steroids selectively.

### 2.5 Initial assessment and monitoring

Baseline: CD4 count, HIV RNA viral load, genotypic resistance test, HLA-B*5701 (if considering abacavir), HBsAg/anti-HBc/anti-HBs, HCV, HAV, syphilis, toxoplasma IgG, CMV IgG, TB screen (symptoms, IGRA or TST), STI screening, cervical and anal cytology as appropriate, renal function, liver enzymes, lipids, glucose, urinalysis, pregnancy test, and G6PD status in some settings. Vaccinate (hepatitis A/B, pneumococcal, influenza, HPV, COVID-19; no live vaccines if CD4 < 200).

### 2.6 Antiretroviral therapy

**Start ART in everyone with HIV regardless of CD4 count, as soon as the patient is ready (ideally within days of diagnosis).** Delay only for cryptococcal or TB meningitis. Rapid start is feasible and improves retention.

Typical first-line regimens combine an **integrase strand transfer inhibitor (INSTI)** with two nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitors (NRTIs). Examples: dolutegravir plus tenofovir/emtricitabine or tenofovir/lamivudine, or bictegravir/tenofovir alafenamide/emtricitabine. Dolutegravir-based regimens are well tolerated, high barrier to resistance, and used in pregnancy. In many settings a fixed-dose single tablet is taken once daily.

Goals: viral load undetectable (< 50 copies/mL) within 6 months (usually 8–24 weeks). Monitor viral load at 4–8 weeks, 3–6 months, then every 6–12 months. Adherence support is as important as the drug choice.

Common issues: INSTI weight gain, insomnia (dolutegravir); polyvalent cations (iron, calcium, antacids) chelate INSTIs; cobicistat and ritonavir boost many drugs (check interactions, including inhaled and injected steroids); tenofovir disoproxil nephrotoxicity and bone loss (tenofovir alafenamide is kinder); abacavir hypersensitivity (HLA-B*5701), and cardiovascular risk. Long-acting injectable cabotegravir/rilpivirine every 2 months is an option for suppressed patients.

### 2.7 Prevention

- **Pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP)**: daily oral tenofovir/emtricitabine (or event-driven in men who have sex with men), or long-acting injectables (cabotegravir every 2 months, lenacapavir every 6 months where available). Check HIV, creatinine, hepatitis B before starting; screen for STIs every 3 months.
- **Post-exposure prophylaxis (PEP)**: start as soon as possible, ideally within 2 hours and **no later than 72 hours**, for 28 days (typically tenofovir/emtricitabine plus dolutegravir or raltegravir). Offer after significant sexual, needlestick or other exposure to a source with unknown or detectable viral load.
- **Prevention of mother-to-child transmission**: ART in pregnancy reduces transmission to < 1%; viral load at 36 weeks guides delivery mode; infant prophylaxis; counsel on infant feeding according to local policy.
- Condoms, harm reduction, treatment as prevention.

### 2.8 Practical points

1. HIV disclosure, confidentiality and partner notification are legal and ethical matters; follow local public health law and obtain consent where required.
2. Opportunistic infection prophylaxis (co-trimoxazole when CD4 < 200; also protects against toxoplasmosis and bacterial infections) can be stopped when CD4 > 200 for 3–6 months on ART.
3. Today, HIV is a chronic condition. Patients on effective ART have near-normal life expectancy but face more cardiovascular disease, cancer and bone disease, so manage risk factors.

## 3. Sexually transmitted infections

### 3.1 The consultation

Take a confidential, non-judgmental sexual history ("the five Ps": partners, practices, prevention of pregnancy, protection from STIs, past STI history). Ask about **sites of exposure**: pharyngeal, rectal, genital. Examine genitals, anus where indicated, inguinal nodes, skin, and mouth. Offer a **full screen**: HIV, syphilis, chlamydia and gonorrhoea NAAT from each exposed site, hepatitis B vaccination status (and C where at risk), and trichomonas and bacterial vaginosis testing in women as appropriate. Treat partners, advise abstaining until treatment is complete (7 days after therapy and when partners are treated), and notify according to local regulations.

### 3.2 Urethritis and cervicitis

**Men.** Dysuria, urethral discharge, sometimes testicular pain. Causes: *Neisseria gonorrhoeae* (purulent discharge, acute onset), *Chlamydia trachomatis* (milder, slower), *Mycoplasma genitalium*, *Trichomonas*, adenovirus and HSV (rarer), and non-infective causes. Diagnose with first-void urine NAAT (and Gram stain of the discharge where available: Gram-negative intracellular diplococci confirm gonorrhoea). Culture gonorrhoea for resistance testing.

**Women.** Often asymptomatic. Vaginal discharge, intermenstrual or post-coital bleeding, dysuria, lower abdominal pain. Causes: chlamydia, gonorrhoea, *M. genitalium*, trichomonas. Diagnose with vulvovaginal swab NAAT.

**Treatment (examples).**
- *Chlamydia (uncomplicated)*: doxycycline 100 mg twice daily for 7 days (first-line; superior for rectal infection); azithromycin 1 g single dose is a second-line alternative, and doxycycline is avoided in pregnancy.
- *Gonorrhoea (uncomplicated)*: ceftriaxone 1 g IM single dose (guidelines range from 500 mg to 1 g; resistance is a growing concern; test of cure by culture or NAAT after at least 2 weeks for pharyngeal infection and treatment failures).
- *M. genitalium*: test for macrolide resistance; doxycycline followed by moxifloxacin or sequential regimens per resistance.
- Empirical treatment of chlamydia and gonorrhoea at the first visit when symptoms and risk are high and tests are pending or unavailable.

### 3.3 Pelvic inflammatory disease (PID)

Lower abdominal pain, abnormal discharge, dyspareunia, bleeding, fever. Minimal criteria: pelvic or lower abdominal tenderness with cervical motion tenderness or uterine or adnexal tenderness. Low threshold for treatment because sequelae (tubal infertility, ectopic pregnancy, chronic pelvic pain) are preventable. Perform a pregnancy test and consider ultrasound if a tubo-ovarian abscess or ectopic pregnancy is possible.

Treatment (outpatient, mild to moderate): ceftriaxone 1 g IM once plus doxycycline 100 mg twice daily plus metronidazole 400 mg twice daily for 14 days; in-patient IV therapy for severe disease, pregnancy, abscess or failure to respond. Treat partners. Remove IUD only if no improvement at 48–72 hours (or according to local guidance).

### 3.4 Genital ulcer disease

| Cause | Ulcer features | Nodes | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Primary syphilis | Single, painless, indurated, clean-based chancre | Painless, non-suppurative, bilateral | Dark-field or PCR; serology may be negative early |
| Genital herpes (HSV-2 > HSV-1) | Multiple painful vesicles, shallow ulcers, recurrent | Tender | PCR of lesion; antivirals shorten episodes |
| Chancroid (*H. ducreyi*) | Painful, ragged, soft ulcer with undermined edge | Tender, suppurative | Rare; tropical |
| Lymphogranuloma venereum (*C. trachomatis* L1–L3) | Transient papule, painless | Inguinal bubo; proctocolitis in MSM | Doxycycline for 21 days |
| Donovanosis | Beefy-red, painless, bleeding | Pseudobubo | Endemic regions |

Always test for HIV and syphilis in anyone with genital ulcers. Non-infectious causes include Behçet disease, fixed drug eruption, trauma, and malignancy.

### 3.5 Syphilis

*Treponema pallidum*, with stages:

- **Primary (3 weeks after exposure, range 10–90 days)**: painless chancre, regional lymphadenopathy. Heals spontaneously in 3–6 weeks.
- **Secondary (4–10 weeks after the chancre)**: generalised, non-itchy maculopapular rash including **palms and soles**, mucous patches, condylomata lata, patchy alopecia, generalised lymphadenopathy, fever, malaise, hepatitis, glomerulonephritis, uveitis.
- **Latent**: positive serology without symptoms (early < 1 year, late ≥ 1 year or unknown).
- **Tertiary**: gummas, cardiovascular (aortitis, aortic regurgitation, aneurysm), neurosyphilis (meningovascular, tabes dorsalis, general paresis).
- **Neurosyphilis, ocular and otic syphilis** can occur at any stage; ask about visual and hearing symptoms and perform neurological examination.

**Diagnosis.** Treponemal tests (EIA, TPPA, chemiluminescence) detect antibodies and remain positive for life; non-treponemal tests (RPR, VDRL) track activity and titres. Most laboratories now use a treponemal test first ("reverse algorithm"), then RPR. A four-fold change (two dilutions) in RPR titre is significant. False-positive RPR occurs in pregnancy, autoimmune disease, malaria, TB and others.

**Treatment.** Benzathine penicillin G 2.4 million units IM once for early syphilis (primary, secondary, early latent); three weekly doses for late latent or unknown duration; neurosyphilis and ocular syphilis need IV benzylpenicillin 18–24 million units/day for 10–14 days. Doxycycline 100 mg twice daily for 14 days (28 days for late disease) is an alternative in penicillin allergy outside pregnancy. Pregnant women with penicillin allergy should be desensitised. **Jarisch–Herxheimer reaction** (fever, myalgia within 24 hours) is common; warn the patient. Follow RPR titres at 3, 6, 12 and 24 months: aim for a fourfold decline by 6–12 months.

### 3.6 Other STIs and related conditions

- **Trichomoniasis**: frothy, malodorous yellow-green discharge, strawberry cervix (rare); metronidazole 400–500 mg twice daily for 7 days (women), single 2 g dose for men is less effective.
- **Bacterial vaginosis**: thin grey discharge, fishy odour, pH > 4.5, clue cells (Amsel criteria). Not strictly an STI. Metronidazole or clindamycin; high recurrence.
- **Vulvovaginal candidiasis**: thick white discharge with itch; fluconazole 150 mg once or topical azole.
- **Human papillomavirus (HPV)**: genital warts (6/11) and cervical, anal, oropharyngeal cancer (16/18). Vaccination (ideally before sexual debut) and cervical screening.
- **Genital herpes**: first episode treated with aciclovir 400 mg three times daily (or valaciclovir 500 mg twice daily) for 7–10 days; suppressive therapy for frequent recurrences or pregnancy near term. Counsel about asymptomatic shedding.
- **Mpox**: vesiculopustular rash in anogenital areas and elsewhere, proctitis, lymphadenopathy, fever; confirm by PCR; supportive care, tecovirimat in selected severe cases; vaccination of contacts and at-risk groups.
- **Hepatitis B**: sexually transmitted; vaccinate all at risk.

## 4. The febrile returned traveller

### 4.1 General approach

Fever in a person who has been in the tropics within the last year (especially the last 3 months) is **malaria until proven otherwise**. Then think about other time-critical diagnoses. Most deaths in returned travellers result from delayed malaria diagnosis, not from exotic diseases.

**Key questions**: destination(s), dates of departure and return, activities, incubation timing, prophylaxis and adherence, vaccinations, bites, food, fresh water, sexual exposure, animal contact, medical care abroad.

**Incubation guide**

| Incubation | Common diagnoses |
|---|---|
| < 10 days | Dengue, chikungunya, Zika, rickettsial infections (spotted fever, scrub typhus), travellers' diarrhoea pathogens, influenza, respiratory viruses, malaria (can be short), typhoid (7–14 d), leptospirosis |
| 10–21 days | Malaria (*P. falciparum* typically within 1 month), typhoid and paratyphoid, leptospirosis, brucellosis, Q fever, viral haemorrhagic fevers (Ebola, Lassa, Marburg, CCHF, up to 21 d), hepatitis A (15–50 d), acute HIV, acute schistosomiasis (Katayama) |
| > 21 days | Malaria (*P. vivax*/*ovale* relapses months later), hepatitis A/E, amoebic liver abscess, TB, HIV, visceral leishmaniasis, schistosomiasis, rabies |

**Initial tests** for every febrile returned traveller: full blood count with differential (thrombocytopenia suggests malaria or dengue; eosinophilia suggests helminths; leukopenia suggests dengue or rickettsia), malaria rapid diagnostic test **plus** thick and thin blood films (repeated at 12–24 hours × 3 if negative and suspicion remains), CRP, creatinine and electrolytes, liver enzymes, glucose, lactate, blood cultures (typhoid), urinalysis, chest X-ray, HIV test, hepatitis serology as indicated, and stool tests for diarrhoea. Dengue NS1 antigen/PCR in the first 5–7 days and IgM later. Save acute serum for paired serology. If viral haemorrhagic fever is possible (travel to affected areas, contact with sick people, bleeding), **isolate the patient immediately, use protective equipment and call infectious-disease and public health authorities** before sending samples.

**Danger signs** requiring admission: impaired consciousness, seizures, respiratory distress, jaundice, hypotension, bleeding, hypoglycaemia, hyperparasitaemia (> 2% in non-immune), acute kidney injury, severe anaemia.

### 4.2 Malaria

**Burden.** About 250 million cases and 600,000 deaths annually, mostly African children under 5. Species: *P. falciparum* (most severe, can kill within days), *P. vivax* and *P. ovale* (relapse from hypnozoites in liver), *P. malariae* (quartan, can recrudesce years later), and *P. knowlesi* (SE Asia, rapidly progressive).

**Clinical features.** Fever (often irregular initially), rigors, headache, myalgia, vomiting, diarrhoea, cough, splenomegaly (later), anaemia, jaundice, thrombocytopenia. **There is no pathognomonic fever pattern.** In non-immune travellers, symptoms are non-specific and mimic influenza or gastroenteritis.

**Severe malaria (usually *P. falciparum*).** Any of: impaired consciousness (GCS < 11) or seizures (cerebral malaria), respiratory distress/acidosis (lactate > 5 mmol/L), pulmonary oedema, shock, jaundice, renal impairment (creatinine > 265 µmol/L), spontaneous bleeding or DIC, severe anaemia (Hb < 70 g/L), hypoglycaemia (< 2.2 mmol/L), parasitaemia > 10% (or > 2% in non-immune travellers as many centres regard).

**Diagnosis.** Thick film (sensitive) and thin film (species and parasitaemia), RDT (detects HRP2 for falciparum and pLDH/aldolase; HRP2 deletions exist). One negative film does not exclude malaria: repeat.

**Treatment.**
- *Severe malaria*: **IV artesunate** 2.4 mg/kg at 0, 12, 24 h, then daily, until able to take oral therapy (minimum 24 hours), followed by a full 3-day course of an artemisinin-based combination therapy (ACT). Manage glucose, fluids carefully (avoid overload: risk of pulmonary oedema), seizures, anaemia, renal failure, secondary sepsis. Quinine is an alternative if artesunate is unavailable. Monitor for post-artesunate delayed haemolysis for 4 weeks.
- *Uncomplicated falciparum*: ACT, e.g., artemether–lumefantrine (take with fatty food, 6 doses over 3 days), dihydroartemisinin–piperaquine, or atovaquone–proguanil.
- *Non-falciparum*: chloroquine where sensitive (not in Indonesia, Papua New Guinea for *vivax*, where resistance is common: use ACT), followed by **primaquine** or **tafenoquine** to eradicate liver hypnozoites in *P. vivax*/*ovale* after G6PD testing.
- *Pregnancy*: artemisinins in the 2nd/3rd trimester; first trimester per WHO now allows ACT if other options are not available; get specialist advice.

**Prevention.** Bite avoidance (nets, DEET repellents, clothing), chemoprophylaxis matched to the destination (atovaquone–proguanil, doxycycline, mefloquine; chloroquine rarely), adherence for the duration plus post-travel period, standby treatment only if exposure is remote from care. R21 and RTS,S vaccines are used in children in endemic Africa.

### 4.3 Enteric (typhoid and paratyphoid) fever

*Salmonella* Typhi/Paratyphi from contaminated food and water. Incubation 7–14 days (range 3–60). Stepwise fever rising over a week ("step-ladder"), headache, malaise, abdominal pain, constipation (more than diarrhoea in adults), **relative bradycardia**, rose spots (faint salmon-pink macules on the trunk), hepatosplenomegaly, leukopenia. Complications in the third week: intestinal perforation and haemorrhage, encephalopathy, myocarditis.

Diagnosis: blood cultures (positive in 40–80%), bone marrow culture, stool and urine cultures later. Treatment depends on regional resistance: azithromycin or ceftriaxone for travellers from South Asia (fluoroquinolone resistance is common, and extensively drug-resistant typhoid is reported in Pakistan; carbapenems for XDR). Dexamethasone for severe typhoid with shock or encephalopathy. Vaccination (Vi polysaccharide or conjugate) and hygiene for prevention.

### 4.4 Dengue

Transmitted by *Aedes* mosquitoes; global spread and urban outbreaks. Incubation 4–10 days. **Febrile phase (2–7 days)**: high fever, severe headache, retro-orbital pain, myalgia/arthralgia ("break-bone"), rash, nausea, positive tourniquet test, leukopenia, thrombocytopenia. **Critical phase (days 3–7, when fever falls)**: plasma leakage, pleural effusion, ascites, haemoconcentration (rising haematocrit), shock, bleeding. **Recovery phase**: reabsorption; beware of fluid overload.

**Warning signs** (WHO): abdominal pain or tenderness, persistent vomiting, clinical fluid accumulation, mucosal bleeding, lethargy or restlessness, liver enlargement > 2 cm, rising haematocrit with rapidly falling platelets. Patients with warning signs, or with comorbidities, pregnancy, infancy, old age, or lack of social support, should be admitted.

Management is supportive: oral fluids, paracetamol (avoid NSAIDs and aspirin), careful intravenous isotonic crystalloid when needed with frequent haematocrit and vital-sign monitoring. Avoid IM injections and steroids. A tetravalent vaccine is available for people previously infected in some countries.

### 4.5 Other tropical and travel-associated infections

- **Chikungunya**: abrupt fever, severe symmetric polyarthralgia (hands, wrists, ankles) lasting weeks to months, rash.
- **Zika**: mild fever, rash, conjunctivitis, arthralgia; congenital Zika syndrome and Guillain–Barré syndrome. Counsel on pregnancy and sexual transmission.
- **Rickettsial infections** (spotted fever, scrub typhus, African tick-bite fever): fever, headache, eschar at the bite site, regional lymphadenopathy, rash; empirical **doxycycline** 100 mg twice daily for 5–7 days without waiting for confirmation, because untreated scrub typhus and Rocky Mountain spotted fever are severe.
- **Leptospirosis**: freshwater/flood/rodent exposure; fever, conjunctival suffusion, myalgia (calf), jaundice, AKI (Weil disease), pulmonary haemorrhage; doxycycline or penicillin/ceftriaxone for severe disease.
- **Amoebic liver abscess**: fever, right upper quadrant pain, raised right hemidiaphragm, solitary abscess on ultrasound; metronidazole followed by luminal agent.
- **Visceral leishmaniasis (kala-azar)**: prolonged fever, massive splenomegaly, pancytopenia, weight loss; liposomal amphotericin B.
- **Schistosomiasis**: freshwater exposure; Katayama fever 4–8 weeks after exposure with eosinophilia and urticaria; chronic haematuria or hepatosplenic disease; praziquantel.
- **African trypanosomiasis, Chagas, filariasis, onchocerciasis, strongyloidiasis**: be aware; eosinophilia in a person from an endemic area should prompt stool/serology and treatment for strongyloides before steroids (hyperinfection risk).
- **Viral haemorrhagic fevers (Ebola, Marburg, Lassa, CCHF, Rift Valley)**: fever with bleeding or multi-organ failure after exposure (health care, funerals, rodents, ticks, livestock); follow strict infection control and national protocols.
- **Tuberculosis** and **brucellosis**: remain important in returning migrants and visitors.
- **Travellers' diarrhoea**: usually self-limiting; rehydrate; azithromycin for severe or dysenteric cases; fluoroquinolone resistance high in Asia.

## 5. Common viral illnesses

### 5.1 Influenza

Winter epidemic respiratory virus. Abrupt fever, myalgia, headache, cough, malaise; may cause pneumonia (viral or secondary bacterial, notably *S. aureus*), myocarditis, encephalopathy, exacerbations of chronic disease. At-risk groups: age < 5 or > 65, pregnancy, chronic disease, immunosuppression, obesity.

Diagnosis: clinical during an epidemic; PCR or rapid antigen (low sensitivity) when it changes management. **Oseltamivir** 75 mg twice daily for 5 days is indicated for hospitalised or high-risk patients and ideally within 48 hours of symptom onset (benefit is modest in healthy people; may still be given to severe illness regardless of timing). Baloxavir is an alternative. Annual vaccination reduces illness and hospitalisation.

### 5.2 COVID-19 and other respiratory viruses

SARS-CoV-2 produces a spectrum from asymptomatic to ARDS, with anosmia/ageusia, fever, cough, dyspnoea, GI symptoms, thromboembolism and "long COVID". Test with PCR or rapid antigen. Management: supportive care; oral antivirals (nirmatrelvir–ritonavir, with major drug interactions, or remdesivir) for high-risk patients early in disease; dexamethasone and respiratory support for hypoxaemic patients; vaccination and boosters according to national schedules. RSV, rhinovirus, adenovirus, parainfluenza and human metapneumovirus cause similar syndromes; RSV is severe in infants and older adults (vaccines now available for those groups and for pregnancy).

### 5.3 Infectious mononucleosis (EBV)

Teenagers and young adults: fever, severe pharyngitis with tonsillar exudate, posterior cervical lymphadenopathy, fatigue, splenomegaly (30–50%; **avoid contact sports for 4 weeks** because of splenic rupture risk), palatal petechiae, rash after amoxicillin or ampicillin. Atypical lymphocytes, raised transaminases. Diagnosis: heterophile antibody (Monospot; less sensitive early) or EBV VCA IgM. Care is supportive; steroids only for airway obstruction or severe thrombocytopenia/haemolysis. Consider CMV, toxoplasma, acute HIV, and streptococcal pharyngitis as alternatives.

### 5.4 Measles

Highly contagious (R₀ 12–18); resurging where vaccination has fallen. Prodrome of fever, cough, coryza and conjunctivitis, **Koplik spots** (white spots on buccal mucosa), then a maculopapular rash starting on the face and spreading downwards, merging. Complications: otitis media, pneumonia (leading cause of death), encephalitis, subacute sclerosing panencephalitis; severe in malnourished and immunocompromised. Isolate (airborne precautions), notify public health, give **vitamin A** to children, supportive care. Post-exposure MMR within 72 hours or immunoglobulin within 6 days for susceptible contacts.

### 5.5 Varicella and herpes zoster

**Chickenpox**: fever and itchy vesicles appearing in crops (papules → vesicles on erythematous base → crusts), all stages present at once. In adults, pregnant women, newborns and the immunocompromised, severe pneumonitis or disseminated disease is possible: treat with aciclovir (800 mg five times daily in adults for 7 days, or IV in severe cases). Isolate until all lesions are crusted. Immunoglobulin for exposed susceptible high-risk contacts.

**Herpes zoster (shingles)**: unilateral dermatomal pain followed by grouped vesicles; start valaciclovir 1 g three times daily or aciclovir 800 mg five times daily for 7 days, ideally within 72 hours, and for any patient with ophthalmic involvement (hutchinson sign: lesions on tip of nose), age > 50, severe pain, or immunosuppression. Complications: postherpetic neuralgia, Ramsay Hunt syndrome (facial palsy plus ear vesicles), ophthalmic zoster (urgent ophthalmology), disseminated disease. Recombinant zoster vaccine from age 50 (or 18 if immunosuppressed) is highly effective.

### 5.6 Viral hepatitis

| | Hepatitis A | Hepatitis B | Hepatitis C | Hepatitis E |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Transmission | Faecal–oral | Blood, sexual, perinatal | Blood (IV drug use), rarely sexual | Faecal–oral (genotypes 1/2), zoonotic (3/4, undercooked pork/game) |
| Chronicity | None | 5% adults, up to 90% infants | 55–85% | Only in immunosuppressed |
| Vaccine | Yes | Yes | No | Available in some countries |
| Treatment | Supportive | Entecavir or tenofovir for selected chronic cases | Direct-acting antivirals (cure > 95% in 8–12 weeks) | Supportive; ribavirin for chronic in immunosuppressed |

Acute hepatitis: fatigue, anorexia, nausea, right upper quadrant pain, dark urine, pale stool, jaundice, transaminases in the thousands. **Acute liver failure** (jaundice with encephalopathy, INR > 1.5) is an emergency. Hepatitis E is dangerous in pregnancy.

Interpretation of hepatitis B serology: HBsAg positive = infected; anti-HBc IgM = acute; anti-HBs alone = vaccinated or immune; anti-HBs plus anti-HBc = natural immunity; HBsAg positive for > 6 months = chronic. Test HBeAg, HBV DNA and ALT to decide on therapy. Screen before immunosuppression or chemotherapy (reactivation risk) and treat or give prophylaxis.

### 5.7 Other common viral illnesses

- **Viral gastroenteritis (norovirus, rotavirus)**: vomiting and watery diarrhoea for 1–3 days; oral rehydration; strict hand hygiene and isolation in institutions; no antibiotics.
- **Hand, foot and mouth disease (coxsackievirus A16, enterovirus 71)**: fever, oral ulcers, vesicles on hands, feet and buttocks, mainly in young children.
- **Parvovirus B19 (erythema infectiosum, "slapped cheek")**: facial erythema, lacy rash; adults develop arthralgia; dangerous in pregnancy (fetal hydrops), sickle cell disease (aplastic crisis) and immunosuppression.
- **Mumps**: parotitis, orchitis, meningitis; vaccination prevents.
- **Rubella**: mild illness; congenital rubella syndrome.
- **Cytomegalovirus**: mononucleosis-like illness; dangerous in transplant, HIV and congenital infection.
- **Viral meningitis (enteroviruses, HSV-2)**: headache, photophobia, fever, lymphocytic CSF with normal glucose; usually self-limited; aciclovir for HSV where indicated. Remember that **HSV encephalitis** (fever, behaviour change, seizures, temporal lobe changes) needs immediate IV aciclovir 10 mg/kg every 8 hours.
- **Rabies**: virtually always fatal once symptomatic. After any animal bite or scratch (especially dogs, bats, monkeys in endemic regions) wash the wound with soap and water for 15 minutes, then give **post-exposure prophylaxis**: rabies vaccine (days 0, 3, 7, 14, or an abbreviated schedule) and rabies immunoglobulin infiltrated around the wound for category III exposures.
- **Tetanus**: *Clostridium tetani* spores in wounds; trismus, risus sardonicus, spasms, autonomic instability. Treat with human tetanus immunoglobulin, metronidazole, wound debridement, benzodiazepines/magnesium, and ICU support. Check immunisation status after every wound.

## 6. Infection emergencies to recognise early

**Meningococcal disease.** Fever, headache, neck stiffness, photophobia, vomiting, **non-blanching petechial or purpuric rash**, rapid progression to septic shock within hours, especially in teenagers and young adults. Do not wait for lumbar puncture or imaging: give **IV ceftriaxone 2 g (or benzylpenicillin in primary care)**, blood cultures and PCR, fluids and organ support, and notify public health for contacts' prophylaxis (ciprofloxacin or rifampicin). Dexamethasone before or with the first antibiotic dose in suspected pneumococcal meningitis (see chapter 41).

**Necrotising soft tissue infection.** Pain out of proportion to skin findings, rapid spread, tense oedema, bullae, crepitus, dusky skin, systemic toxicity. Risk factors: diabetes, injecting drug use, recent surgery, NSAIDs, immunosuppression, SGLT2 inhibitors (Fournier). Emergency surgical exploration, broad-spectrum IV antibiotics (carbapenem or piperacillin–tazobactam plus clindamycin plus vancomycin or linezolid), ICU support. Do not wait for imaging.

**Toxic shock syndrome.** Fever, diffuse erythroderma, hypotension and multi-organ failure caused by staphylococcal or streptococcal toxins; focus may be small (tampon, wound, burn). Remove the source, give IV antibiotics including clindamycin, fluids, and consider IV immunoglobulin.

**Infective endocarditis.** Fever with a new or changing murmur, embolic phenomena (splinter haemorrhages, Janeway lesions, Osler nodes, Roth spots), heart failure, stroke. Three sets of blood cultures before antibiotics; echocardiography; modified Duke criteria; prolonged IV antibiotics and surgical consultation (heart failure, uncontrolled infection, large vegetations, embolism). Think of it in injecting drug users, prosthetic valves, new murmurs with unexplained fever.

**Spinal epidural abscess.** Back pain, fever, neurological deficit; risk factors include IV drug use, diabetes, recent spinal procedures. MRI with contrast urgently; surgical decompression and antibiotics.

**Severe malaria, sepsis and neutropenic fever**: see sections above and chapters 39 and 62.

## 7. Antimicrobial stewardship at the bedside

1. **Culture before antibiotics** whenever it does not delay treatment of a sick patient.
2. **Start broad when severely ill; narrow at 48–72 hours** using cultures and clinical response ("start smart, then focus").
3. **Define the source and the likely pathogens** before choosing a drug.
4. **Dose correctly**, including loading doses and adjusting for kidney function.
5. **Shortest effective duration**: for example, community-acquired pneumonia 5 days; uncomplicated cystitis 3–5 days; cellulitis 5–6 days; intra-abdominal infection after source control 4 days; bacteraemia from a controlled source 7 days for Gram-negative rods.
6. **Switch from IV to oral** as soon as the patient is improving, afebrile, and absorbing.
7. **Do not treat colonisation** (asymptomatic bacteriuria, a swab from a chronic ulcer, positive sputum in a ventilated patient without clinical infection).
8. **Check allergy history critically**: most reported penicillin allergy is not true allergy; formal assessment allows safer, narrower therapy.
9. **Avoid unnecessary antibiotics** for viral respiratory infections, sore throat with low Centor score, and uncomplicated acute bronchitis.
10. **Review daily** and document indication and stop date.

## Worked cases

### Case 1: Fever after a safari

**Presentation.** Marco, 41, returned 10 days ago from Tanzania. He took no malaria prophylaxis. Three days of fever to 39.5 °C, headache, myalgia, vomiting. HR 112, BP 108/66, temperature 39.2 °C, SpO₂ 96%. No rash, mildly icteric, spleen tip palpable.

**Data.** Hb 128 g/L, platelets 62 × 10⁹/L, WBC 5.1, bilirubin 52 µmol/L, creatinine 128 µmol/L (baseline 85), glucose 4.6 mmol/L, lactate 2.4 mmol/L. RDT: *P. falciparum* positive. Thick film: *P. falciparum*, parasitaemia 6% (rings and no schizonts).

**Reasoning.** Fever after travel to an endemic area without prophylaxis is malaria until proven otherwise. Parasitaemia above 2% in a non-immune traveller, jaundice with bilirubin > 50 µmol/L, and creatinine rising mark **severe malaria** by most criteria (parasitaemia > 2%, jaundice plus parasitaemia > 100,000/µL, renal impairment).

**Action.** Admit to a high-dependency area. IV artesunate 2.4 mg/kg at 0, 12 and 24 hours, then daily. Monitor glucose every 4–6 hours, lactate, creatinine, urine output, and parasitaemia daily. Give fluids carefully (euvolaemia; avoid overload). Switch to a full course of oral artemether–lumefantrine once parasitaemia is falling and he can drink (after at least 24 hours of IV therapy and 3 doses). Follow Hb for 4 weeks (post-artesunate delayed haemolysis). Counsel on prophylaxis for future travel.

**Lessons.** Treat promptly and do not wait for a second film in a sick patient. Severe malaria is a medical emergency; IV artesunate, not quinine, is first-line.

### Case 2: A sore throat that is not strep

**Presentation.** Chiara, 19, has had 6 days of fever, severe sore throat, fatigue, and tender neck nodes. She was given amoxicillin 3 days ago and developed a diffuse maculopapular rash. Tonsils enlarged with exudate, bilateral posterior cervical nodes, palpable spleen tip. WBC 12.8 with 55% lymphocytes (atypical forms), ALT 148 U/L. Monospot positive.

**Reasoning.** Infectious mononucleosis (EBV). Amoxicillin-associated rash is common in EBV infection and does **not** denote true penicillin allergy. Splenomegaly raises the risk of rupture.

**Action.** Supportive care; hydration; paracetamol. Stop amoxicillin. Advise no contact sports or heavy lifting for 4 weeks (4–6 weeks if the spleen is enlarged on ultrasound). Return urgently for left upper abdominal or shoulder tip pain, difficulty breathing or swallowing, or inability to drink. Do not label her as penicillin-allergic. Consider HIV and CMV tests if the course is atypical; recheck if jaundice or prolonged illness.

**Lessons.** Rash after amoxicillin in mononucleosis is not allergy. Test before labelling.

### Case 3: Genital sore and a rash on the palms

**Presentation.** Lorenzo, 31, a man who has sex with men, presents with a 2-week widespread, non-itchy, coppery rash on the trunk, palms and soles, along with sore throat and generalised lymphadenopathy. 5 weeks ago he had a painless penile sore that healed. Examination shows mucous patches in the mouth and condylomata lata perianally.

**Data.** RPR 1:128, TPPA positive. HIV Ag/Ab negative. Gonorrhoea/chlamydia NAATs (urine, throat, rectal) negative. No visual or hearing symptoms; neurological examination normal.

**Reasoning.** Secondary syphilis. Ocular, otic and neurological symptoms must be excluded because they change the treatment.

**Action.** Benzathine penicillin G 2.4 million units IM once. Warn about the Jarisch–Herxheimer reaction (paracetamol). Refrain from sex until lesions heal and partners are treated. Partner notification for the past 3 months plus duration of symptoms. Repeat HIV test in 4 weeks (window period) and discuss PrEP. RPR at 3, 6, 12 and 24 months; expect a fourfold fall by 6–12 months. Hepatitis B vaccination, HPV vaccination, and mpox vaccination are offered in accordance with local policy.

**Lessons.** The palms and soles are the rash location to remember. Syphilis co-occurs with HIV; test and discuss prevention.

### Case 4: Weight loss, cough and low-grade fever

**Presentation.** Thomas, 36, reports 2 months of night sweats, weight loss of 8 kg, dry cough, and exertional breathlessness that is worse over the last 10 days. SpO₂ 95% at rest and 87% after walking the corridor. Oral thrush. Chest examination is clear. Chest X-ray: bilateral perihilar ground-glass opacities. LDH 560 U/L.

**Reasoning.** The combination of subacute dyspnoea, exertional desaturation, interstitial infiltrates, raised LDH, oral thrush, and weight loss suggests **HIV infection with *Pneumocystis* pneumonia**. Alternative diagnoses: TB, atypical pneumonia, lymphoma, bacterial pneumonia.

**Action.** After counselling, HIV Ag/Ab test: reactive; viral load 780,000 copies/mL; CD4 48 cells/µL. Induced sputum PCR: *P. jirovecii* positive; sputum Xpert for TB negative. ABG: PaO₂ 8.2 kPa (62 mmHg) on air. Start high-dose co-trimoxazole for 21 days plus prednisolone 40 mg twice daily for 5 days, then taper. Check G6PD, potassium, creatinine. Start ART within 2 weeks of PJP treatment (e.g., dolutegravir plus tenofovir/emtricitabine) after excluding cryptococcal infection (serum CrAg) and TB. Secondary prophylaxis with co-trimoxazole until CD4 > 200 for 3 months. Screen for toxoplasma, hepatitis B/C, syphilis. Discuss disclosure, partner testing, and support.

**Lessons.** Exertional desaturation is a bedside clue to PJP. Test for HIV in unexplained weight loss and interstitial lung disease.

### Case 5: Fever and headache without a source

**Presentation.** Sara, 24, returned 6 days ago from Thailand. She has fever, retro-orbital headache, severe muscle and joint pain and a fine rash. Day 5 of illness; the fever resolved this morning but she now feels worse, with abdominal pain and vomiting. BP 96/72, HR 108, capillary refill 3 s. Platelets 38 × 10⁹/L, haematocrit 49% (baseline 40%), AST 210. Ultrasound: small right pleural effusion and gallbladder wall oedema. NS1 antigen positive.

**Reasoning.** Dengue entering the critical phase (defervescence with warning signs: abdominal pain, persistent vomiting, rising haematocrit, thrombocytopenia, effusion). Admission is needed.

**Action.** Admit. Isotonic crystalloid IV 5–7 mL/kg/h for 1–2 hours, then reassess and reduce to 3–5 mL/kg/h (and to 2–3 mL/kg/h) according to haematocrit, urine output, and perfusion. Paracetamol for fever and pain; avoid NSAIDs and aspirin. Monitor vital signs hourly, haematocrit every 4–6 hours, and platelets daily. Watch for bleeding. Transfuse only for significant bleeding. Over-resuscitation in the recovery phase causes pulmonary oedema, so reduce fluids when the haematocrit falls and the patient improves.

**Lessons.** The fall of the fever is the danger point in dengue, not the relief. Anchor fluid therapy to haematocrit and perfusion.

### Case 6: "Cystitis" with a fever and an unusual history

**Presentation.** A 28-year-old woman presents with 4 days of fever, malaise, and dysuria after a camping holiday. She has a transient rash. Abdominal tenderness and low-grade fever. Urinalysis is negative for nitrite and leukocytes. Genital examination reveals a few shallow painful ulcers on the vulva and tender inguinal nodes; urine retention develops.

**Reasoning.** Dysuria with a negative dipstick, painful ulcers, tender nodes and retention suggests **primary genital herpes** with urethritis and sacral radiculitis (urinary retention), not cystitis.

**Action.** PCR swab from the lesion for HSV (and test for HIV, syphilis, chlamydia and gonorrhoea). Aciclovir 400 mg three times daily or valaciclovir 500 mg twice daily for 7–10 days, saline bathing, topical lidocaine and analgesics, and catheterisation if retention persists. Counsel about recurrences, asymptomatic shedding, and transmission; discuss suppressive therapy and disclosure to partners. Avoid sex while lesions are present.

**Lessons.** Dysuria with a negative dipstick suggests urethritis or vulvar disease rather than cystitis.

## Common pitfalls

1. **Missing malaria**: failing to ask about travel or accepting one negative film in a febrile traveller.
2. **Treating a fever abroad as "just viral"** without considering time-critical diagnoses (malaria, typhoid, meningococcus, rickettsia).
3. **Testing for HIV only in "high-risk" groups**, missing indicator conditions and late presentation.
4. **Misinterpreting a negative antibody test** in acute HIV: use RNA or repeat in the window period.
5. **Starting ART in cryptococcal or TB meningitis too early**, causing fatal IRIS.
6. **Treating a positive gonorrhoea test with outdated regimens** and not testing pharyngeal and rectal sites.
7. **Forgetting syphilis** as the great imitator; rash on the palms and soles, neurological or visual symptoms.
8. **Labelling mononucleosis rash as penicillin allergy.**
9. **Giving aggressive fluids in dengue recovery** or NSAIDs and IM injections in dengue.
10. **Delaying antibiotics for suspected meningococcal disease** for imaging or lumbar puncture.
11. **Missing necrotising fasciitis** by waiting for signs of skin necrosis. Pain out of proportion is the clue.
12. **Not isolating** (measles, chickenpox, TB, viral haemorrhagic fever suspects).
13. **Giving steroids to a patient with unrecognised strongyloidiasis** (eosinophilia, endemic exposure) leading to hyperinfection.
14. **Prolonged antibiotic courses** and treating colonisation.
15. **Not notifying** public health for notifiable diseases and not offering partner management for STIs.

---

## Key points

- Take the exposure history first: travel, contacts, sexual history, animals, food, immune status.
- Fever after tropical travel is malaria until excluded: RDT plus thick and thin films, repeated if negative; severe malaria gets IV artesunate.
- Offer HIV tests widely; start ART for everyone, early; U=U; PrEP and PEP are effective prevention tools.
- Do a full STI screen with site-specific NAATs, treat partners, and always think of syphilis.
- Know incubation periods; dengue's critical phase begins as fever falls.
- Recognise emergencies early: meningococcal disease, necrotising infection, toxic shock, severe malaria, HSV encephalitis, endocarditis, epidural abscess.
- Stewardship: culture first, narrow quickly, shortest effective course, do not treat colonisation.

## Self-assessment

**Questions**

1. A traveller back from Nigeria 12 days ago has fever; the first thick film and RDT are negative. What do you do?
2. Name four situations in which you would test for HIV even without risk-factor history.
3. What is the time limit for starting HIV post-exposure prophylaxis, and how long is it given?
4. A man has a painless firm ulcer on his penis and painless inguinal nodes. Which tests and which treatment?
5. In which HIV-related meningitis should ART be delayed, and why?
6. List four WHO warning signs of severe dengue.
7. Why should a patient with infectious mononucleosis avoid contact sports?
8. A child with a purpuric rash, fever and drowsiness arrives in primary care, 1 hour from hospital. What do you do?
9. What are the elements of rabies post-exposure prophylaxis for a category III exposure?
10. A 78-year-old nursing home resident has a positive urine dipstick and mild confusion. What is your approach?

**Answers**

1. Do not exclude malaria. Repeat thick and thin films (and RDT) every 12–24 hours for a total of three sets; send other tests (blood cultures, dengue, etc.). Consider empirical treatment if she is unwell and malaria remains likely, and discuss with infectious diseases.
2. Any of: sexually transmitted infection, hepatitis B/C, TB, lymphoma, mononucleosis-like illness, unexplained weight loss or lymphadenopathy, zoster in a young adult, persistent oral thrush, unexplained cytopenias, pneumonia, pregnancy, injecting drug use.
3. As soon as possible, ideally within hours and no later than 72 hours after exposure; 28 days of therapy.
4. Suspected primary syphilis. Treponemal and non-treponemal serology (may be negative early, repeat in 2–4 weeks), dark-field microscopy or PCR of lesion, HIV and other STI tests. Benzathine penicillin G 2.4 million units IM once.
5. Cryptococcal meningitis (and TB meningitis). Early ART increases mortality because of IRIS; start 4–6 weeks after antifungal (or around 4–8 weeks after TB meningitis treatment starts).
6. Any four: abdominal pain/tenderness, persistent vomiting, clinical fluid accumulation (ascites, effusion), mucosal bleeding, lethargy or restlessness, liver enlargement > 2 cm, rising haematocrit with rapid fall in platelets.
7. Splenic enlargement raises the risk of traumatic rupture, which can be fatal; avoid contact sports for at least 4 weeks.
8. Give benzylpenicillin (or ceftriaxone) immediately, IM or IV, then transfer urgently to hospital with notification; do not delay for investigation. Provide oxygen and fluids as needed.
9. Thorough wound cleansing with soap and water (15 minutes), rabies vaccine series (days 0, 3, 7, 14 or abbreviated regimen) and rabies immunoglobulin infiltrated around the wound(s) at the start.
10. Probable asymptomatic bacteriuria. Check vital signs and look for other causes of delirium (hydration, constipation, medications, retention, hypoxia, pain, infection elsewhere). Treat as UTI only with localising urinary symptoms, fever without another source or sepsis; send a culture if treating.
