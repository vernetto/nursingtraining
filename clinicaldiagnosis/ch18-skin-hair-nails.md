## Chapter 18. Skin, Hair and Nails

### Learning objectives

After studying this chapter you should be able to:

1. Examine the skin systematically and describe a lesion using the correct primary and secondary morphological terms, distribution and configuration.
2. Use the blanch test, palpation, and simple bedside tests (Nikolsky sign, diascopy, Wood's lamp, dermoscopy) appropriately.
3. Recognise the dermatological emergencies: non-blanching purpura with fever, Stevens–Johnson syndrome and toxic epidermal necrolysis, necrotising fasciitis, erythroderma, DRESS, anaphylaxis, and severe cellulitis.
4. Apply the ABCDE criteria and the "ugly duckling" sign to pigmented lesions, and recognise basal cell carcinoma, squamous cell carcinoma and melanoma.
5. Recognise common patterns: eczema, psoriasis, fungal and bacterial infections, scabies, urticaria, drug eruptions, vasculitis.
6. Examine the hair and nails and interpret common abnormalities.
7. Recognise how skin signs vary with skin pigmentation, and avoid missing erythema and cyanosis in darker skin.

---

### 18.1 The skin as a diagnostic organ

The skin is the largest organ, visible without any instrument, and it reflects internal disease: liver disease (jaundice, spider naevi), kidney disease (itch, pallor), endocrine disease (hyperpigmentation, striae, myxoedema), cardiovascular disease (xanthomata, peripheral ischaemia), infection (rash patterns), malignancy (acanthosis nigricans, dermatomyositis, paraneoplastic itch), allergy and drug reactions, autoimmune disease (SLE, scleroderma). A single glance may establish the diagnosis (shingles, scabies, psoriasis) or an emergency (non-blanching rash in a febrile patient).

Dermatology is learned by **seeing many lesions and describing them in a standard language.** Without precise terminology, a rash is just "a rash," and neither a diagnosis nor a differential can be developed.

---

### 18.2 History

Ask about:

- **Onset and evolution:** When did it start? Where? How did it spread? Does it come and go?
- **Symptoms:** itch (pruritus), pain, burning, numbness, bleeding, weeping.
- **Triggers:** drugs (including topical, over-the-counter, herbal), foods, contact (new cosmetics, jewellery, plants, occupational), sun exposure, heat, stress, infection, travel, insect bites, animals.
- **Previous skin disease, atopy** (asthma, hay fever, eczema), family history (psoriasis, atopy, melanoma).
- **Occupation, hobbies.**
- **Treatments tried** and the response (including steroids, which may modify appearance).
- **Systemic symptoms:** fever, malaise, joint pain, mouth or genital ulcers, weight loss.
- **Contacts with similar symptoms** (scabies, impetigo, ringworm).
- **Sun exposure history and sunburns,** use of tanning beds (for pigmented lesions).
- **Immunosuppression, HIV risk, medication list** (new drug in the last 2–8 weeks for suspected drug eruption).
- **Sexual history** where relevant (genital lesions, syphilis rash).
- **Impact on quality of life:** sleep, work, mood.

### 18.3 Examination technique

- **Good lighting:** daylight or bright, diffuse light; a tangential light accentuates surface features (papules, scale).
- **Expose the whole skin** where relevant (with permission, chaperone, and respect for dignity). It is often useful to examine the entire skin in patients with pigmented lesions or a generalised rash; include scalp, mouth, genitalia, nails, between the toes, and the back.
- **Look first, then touch.** Use gloves for infectious or wet lesions.
- **Palpate:** texture, temperature, tenderness, depth, induration, mobility, blanching.
- **Assess the distribution and configuration** (see below).
- **Compare** with the other side and with normal skin.
- **Measure** lesions (in mm) and photograph (with consent) for follow-up.

#### Simple bedside tests

| Test | Method | Use |
|---|---|---|
| **Blanch test (diascopy)** | Press a clear glass or plastic slide on the lesion | Erythema blanches; **purpura (extravasated blood) does not.** Non-blanching spots in a febrile patient: meningococcaemia until proven otherwise |
| **Nikolsky sign** | Lateral pressure with a finger on apparently normal skin near a lesion causes the epidermis to slide off | Positive in pemphigus vulgaris, staphylococcal scalded skin syndrome, SJS/TEN |
| **Koebner phenomenon** | Lesions appear at sites of trauma | Psoriasis, lichen planus, vitiligo, warts |
| **Scratching** | Scratch the surface of a lesion lightly | Scale: psoriasis (silvery scale reveals pinpoint bleeding: Auspitz sign) |
| **Dermatographism** | Stroking the skin produces a wheal | Urticaria |
| **Darier sign** | Stroking a lesion causes urtication | Mastocytosis |
| **Wood's lamp (UV 365 nm)** | Fluorescence | Tinea capitis (some species), erythrasma (coral red), pseudomonas (green), vitiligo (accentuates depigmentation) |
| **Skin scraping with KOH** | Microscopy of scale | Dermatophytes, Candida |
| **Dermoscopy** | Magnified, illuminated examination | Pigmented lesions, scabies burrows, warts, vascular patterns |
| **Tzanck smear** | Scrape base of a vesicle | Multinucleated giant cells in herpes infection (largely replaced by PCR) |

### 18.4 Describing a lesion: the language of dermatology

Describe in this order: **(1) primary lesion type, (2) size, shape, colour and surface, (3) borders, (4) secondary changes, (5) distribution and configuration, (6) symptoms.**

#### Primary lesions (the initial lesion)

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Macule** | Flat, circumscribed change in colour, <1 cm (freckle, vitiligo, petechia) |
| **Patch** | Flat, circumscribed colour change, >1 cm (café-au-lait spot, vitiligo, melasma) |
| **Papule** | Raised, solid, <1 cm (wart, lichen planus, acne) |
| **Plaque** | Raised, flat-topped, >1 cm (psoriasis) |
| **Nodule** | Raised, solid, >1 cm, deeper (cyst, lipoma, rheumatoid nodule, erythema nodosum) |
| **Tumour** | Large nodule |
| **Vesicle** | Fluid-filled, <1 cm (herpes simplex, varicella, eczema) |
| **Bulla** | Fluid-filled, >1 cm (bullous pemphigoid, pemphigus, burns, impetigo) |
| **Pustule** | Pus-filled vesicle (acne, folliculitis, pustular psoriasis) |
| **Wheal (weal)** | Transient, raised, oedematous, pink (urticaria) |
| **Petechiae** | Non-blanching red/purple spots <3 mm |
| **Purpura** | Non-blanching red/purple lesions 3–10 mm; **palpable purpura** suggests vasculitis |
| **Ecchymosis** | Non-blanching haemorrhage >1 cm (bruise) |
| **Telangiectasia** | Dilated superficial vessels that blanch |
| **Comedone** | Plugged follicle (acne): open (blackhead) or closed (whitehead) |
| **Burrow** | Linear track (scabies) |
| **Cyst** | Encapsulated, fluid- or semi-solid-filled nodule |

#### Secondary changes

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Scale** | Flakes of keratin (psoriasis: silvery; fungal: fine; seborrhoeic: greasy) |
| **Crust** | Dried exudate (serum, blood, pus: honey-coloured in impetigo) |
| **Erosion** | Loss of epidermis, heals without scarring |
| **Ulcer** | Loss of epidermis and dermis, heals with scarring |
| **Fissure** | Linear crack |
| **Excoriation** | Scratch mark |
| **Lichenification** | Thickened skin with accentuated markings from chronic rubbing (chronic eczema) |
| **Atrophy** | Thinning of the skin (steroid use, lichen sclerosus) |
| **Scar** | Fibrous replacement of skin |
| **Hyperpigmentation / hypopigmentation / depigmentation** | Post-inflammatory change, vitiligo |
| **Sclerosis** | Hardening (scleroderma) |

#### Configuration and distribution

| Pattern | Examples |
|---|---|
| **Annular (ring)** | Tinea corporis, granuloma annulare, erythema migrans |
| **Linear** | Contact dermatitis (plants), Koebner phenomenon, scabies burrows, lichen striatus |
| **Grouped (herpetiform)** | Herpes simplex, dermatitis herpetiformis |
| **Dermatomal** | **Herpes zoster** |
| **Target (iris) lesions** | Erythema multiforme |
| **Reticulate (net-like)** | Livedo reticularis, erythema ab igne |
| **Flexural** (elbow and knee creases) | Atopic eczema |
| **Extensor** (elbows, knees) | Psoriasis |
| **Sun-exposed sites** | Photosensitivity, lupus, porphyria, actinic keratosis, drug photosensitivity, skin cancers |
| **Palms and soles** | Secondary syphilis, hand-foot-mouth disease, Rocky Mountain spotted fever, palmoplantar psoriasis, endocarditis (Janeway lesions) |
| **Seborrhoeic areas** (scalp, eyebrows, nasolabial folds, chest) | Seborrhoeic dermatitis |
| **Face (butterfly, malar)** | Lupus, rosacea (spares nasolabial folds in lupus) |
| **Generalised symmetrical** | Viral exanthem, drug eruption, pityriasis rosea |
| **Intertriginous** (skin folds) | Candida, intertrigo, inverse psoriasis |
| **Acral** | Frostbite, chilblains, vasculitis, Raynaud |
| **Trunk predominantly** | Pityriasis rosea, viral exanthem, seborrhoeic dermatitis |

#### Example descriptions

> **Poor:** "An itchy rash on the arm."
> **Good:** "A 4 × 3 cm well-demarcated, erythematous plaque with adherent silvery-white scale on the extensor surface of the right elbow; similar plaques on the left elbow and both knees; pitting of the fingernails."

The second leads straight to **psoriasis**.

### 18.5 The dermatological emergencies

These must be recognised on sight.

| Emergency | Recognition | Immediate action |
|---|---|---|
| **Meningococcal septicaemia** | Fever, **non-blanching petechial or purpuric rash,** drowsiness, hypotension, cold hands and feet, limb pain; may begin as a blanching rash | **IV/IM antibiotics (ceftriaxone) immediately,** fluids, urgent senior/ICU help. Do not wait for lumbar puncture or cultures |
| **Stevens–Johnson syndrome (SJS) / toxic epidermal necrolysis (TEN)** | Painful, dusky, target-like lesions or blisters with mucosal erosions (eyes, mouth, genitalia), skin detachment (**Nikolsky positive**); <10% BSA (SJS), >30% (TEN); 1–3 weeks after a new drug (allopurinol, anticonvulsants, sulfonamides, NSAIDs, nevirapine) | Stop the culprit drug, admit (burns or ICU), fluids, wound care, eye review; early specialist input |
| **Staphylococcal scalded skin syndrome** | Children, fever, tender erythema, flaccid blisters, Nikolsky positive, no mucosal involvement | Anti-staphylococcal antibiotics, fluids |
| **Necrotising fasciitis** | **Pain out of proportion** to skin findings, rapidly spreading erythema, bullae, dusky skin, crepitus, systemic toxicity, risk factors (diabetes, IV drug use, post-surgery) | **Urgent surgical exploration,** broad-spectrum antibiotics |
| **Erythroderma** | >90% of the skin red and scaly; fever, fluid and heat loss, hypothermia; causes: psoriasis, eczema, drug reaction, lymphoma | Admit, fluids, emollients, identify and treat cause |
| **DRESS (drug reaction with eosinophilia and systemic symptoms)** | Rash, fever, facial oedema, lymphadenopathy, eosinophilia, hepatitis, onset 2–8 weeks after a drug | Stop the drug, systemic steroids, specialist care |
| **Anaphylaxis / angioedema** | Urticaria, swelling of lips/tongue/throat, wheeze, stridor, hypotension | **Intramuscular adrenaline,** airway, oxygen, fluids |
| **Toxic shock syndrome** | Fever, diffuse macular erythroderma, hypotension, multi-organ dysfunction, desquamation of palms/soles later | Source control, antibiotics, supportive care |
| **Herpes zoster ophthalmicus** | Vesicles in the V1 distribution, **Hutchinson sign** (nasal tip) | Antivirals, ophthalmology review |
| **Eczema herpeticum** | Punched-out erosions and vesicles in eczematous skin, fever | IV aciclovir |
| **Acute generalised exanthematous pustulosis, generalised pustular psoriasis** | Sudden numerous sterile pustules, fever | Admit, specialist care |
| **Severe cellulitis / erysipelas** | Spreading erythema, fever, lymphangitis, rapid progression | Antibiotics; mark the borders; suspect necrotising infection if rapid |
| **Acute limb ischaemia, vasculitis with skin necrosis** | Pale, cold limb; purpuric necrotic lesions | Urgent vascular/rheumatology assessment |
| **Pemphigus vulgaris** | Flaccid blisters and mucosal erosions, Nikolsky positive | Admit, high-dose steroids |

### 18.6 Pigmented lesions and skin cancer

#### Benign naevi

Most adults have 20–50 naevi. Typically symmetrical, uniform in colour, regular borders, <6 mm.

#### Melanoma: the ABCDE rule

| Letter | Feature |
|---|---|
| **A**symmetry | One half unlike the other |
| **B**order | Irregular, notched, blurred |
| **C**olour | Variegated (brown, black, red, white, blue) |
| **D**iameter | >6 mm (but melanomas can be smaller) |
| **E**volving | Change in size, shape, colour, **or new symptoms (itch, bleeding, crusting)** |

Additional clues: the **"ugly duckling" sign** (a mole that looks different from the patient's other moles), a **new lesion in an adult**, a **non-healing** pigmented lesion, pigmented streak under a nail (**subungual melanoma:** Hutchinson sign, pigment spreading to the nail fold). **Nodular melanoma** may be amelanotic, symmetrical, firm and rapidly growing, so ABCDE alone can miss it (use also **EFG: Elevated, Firm, Growing**).

**Risk factors:** fair skin, red hair, many naevi (>100), atypical naevi, family history of melanoma, previous melanoma, sunburn in childhood, intermittent intense UV exposure, tanning beds, immunosuppression.

**Management:** do not biopsy a suspected melanoma with a shave or partial biopsy; use **complete excision with a narrow margin** (e.g. 2 mm) to allow **Breslow thickness** (the strongest prognostic factor) to be measured accurately; wide excision follows. Urgent referral by the suspected-skin-cancer pathway.

#### Other skin cancers

| Cancer | Appearance | Behaviour |
|---|---|---|
| **Basal cell carcinoma (BCC)** | Pearly, translucent papule or nodule with telangiectasia and rolled edge; may ulcerate ("rodent ulcer"); morphoeic type: scar-like plaque | Most common cancer; sun-exposed sites, especially the face; locally invasive, rarely metastasises |
| **Squamous cell carcinoma (SCC)** | Scaly, crusted, firm, hyperkeratotic nodule or non-healing ulcer on sun-damaged skin, lower lip, ear; arises in actinic keratosis, chronic wounds, or in immunosuppressed (transplant recipients) | Can metastasise (higher risk on the ear, lip, in immunosuppression); rapid growth suggests keratoacanthoma or SCC |
| **Actinic keratosis** | Rough, scaly, erythematous macules/papules on sun-exposed skin | Precancerous; risk of SCC |
| **Bowen disease (SCC in situ)** | Well-defined scaly red plaque | Treat to prevent invasion |
| **Cutaneous lymphoma (mycosis fungoides)** | Persistent patches and plaques, "eczema" that does not respond to treatment | Biopsy |
| **Merkel cell carcinoma, Kaposi sarcoma** | Rapidly growing red/violaceous nodule, older or immunosuppressed (HHV8 in Kaposi) | Specialist |

**Seborrhoeic keratoses** (warty, "stuck-on" greasy brown plaques) and **cherry angiomas** (bright red papules) are common and benign; **dermatofibroma** is a firm brown papule that dimples on pinching.

#### Skin examination for cancer

Examine the whole skin including the scalp, soles, nails and genitals; use a ruler; photograph; teach patients self-examination and sun protection. Dermoscopy improves accuracy when used by trained clinicians.

### 18.7 Common patterns and conditions

| Condition | Typical appearance and clues |
|---|---|
| **Atopic eczema** | Itchy, dry, red, excoriated patches; flexural in older children/adults, face and extensor surfaces in infants; lichenification when chronic; atopic history |
| **Contact dermatitis** | Eczema at the site of contact; shape follows the allergen or irritant; nickel (under watches, jewellery), hand eczema in hairdressers and cleaners |
| **Seborrhoeic dermatitis** | Greasy scale and erythema on the scalp, eyebrows, nasolabial folds, central chest; dandruff; Parkinson disease, HIV |
| **Psoriasis (plaque)** | Well-demarcated red plaques with silvery scale on extensors, scalp, sacrum; nail pitting, onycholysis; Koebner; joint pain (psoriatic arthritis) |
| **Guttate psoriasis** | Many small "drop-like" plaques on the trunk, 2–3 weeks after streptococcal throat infection |
| **Tinea (dermatophyte infection)** | Annular scaly plaques with raised edge and central clearing (corporis); scaling between toes (pedis); groin (cruris, spares scrotum); scalp (capitis) in children with hair loss; confirm with scrapings |
| **Candidiasis** | Beefy red, moist, with satellite pustules in folds; thrush in the mouth |
| **Pityriasis versicolor** | Fine scaly hypo- or hyperpigmented macules on trunk, fluoresce yellow-green; yeast |
| **Impetigo** | Honey-coloured crusts on the face, around the nose, children; bullous type |
| **Cellulitis / erysipelas** | Erythema, warmth, swelling, tenderness; fever; unilateral lower leg most common; look for entry (tinea pedis, ulcer) |
| **Folliculitis, boils, abscess** | Pustules/nodules around follicles; staphylococcal; MRSA in recurrent |
| **Scabies** | Intense itch, worse at night, **burrows** in finger webs, wrists, genitals, nipples, with papules and excoriations; contacts affected; sparing of the face (except infants) |
| **Head lice, bed bugs, insect bites** | Clusters of bites, itchy papules, grouped |
| **Herpes simplex** | Grouped vesicles on an erythematous base at the lips or genital area, preceded by tingling; recurrences |
| **Herpes zoster (shingles)** | Painful, unilateral, dermatomal band of vesicles; prodromal pain; post-herpetic neuralgia |
| **Varicella (chickenpox)** | Crops of itchy vesicles ("dew drops on a rose petal") on the trunk in various stages |
| **Viral warts, molluscum contagiosum** | Rough papules (warts); umbilicated pearly papules (molluscum) |
| **Urticaria** | Itchy transient wheals lasting <24 hours each; with angioedema (swelling of deeper tissue); triggers: drugs, food, infection, physical (dermatographism, cold, pressure) |
| **Drug eruption (exanthematous)** | Symmetric morbilliform rash on the trunk, 1–2 weeks after starting a drug (antibiotics, allopurinol, anticonvulsants), may be itchy; fixed drug eruption: recurrent round dusky plaque at the same site |
| **Pityriasis rosea** | Herald patch followed by oval scaly plaques in a "Christmas tree" distribution on the trunk; self-limiting 6–8 weeks |
| **Lichen planus** | Pruritic, violaceous, flat-topped polygonal papules with fine white lines (Wickham striae), wrists, ankles, oral lacy white patches |
| **Acne vulgaris** | Comedones, papules, pustules, nodules on face, chest, back |
| **Rosacea** | Central facial erythema, flushing, telangiectasia, papules and pustules; no comedones |
| **Vitiligo** | Well-demarcated depigmented macules, symmetrical; autoimmune associations |
| **Erythema nodosum** | Tender red nodules on the shins; sarcoid, streptococcal infection, TB, IBD, drugs (oral contraceptive), pregnancy |
| **Erythema multiforme** | Target lesions on the acral skin; herpes simplex, Mycoplasma, drugs |
| **Erythema migrans** | Expanding annular erythema at a tick bite: Lyme disease |
| **Vasculitis (leucocytoclastic)** | **Palpable purpura,** especially on the legs; infection, drugs, IgA vasculitis (Henoch–Schönlein purpura: children, with abdominal pain, arthralgia, nephritis), connective tissue disease |
| **Livedo reticularis** | Net-like mottling; cold, antiphospholipid syndrome, vasculitis |
| **Pressure ulcer** | Over bony prominences; staging by depth |
| **Venous ulcer / arterial ulcer / diabetic neuropathic ulcer** | Venous: shallow, irregular, medial malleolus, with oedema, varicose veins, haemosiderin; arterial: punched-out, painful, toes and pressure points, cold limb with weak pulses; neuropathic: painless, plantar surface, callus, diabetes |
| **Pyoderma gangrenosum** | Rapidly expanding painful ulcer with violaceous undermined edge; IBD, rheumatoid arthritis, haematological disease |
| **Secondary syphilis** | Non-itchy symmetrical rash on trunk, palms and soles, mucosal patches, condylomata lata, generalised lymphadenopathy |
| **Acanthosis nigricans** | Velvety hyperpigmented thickened skin in flexures (neck, axillae); insulin resistance; rarely gastric malignancy (sudden extensive onset) |
| **Xanthomata, xanthelasma** | Yellow lipid deposits; hyperlipidaemia |
| **Dermatomyositis** | Heliotrope rash on the eyelids, Gottron papules over knuckles, shawl sign; proximal weakness; association with malignancy |
| **SLE (acute cutaneous)** | Malar rash sparing nasolabial folds; discoid lesions (scarring); photosensitivity; oral ulcers |
| **Systemic sclerosis** | Tight skin, sclerodactyly, telangiectasia, calcinosis, Raynaud |

### 18.8 Hair and nails

#### Hair

| Pattern | Causes |
|---|---|
| **Diffuse non-scarring hair loss (telogen effluvium)** | 2–3 months after stress: childbirth, major illness, surgery, crash dieting, drugs; iron deficiency, thyroid disease |
| **Patterned (androgenetic) alopecia** | Receding hairline and vertex in men; diffuse thinning of the crown in women; hormonal and genetic; consider PCOS and androgen excess in women with hirsutism |
| **Patchy non-scarring (alopecia areata)** | Smooth, round bald patches, "exclamation mark" hairs; autoimmune; may be associated with thyroid disease, vitiligo |
| **Tinea capitis** | Scaly patches with broken hairs, children, lymph nodes (kerion) |
| **Scarring alopecia** | Lichen planopilaris, discoid lupus, folliculitis decalvans; loss of follicular openings; biopsy and early treatment to prevent permanent loss |
| **Traction alopecia, trichotillomania** | Hair-styling pull; irregular broken hairs of different lengths |
| **Chemotherapy and drugs** | Anagen effluvium, rapid diffuse loss |
| **Hirsutism** | Excess terminal hair in a male pattern in women (PCOS, congenital adrenal hyperplasia, androgen-secreting tumour: rapid onset, virilisation) |

Perform the **pull test** (gentle traction on ~60 hairs; >6 hairs extracted suggests active shedding).

#### Nails

(See also Chapter 10.) Important changes: **pitting** (psoriasis, alopecia areata), **onycholysis**, **koilonychia** (iron deficiency), **clubbing**, **Beau lines** (acute illness), **Terry nails**, **leuconychia**, **splinter haemorrhages**, **thickened yellow nails** (fungal infection, psoriasis, yellow nail syndrome), **paronychia** (swelling of the nail fold: Candida, bacteria), **longitudinal pigmented streak** (benign in dark-skinned patients, but a new, wide, irregular streak, or one with Hutchinson sign suggests subungual melanoma; biopsy), **brittle, ridged nails** (ageing, thyroid disease, lichen planus), **nail fold telangiectasia** (dermatomyositis, scleroderma, lupus).

### 18.9 Skin of colour

- **Erythema** appears violaceous, dusky, brown, or dark brown rather than bright red; look for warmth, swelling and texture change, and compare with the surrounding skin.
- **Cyanosis and pallor** are harder to see: examine the lips, tongue, nail beds, conjunctivae, palms and soles.
- **Petechiae** and **jaundice:** check mucous membranes and sclerae in daylight.
- **Post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation or hypopigmentation** is common after any inflammatory condition (eczema, acne) and may cause more distress than the original condition.
- **Keloid scarring** is more common.
- **Melanoma** in darker skin more commonly occurs on the palms, soles, nails and mucosal surfaces (acral lentiginous melanoma), and is often diagnosed late; examine these sites.
- **Pseudofolliculitis barbae** and **traction alopecia/central centrifugal cicatricial alopecia** are common.
- Medical education images have historically under-represented dark skin; seek out representative images and examples.

### 18.10 Worked cases

#### Case 1: Fever and a rash in a young adult

*A 19-year-old student has had 12 hours of fever, severe headache, vomiting and limb pain. He is drowsy; temperature 39.5 °C, pulse 134, BP 88/50, capillary refill 5 seconds. There are scattered purpuric spots over the trunk and legs which do not blanch on pressing with a glass.*

**Reasoning.** Fever, rapid deterioration and a **non-blanching purpuric rash** with shock: **meningococcal septicaemia** (with or without meningitis) until proven otherwise.

**Action.** This is a time-critical emergency. **Give ceftriaxone IV (or IM if no access) immediately,** high-flow oxygen, IV fluid resuscitation, call for senior and ICU help, then blood cultures (do not delay antibiotics), blood tests, lumbar puncture only if safe, and notify public health; arrange prophylaxis for close contacts.

#### Case 2: Six weeks of "eczema" that is not eczema

*A 24-year-old woman has had an itchy rash in the finger webs, wrists and around the waist for 5 weeks, worse at night. Her boyfriend and her flatmate are also itchy. Examination: excoriated papules and short, thin, grey, linear burrows between the fingers and on the wrists.*

**Reasoning.** Nocturnal itch, burrows in the finger webs and wrists, household contacts: **scabies.** Misdiagnosis as eczema is common; steroid creams may mask or worsen the infection.

**Management.** Treat with permethrin 5% (or oral ivermectin) applied to the whole body, repeated after a week; **treat all household and close contacts simultaneously;** wash bedding and clothes at 60 °C; warn that itch may persist for 2–4 weeks after successful treatment.

#### Case 3: A changing mole

*A 47-year-old man with fair skin and a history of blistering sunburns notices that a mole on his back has darkened and enlarged over six months and sometimes itches. It measures 9 mm, has an irregular border and shades of brown and black, with a blue-grey area.*

**Reasoning.** Asymmetry, border irregularity, colour variegation, diameter >6 mm and evolution: **suspected melanoma.** The change over time is the most important feature.

**Action.** Urgent referral under the suspected-skin-cancer pathway; **excision biopsy with a narrow margin** (not shave or incisional biopsy); examine regional nodes and the whole skin; histology determines Breslow thickness, ulceration and mitotic rate, which guide wide excision and sentinel node biopsy.

#### Case 4: Painful blisters in a dermatomal band

*A 71-year-old woman has had a burning pain on the left side of her chest for 3 days. Today, a band of grouped vesicles on an erythematous base appeared from the spine around to the sternum on the left, not crossing the midline.*

**Reasoning.** **Herpes zoster** (shingles) in the T6 dermatome. Antiviral treatment within 72 hours of the rash onset reduces the duration and risk of complications; it is particularly valuable in patients over 50, in severe pain, or with ophthalmic or immunocompromised involvement.

**Management.** Oral valaciclovir/famciclovir/aciclovir, analgesia (neuropathic agents if needed), wound care, advice on infectiousness to non-immune contacts (pregnant women, newborns, immunosuppressed), and follow-up for post-herpetic neuralgia. Vaccination (recombinant zoster vaccine) in eligible adults.

#### Case 5: Drug rash

*A 52-year-old man started allopurinol for gout 4 weeks ago. He now has a widespread red rash, fever, facial swelling, enlarged neck nodes and abnormal liver function; eosinophils are 2.4 × 10⁹/L. There are no mucosal erosions or blisters.*

**Reasoning.** A rash, fever, facial oedema, lymphadenopathy, eosinophilia and hepatitis 2–8 weeks after starting an offending drug: **DRESS syndrome.** HLA-B*58:01 is a strong genetic risk factor for allopurinol hypersensitivity, particularly in some Asian populations.

**Action.** Stop allopurinol immediately, admit, monitor organ function, topical or systemic corticosteroids, and avoid re-challenge; list the drug as an allergy.

#### Case 6: Painful purpura in a child

*A 6-year-old boy had a sore throat 2 weeks ago. He now has a rash of raised, red-purple spots on his buttocks and legs that do not blanch, with knee swelling and colicky abdominal pain. He is afebrile and well-appearing. Urine dipstick: blood ++, protein +.*

**Reasoning.** Palpable purpura on dependent areas, arthritis, abdominal pain and haematuria in a well child: **IgA vasculitis (Henoch–Schönlein purpura).** Complications include renal involvement, intussusception, and testicular torsion-like pain.

**Action.** Check blood pressure, urinalysis (and monitor for several months), full blood count, renal function; exclude other causes of non-blanching rash (meningococcal disease if febrile or unwell; thrombocytopenia); supportive treatment; refer if there is significant proteinuria, renal impairment, or severe abdominal symptoms.

### 18.11 Common pitfalls

- **Calling everything "eczema"** and treating with steroids (scabies, tinea, psoriasis, cutaneous lymphoma, contact dermatitis).
- **Prescribing topical steroids on suspected fungal infection** ("tinea incognito").
- **Missing the non-blanching rash** in a febrile patient, or relying on the rash to be present before treating suspected meningococcal disease.
- **Not asking about new drugs** in the preceding 8 weeks.
- **Examining only the lesion that the patient points out;** missing a melanoma on the back.
- **Taking a partial biopsy of a pigmented lesion.**
- **Dismissing a long-standing but changing lesion.**
- **Not examining mucosal surfaces, nails, scalp, genitals.**
- **Forgetting skin tone variation** when looking for erythema, cyanosis, jaundice and petechiae.
- **Omitting the sexual history** in a patient with a rash that may be syphilis.
- **Missing necrotising infection** because the skin looks only mildly inflamed.

---

### 18.12 Key points

- Describe skin lesions precisely: primary lesion, secondary changes, colour, shape, distribution and configuration.
- Use the blanch test: a non-blanching rash with fever is an emergency.
- Recognise SJS/TEN, necrotising fasciitis, erythroderma, DRESS and anaphylaxis early; stop the culprit drug.
- Apply ABCDE and the ugly-duckling sign to pigmented lesions; excise suspected melanoma with a narrow margin.
- Common conditions have recognisable patterns: eczema, psoriasis, tinea, scabies, impetigo, zoster, urticaria, drug eruptions.
- The skin reflects systemic disease; examine hair and nails.
- Adjust technique for skin of colour; examine mucosae and acral sites.

### 18.13 Self-assessment

1. Distinguish a macule from a papule, and a vesicle from a bulla.
   *Answer: A macule is a flat colour change; a papule is raised and solid (<1 cm). A vesicle is a fluid-filled lesion <1 cm; a bulla is >1 cm.*

2. What does a positive Nikolsky sign indicate, and give two conditions in which it occurs.
   *Answer: Epidermal detachment with lateral pressure on apparently normal skin; pemphigus vulgaris, staphylococcal scalded skin syndrome, SJS/TEN.*

3. List the ABCDE features of melanoma.
   *Answer: Asymmetry, Border irregularity, Colour variegation, Diameter >6 mm, Evolving.*

4. A patient has nocturnal itch, burrows in the finger webs and itchy household contacts. What is the diagnosis and what is the key management principle?
   *Answer: Scabies; treat the patient and all close contacts simultaneously, repeat treatment after a week, wash bedding.*

5. What are the first actions for a febrile patient with a non-blanching purpuric rash and hypotension?
   *Answer: Immediate parenteral antibiotics (ceftriaxone), oxygen, IV fluids, senior/ICU help; do not delay for lumbar puncture or cultures.*

6. Name three clues that suggest necrotising fasciitis rather than cellulitis.
   *Answer (any three): pain out of proportion, rapid spread, systemic toxicity, bullae, dusky skin, crepitus, anaesthesia over the area, failure to respond to antibiotics.*
