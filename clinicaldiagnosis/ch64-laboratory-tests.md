# Chapter 64. Laboratory Tests

## Learning objectives

After studying this chapter you should be able to:

1. Explain what a reference interval is, why 5% of healthy people fall outside it, and how pre-test probability, sensitivity, specificity and likelihood ratios determine the usefulness of a result.
2. Identify pre-analytical, analytical and biological sources of error and know when to repeat a test.
3. Interpret the full blood count (anaemia, polycythaemia, leukocytosis, leukopenia, thrombocytopenia, thrombocytosis) and the blood film.
4. Interpret coagulation tests (PT/INR, aPTT, fibrinogen, D-dimer) and recognise the main bleeding and thrombotic patterns.
5. Interpret urea and electrolytes, calcium, magnesium, phosphate, glucose and HbA1c, acid–base status and blood gases.
6. Interpret liver tests by pattern (hepatocellular, cholestatic, synthetic failure) and renal function tests (creatinine, eGFR, urinalysis, albumin/creatinine ratio).
7. Interpret cardiac biomarkers (high-sensitivity troponin, natriuretic peptides), inflammatory markers (CRP, ESR, procalcitonin, ferritin), thyroid, iron, B12/folate and lipid tests.
8. Interpret urine, CSF and pleural/peritoneal fluid results using standard criteria.
9. Recognise critical values and interferences (haemolysis, biotin, heterophile antibodies, pseudohyponatraemia, drugs).
10. Use laboratory data to support a diagnosis rather than to replace clinical reasoning, avoiding "treating the number".

> **Note on reference ranges.** Ranges quoted are typical adult values in SI units. They vary by laboratory, assay, age, sex, ethnicity, pregnancy and altitude. Always use the range printed on the report. Appendix C lists age-specific and pregnancy-specific values.

## 1. Principles of laboratory interpretation

### 1.1 Reference intervals

A reference interval usually contains the central 95% of results in a healthy reference population. By construction, **1 in 20 healthy people is "abnormal" on any single test**, and with a panel of 20 independent tests the chance that at least one is flagged is about 64%. Therefore:

- A slightly abnormal isolated result in a well patient is often **normal variation**; repeat before investigating further.
- A normal result does not exclude disease, and an abnormal result does not prove it.
- Results should be compared with the **patient's own baseline** (biological variation within an individual is narrower than between individuals). A creatinine rising from 60 to 100 µmol/L is within the "normal" range yet represents a 65% increase and AKI.

### 1.2 Test performance

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Sensitivity | Proportion of diseased people with a positive test ("rule out" when high: SnNout) |
| Specificity | Proportion of non-diseased people with a negative test ("rule in" when high: SpPin) |
| Positive predictive value (PPV) | Probability of disease given a positive test; depends on prevalence |
| Negative predictive value (NPV) | Probability of no disease given a negative test |
| Likelihood ratio positive (LR+) | Sensitivity / (1 − specificity); LR+ > 10 strongly increases probability |
| Likelihood ratio negative (LR−) | (1 − sensitivity) / specificity; LR− < 0.1 strongly decreases probability |

**Bayesian reasoning**: post-test odds = pre-test odds × LR. The same test result means different things in different patients. A D-dimer of 600 ng/mL in a low-risk young woman with pleuritic pain (pre-test probability of PE about 5%) and in a bedbound cancer patient (probability > 40%) leads to different decisions. (Chapters 69–70 develop this fully.)

**Working example.** Troponin for MI: if pre-test probability is 5% (odds 0.053) and the test has LR+ of 15, post-test odds = 0.79 (probability 44%); with pre-test probability 50% (odds 1), post-test odds = 15 (probability 94%).

### 1.3 Sources of error

| Phase | Examples |
|---|---|
| **Pre-analytical** (most common, about 60–70% of errors) | Wrong patient/labelling, sample drawn above an IV drip (dilution, glucose or potassium contamination), prolonged tourniquet (raises K, protein, calcium), haemolysis (raises K, LDH, AST, phosphate), EDTA contamination (high K, low Ca and Mg, low ALP), delayed processing (falls glucose, rises K), underfilled citrate tube (prolonged PT/aPTT), cold agglutinins (spurious MCV, haematocrit), lipaemia, fasting status, posture, time of day (cortisol, iron, TSH), recent exercise (CK), drugs |
| **Analytical** | Interference (biotin with immunoassays: false high or low TSH, troponin, hCG; heterophile antibodies; paraproteins causing pseudohyponatraemia or spurious phosphate; high-dose vitamin C), calibration, hook effect (very high analyte gives falsely low result, e.g., prolactin, hCG, ferritin) |
| **Post-analytical** | Transcription errors, wrong units (mg/dL vs mmol/L), misread flags |

**Rule**: if a result does not fit the clinical picture, **question the result** (repeat on a fresh sample, ask the laboratory about interferences) before questioning the patient.

### 1.4 Units

SI units are used here. Conversions: glucose mg/dL ÷ 18 = mmol/L; creatinine mg/dL × 88.4 = µmol/L; urea (BUN) mg/dL × 0.357 = mmol/L; cholesterol mg/dL ÷ 38.7 = mmol/L; triglycerides mg/dL ÷ 88.5 = mmol/L; calcium mg/dL ÷ 4 = mmol/L; bilirubin mg/dL × 17.1 = µmol/L; haemoglobin g/dL × 10 = g/L; troponin ng/L = pg/mL.

### 1.5 Critical values (phone to clinician immediately)

Typical thresholds (laboratory-specific): K⁺ < 2.8 or > 6.5 mmol/L; Na⁺ < 120 or > 160; Ca²⁺ (corrected) < 1.8 or > 3.5; glucose < 2.5 or > 25; Hb < 70 g/L; platelets < 20 × 10⁹/L; INR > 5; pH < 7.2 or > 7.6; positive blood culture; troponin above the 99th percentile with a rising pattern; positive CSF Gram stain; malaria film positive.

## 2. Full blood count and blood film

### 2.1 Components

Haemoglobin (Hb), haematocrit, red cell count, **MCV** (mean cell volume), MCH, MCHC, **RDW** (red cell distribution width), white cell count with differential (neutrophils, lymphocytes, monocytes, eosinophils, basophils), platelets, reticulocytes (on request).

Typical adult ranges: Hb men 130–175 g/L, women 120–155 g/L; MCV 80–100 fL; WBC 4–11 × 10⁹/L; neutrophils 2.0–7.5; lymphocytes 1.0–4.0; monocytes 0.2–1.0; eosinophils 0.0–0.5; platelets 150–400 × 10⁹/L.

### 2.2 Anaemia: a stepwise approach

1. Confirm: Hb below the reference (WHO: < 130 g/L men, < 120 non-pregnant women, < 110 pregnant). Look at **MCV**, **reticulocytes**, other cell lines, and the film.
2. Is it a **production** problem (low reticulocytes) or **loss/destruction** (high reticulocytes)?

| MCV | Common causes | Key tests |
|---|---|---|
| **Microcytic** (< 80 fL) | Iron deficiency (blood loss: GI, menstrual), thalassaemia trait, anaemia of chronic disease (may be normocytic), sideroblastic anaemia, lead poisoning | Ferritin, transferrin saturation, TIBC, CRP, Hb electrophoresis; in men and postmenopausal women think GI tract loss |
| **Normocytic** (80–100 fL) | Anaemia of chronic disease/inflammation, CKD, acute blood loss, haemolysis, bone marrow failure/infiltration, early iron deficiency, endocrine (hypothyroidism), mixed deficiency | Reticulocytes, ferritin, creatinine/eGFR, LDH, bilirubin, haptoglobin, DAT (Coombs), film |
| **Macrocytic** (> 100 fL) | Vitamin B12 or folate deficiency, alcohol, liver disease, hypothyroidism, drugs (methotrexate, hydroxycarbamide, azathioprine, zidovudine), myelodysplasia, reticulocytosis, haemolysis | B12, folate, TSH, LFT, film (hypersegmented neutrophils, oval macrocytes in megaloblastic) |

**Iron studies**:

| | Iron deficiency | Chronic disease | Thalassaemia trait |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ferritin | Low (< 30 µg/L; < 100 if inflammation or CKD/heart failure) | Normal/high | Normal |
| Transferrin saturation | Low (< 20%) | Low | Normal |
| TIBC | High | Low/normal | Normal |
| RDW | High | Normal | Normal |

Ferritin is an acute-phase protein: a value of 60 µg/L with CRP 80 does not exclude iron deficiency. In heart failure, deficiency is ferritin < 100 µg/L or 100–299 with TSAT < 20%.

**Haemolysis**: raised reticulocytes, LDH, indirect bilirubin, low haptoglobin, spherocytes/schistocytes/bite cells on the film, positive DAT (autoimmune), urinary haemosiderin. Schistocytes plus thrombocytopenia plus renal failure and neurological signs → **TMA (TTP/HUS)**: emergency (plasma exchange; ADAMTS13).

**Red flags**: Hb < 70 g/L or rapid fall; pancytopenia; blasts on film; schistocytes; leucoerythroblastic film (nucleated red cells, immature granulocytes: marrow infiltration).

### 2.3 Polycythaemia (erythrocytosis)

Hb > 165 g/L (men) or > 160 g/L (women) (or haematocrit > 49%/48%). **Relative** (dehydration, diuretics, stress), **secondary** (hypoxia: COPD, sleep apnoea, high altitude, smoking, right-to-left shunt; EPO-producing tumours; testosterone, SGLT2 inhibitors), **primary** (polycythaemia vera: JAK2 V617F in about 95%, low EPO, often high WCC/platelets, pruritus after bathing, splenomegaly, thrombosis). Test: EPO level, JAK2, SpO₂, sleep study, renal imaging as indicated.

### 2.4 White cells

**Neutrophilia** (> 7.5 × 10⁹/L): bacterial infection, inflammation, steroids, stress/exercise, smoking, myeloproliferative disorders, G-CSF; left shift (bands) suggests infection. **Neutropenia** (< 1.5; severe < 0.5): drugs (chemotherapy, carbimazole, clozapine, sulfasalazine, antibiotics), viral infection, autoimmune, marrow failure, benign ethnic neutropenia (Duffy-null, ANC 1.0–1.5, no infection risk), hypersplenism, B12 deficiency. **Lymphocytosis**: viral infection (EBV, CMV), pertussis, CLL (smear cells), lymphoma. **Lymphopenia**: HIV, steroids, sepsis, SLE, chemotherapy. **Eosinophilia** (> 0.5): allergy/asthma, drugs, parasites (Strongyloides, Toxocara, schistosomiasis), eosinophilic granulomatosis with polyangiitis, hypereosinophilic syndrome, Addison disease, lymphoma. **Monocytosis**: TB, endocarditis, CMML, recovery. **Leukaemoid reaction vs leukaemia**: toxic granulation and a high leukocyte alkaline phosphatase point towards reactive; blasts, basophilia and Philadelphia chromosome (BCR-ABL) towards CML.

**Urgent referral**: blasts on film; WBC > 50 × 10⁹/L or < 0.5 neutrophils with fever; unexplained persistent lymphocytosis with cytopenias.

### 2.5 Platelets

**Thrombocytopenia** (< 150): pseudothrombocytopenia (EDTA clumping: repeat in citrate), immune thrombocytopenia (ITP), drugs (heparin: HIT 5–10 days after exposure, with thrombosis; quinine, linezolid, valproate, chemotherapy), sepsis/DIC, TMA (TTP, HUS), liver disease/hypersplenism, marrow failure, HIV, HELLP and gestational, B12/folate deficiency. Bleeding risk: spontaneous at < 20–30; surgical prophylaxis thresholds at < 50. **4T score** for HIT (Thrombocytopenia, Timing, Thrombosis, oTher cause). **Thrombocytosis** (> 400): reactive (iron deficiency, infection, inflammation, malignancy, post-splenectomy, surgery) is far more common than primary (essential thrombocythaemia: JAK2/CALR/MPL); platelets > 1000 can cause acquired von Willebrand disease.

### 2.6 The blood film: what to look for

| Finding | Suggests |
|---|---|
| Hypochromic microcytes, pencil cells | Iron deficiency |
| Target cells | Liver disease, thalassaemia, hyposplenism |
| Spherocytes | Hereditary spherocytosis, autoimmune haemolysis |
| Schistocytes | TMA, DIC, mechanical valve |
| Sickle cells | Sickle cell disease |
| Howell–Jolly bodies | Hyposplenism/asplenia |
| Hypersegmented neutrophils | B12/folate deficiency |
| Rouleaux | Raised ESR, paraprotein, inflammation |
| Blasts | Acute leukaemia |
| Smear cells with mature lymphocytosis | CLL |
| Atypical lymphocytes | EBV, CMV, viral, toxoplasma |
| Parasites | Malaria, babesia (Maurer's clefts, rings) |
| Leucoerythroblastic picture | Marrow infiltration/fibrosis |

## 3. Coagulation

| Test | Measures | Prolonged in |
|---|---|---|
| **PT / INR** | Extrinsic and common pathway (factors VII, X, V, II, fibrinogen) | Warfarin, vitamin K deficiency, liver disease, DIC, factor VII deficiency, DOACs (anti-Xa, thrombin inhibitors, variable) |
| **aPTT** | Intrinsic and common pathway (XII, XI, IX, VIII, X, V, II, fibrinogen) | Heparin (unfractionated), haemophilia A/B, von Willebrand disease (variable), factor XI deficiency, lupus anticoagulant (paradoxical thrombosis), DIC, liver disease |
| **Thrombin time** | Fibrin formation | Heparin, DOAC (dabigatran), hypo/dysfibrinogenaemia, FDPs |
| **Fibrinogen** | | Low in DIC, liver failure, consumption, L-asparaginase; high as an acute-phase reactant (pregnancy, inflammation) |
| **D-dimer** | Fibrin degradation | High in VTE, DIC, sepsis, cancer, pregnancy, surgery, trauma, age, inflammation; useful **only to rule out** VTE in low/intermediate pre-test probability |
| **Platelet count/function** | | See above; PFA-100, aggregometry for function |

**Patterns**:

- **Prolonged PT only**: warfarin, vitamin K deficiency (obstructive jaundice, malabsorption, antibiotics), early liver disease, factor VII deficiency.
- **Prolonged aPTT only**: heparin, haemophilia, VWD, lupus anticoagulant (does not correct fully on mixing study and may be associated with thrombosis/miscarriage), factor XII (asymptomatic).
- **Both prolonged**: DIC, liver failure, common pathway deficiency, anticoagulant excess (DOAC, heparin + warfarin), massive transfusion, vitamin K deficiency severe.
- **Mixing study**: normal plasma 1:1 corrects factor deficiencies but not inhibitors (lupus anticoagulant, factor VIII inhibitor).
- **DIC**: low platelets, prolonged PT/aPTT, low fibrinogen, high D-dimer, schistocytes; treat the cause; support with components if bleeding.
- **Anti-Xa levels** for monitoring LMWH in renal failure/pregnancy/obesity; DOAC-calibrated levels when needed.
- **INR targets**: usually 2.0–3.0 (mechanical mitral valve 2.5–3.5). INR > 5 without bleeding: omit warfarin, consider oral vitamin K 1–2.5 mg; with major bleeding: four-factor PCC + vitamin K 5–10 mg IV.
- **Bleeding history** is a better screen for bleeding disorder than routine PT/aPTT preoperatively; normal PT/aPTT do not exclude VWD, platelet function disorders or mild factor deficiencies.

## 4. Urea, electrolytes and renal function

### 4.1 Creatinine and eGFR

Creatinine 60–110 µmol/L (men), 45–90 (women) depends on muscle mass. Rises late (a ≥ 50% GFR loss may be needed before creatinine exceeds range). **eGFR** (CKD-EPI 2021, race-free) classifies CKD: G1 ≥ 90, G2 60–89, G3a 45–59, G3b 30–44, G4 15–29, G5 < 15 mL/min/1.73 m². Diagnose CKD with eGFR < 60 or albuminuria for ≥ 3 months. eGFR is unreliable in AKI (non-steady state), extremes of body size, amputees, very low muscle mass, high-protein or creatine intake, and drugs that block tubular creatinine secretion (trimethoprim, cimetidine, fenofibrate: creatinine rises without GFR change). **Cystatin C** helps in sarcopenia/older adults.

**AKI (KDIGO)**: creatinine rise ≥ 26.5 µmol/L within 48 h, or ≥ 1.5 × baseline within 7 days, or urine output < 0.5 mL/kg/h for 6 h. Stages 1–3 by magnitude or RRT. See chapter 49.

**Urea** rises with dehydration, GI bleeding (upper), high protein intake, catabolism (steroids, sepsis), renal failure; falls in liver failure, malnutrition, pregnancy, SIADH. **Urea:creatinine ratio** (molar) > 100 suggests pre-renal state or upper GI bleed.

### 4.2 Sodium

Normal 135–145 mmol/L. **Always check serum osmolality, volume status, urine Na and osmolality.**

**Hyponatraemia** (< 135):
1. **Pseudohyponatraemia / isotonic**: severe hyperlipidaemia, hyperproteinaemia (indirect ion-selective electrode; osmolality normal).
2. **Hypertonic**: hyperglycaemia (corrected Na = measured Na + 0.3 × (glucose mmol/L − 5.5) approximately 2.4 mmol/L per 5.6 mmol/L above normal, roughly 1.6 mEq per 100 mg/dL), mannitol.
3. **Hypotonic (true)**: assess volume:
   - **Hypovolaemic**: urine Na > 30 → renal loss (diuretics especially thiazides, adrenal insufficiency, salt-wasting, osmotic diuresis); urine Na < 20 → extrarenal loss (vomiting, diarrhoea, burns).
   - **Euvolaemic**: SIADH (urine osmolality > 100, urine Na > 30, low urea, normal TSH/cortisol; causes: lung disease, CNS disease, malignancy (small cell), drugs (SSRIs, carbamazepine, cyclophosphamide, vincristine), pain, nausea), hypothyroidism, adrenal insufficiency, primary polydipsia (urine osmolality < 100), low solute intake (beer potomania, tea and toast).
   - **Hypervolaemic**: heart failure, cirrhosis, nephrotic syndrome, advanced CKD.
4. **Treatment**: severe symptoms (seizure, coma): 150 mL 3% hypertonic saline over 20 minutes, repeat up to twice, aiming for a rise of 4–6 mmol/L; **limit correction to ≤ 8–10 mmol/L in 24 h** (risk of osmotic demyelination if chronic, malnourished, alcoholic, hypokalaemic, or Na < 105). Chronic asymptomatic: treat the cause; fluid restriction, salt tablets/urea, stop offending drugs; vaptans in selected cases.

**Hypernatraemia** (> 145): water deficit (insensible loss, diabetes insipidus: central or nephrogenic (lithium), osmotic diuresis) or sodium excess (hypertonic saline, bicarbonate). Replace water slowly (enteral or 5% dextrose/hypotonic fluids), correct by ≤ 10 mmol/L per 24 h in chronic cases. Calculate free water deficit: 0.5 × weight (kg) × (Na/140 − 1) litres for men (0.6 for young men, 0.45 for women).

### 4.3 Potassium

Normal 3.5–5.0 mmol/L. Remember: spurious hyperkalaemia from haemolysis, thrombocytosis, leukocytosis, delayed processing, EDTA contamination.

- **Hyperkalaemia**: AKI/CKD, drugs (ACE inhibitors, ARBs, spironolactone, trimethoprim, NSAIDs, heparin, ciclosporin, tacrolimus, beta-blockers, digoxin toxicity), acidosis, tissue breakdown (rhabdomyolysis, tumour lysis, burns, haemolysis), Addison disease, type IV RTA. ECG changes: peaked T waves, flattened P, wide QRS, sine wave. Treat per chapter 65 and 43: calcium gluconate 10% 10–30 mL IV (cardioprotection), insulin 10 units + glucose 25 g, salbutamol nebuliser 10–20 mg, bicarbonate if acidotic, remove source, diuretics, binders (patiromer, sodium zirconium cyclosilicate), dialysis if refractory.
- **Hypokalaemia**: GI losses (vomiting, diarrhoea, laxatives), diuretics, hyperaldosteronism, Cushing, Bartter/Gitelman, renal tubular acidosis, insulin/β-agonists/alkalosis (shift), hypomagnesaemia (correct Mg first), poor intake, licorice. Urine K > 20 mmol/day (or urinary K:creatinine > 1.5 mmol/mmol) with hypokalaemia indicates renal loss. Check BP, acid–base, renin/aldosterone. Replace orally when possible; IV max 10–20 mmol/h in monitored settings (central line for > 10 mmol/h); ECG changes: U waves, ST depression, arrhythmias (torsades).

### 4.4 Calcium, magnesium, phosphate

- **Calcium** 2.20–2.60 mmol/L total; **correct for albumin** (corrected Ca = measured + 0.02 × (40 − albumin g/L)) or measure ionised Ca. **Hypercalcaemia**: primary hyperparathyroidism (outpatient, PTH raised or inappropriately normal) and malignancy (inpatient: PTHrP, bone metastases, myeloma, vitamin D from lymphoma) account for > 90%; others: vitamin D excess, thiazides, lithium, sarcoidosis/granulomatous disease (raised 1,25-OH vitamin D), thyrotoxicosis, immobilisation, milk-alkali, familial hypocalciuric hypercalcaemia (low urine calcium). Check PTH: high/normal with high Ca = PTH-dependent (primary hyperparathyroidism); suppressed = PTH-independent (malignancy, vitamin D, etc.). Treatment of severe hypercalcaemia: IV fluids, bisphosphonate, calcitonin, dialysis (chapter 63). **Hypocalcaemia**: hypoparathyroidism (post-thyroid surgery), vitamin D deficiency/CKD, hypomagnesaemia, acute pancreatitis, rhabdomyolysis, massive transfusion, hyperphosphataemia, drugs (bisphosphonates, denosumab, cinacalcet, foscarnet). Signs: perioral tingling, Chvostek/Trousseau signs, tetany, seizures, prolonged QT. Treat symptomatic or Ca < 1.9: calcium gluconate 10% 10–20 mL IV over 10 minutes, then infusion; correct Mg.
- **Magnesium** 0.7–1.0 mmol/L. Low in alcohol use, diuretics, PPIs, diarrhoea, aminoglycosides, cisplatin, amphotericin; causes refractory hypokalaemia/hypocalcaemia, torsades, tremor, seizures. IV magnesium sulfate 2 g (8 mmol) over 15–20 min for severe or arrhythmia. High in renal failure with Mg-containing laxatives/antacids, eclampsia treatment.
- **Phosphate** 0.8–1.5 mmol/L. Low in refeeding syndrome (start feeding slowly in malnutrition; thiamine), alcohol, DKA recovery, hyperparathyroidism, respiratory alkalosis; severe low causes weakness, rhabdomyolysis, haemolysis, respiratory failure. High in CKD, tumour lysis, rhabdomyolysis, hypoparathyroidism, haemolysed samples.

### 4.5 Glucose and HbA1c

- Fasting plasma glucose: normal < 5.6 (WHO: < 6.1), impaired fasting 5.6/6.1–6.9, diabetes ≥ 7.0 mmol/L (126 mg/dL). 2-hour OGTT ≥ 11.1; random ≥ 11.1 with symptoms; **HbA1c ≥ 48 mmol/mol (6.5%)**. Confirm with a second test unless unequivocal hyperglycaemia with symptoms. HbA1c 42–47 mmol/mol (6.0–6.4%) = prediabetes (WHO/NICE; ADA 39–47). HbA1c is unreliable in haemoglobinopathies, haemolysis, recent transfusion, iron deficiency (falsely high), pregnancy, CKD, recent blood loss; use fructosamine or glucose monitoring.
- **Hypoglycaemia**: < 3.0 mmol/L with symptoms (Whipple's triad). In non-diabetics: insulinoma, drugs (sulfonylureas, insulin, quinine), alcohol, sepsis, adrenal insufficiency, liver failure, non-islet cell tumours, reactive. During a spontaneous episode measure glucose, insulin, C-peptide, proinsulin, beta-hydroxybutyrate, sulfonylurea screen: high insulin/high C-peptide = endogenous hyperinsulinism; high insulin/low C-peptide = exogenous insulin.
- **DKA/HHS**: see chapter 44 (glucose, ketones ≥ 3 mmol/L, pH < 7.3, bicarbonate < 15; HHS osmolality > 320, glucose > 30, no significant ketosis).

### 4.6 Acid–base and blood gases

Normal arterial: pH 7.35–7.45, PaCO₂ 4.7–6.0 kPa (35–45 mmHg), PaO₂ 10–13 kPa (75–100 mmHg) on air, HCO₃⁻ 22–28 mmol/L, base excess −2 to +2, lactate < 2.0 mmol/L.

**Stepwise approach**:
1. **pH**: acidaemia (< 7.35) or alkalaemia (> 7.45).
2. **Primary disorder**: PaCO₂ (respiratory) or HCO₃⁻ (metabolic) moving in the direction that explains the pH.
3. **Compensation**: metabolic acidosis: expected PaCO₂ (mmHg) = 1.5 × HCO₃⁻ + 8 (± 2) (Winter's formula); metabolic alkalosis: PaCO₂ rises 0.7 mmHg per 1 mmol/L HCO₃⁻ rise; acute respiratory acidosis: HCO₃⁻ rises 1 mmol/L per 10 mmHg PaCO₂ rise; chronic: 3.5–4; acute respiratory alkalosis: HCO₃⁻ falls 2 per 10 mmHg PaCO₂ fall; chronic: 4–5. Inadequate or excessive compensation indicates a **mixed disorder**.
4. **Anion gap** = Na⁺ − (Cl⁻ + HCO₃⁻); normal 8–12 (without K); correct for albumin (add 0.25 × (40 − albumin g/L) mmol/L).
   - **High anion gap metabolic acidosis (HAGMA)**, mnemonic **GOLDMARK**: Glycols (ethylene glycol, propylene glycol), Oxoproline (chronic paracetamol), L-lactate, D-lactate, Methanol, Aspirin, Renal failure, Ketoacidosis (DKA, alcoholic, starvation). Calculate **osmolar gap** (measured − calculated osmolality [2Na + glucose + urea + ethanol]; > 10 suggests toxic alcohol) in unexplained HAGMA.
   - **Normal anion gap (hyperchloraemic) acidosis**: diarrhoea, renal tubular acidosis, saline infusion, pancreatic fistula, acetazolamide, ureteral diversion. Urine anion gap (Na + K − Cl) positive suggests RTA, negative suggests GI loss.
5. **Delta ratio** (ΔAG / ΔHCO₃⁻): 1–2 pure HAGMA; < 1 concomitant normal AG acidosis; > 2 concomitant metabolic alkalosis or chronic respiratory acidosis.
6. **Metabolic alkalosis**: vomiting/NG suction, diuretics (chloride-responsive: urine Cl < 20), mineralocorticoid excess, Cushing, Bartter/Gitelman (urine Cl > 20, chloride-resistant), milk-alkali, post-hypercapnia.
7. **Respiratory**: acidosis (hypoventilation: COPD, opioids, neuromuscular, obesity, CNS) with A–a gradient normal; respiratory alkalosis (hyperventilation: anxiety, pain, PE, pneumonia, sepsis, salicylate, pregnancy, liver disease, high altitude).
8. **Lactate**: type A (hypoperfusion: shock, sepsis, ischaemic bowel, seizures), type B (metformin, liver failure, malignancy, thiamine deficiency, adrenaline infusion). Lactate > 2 mmol/L in sepsis is significant; > 4 mmol/L indicates high mortality. Repeat to assess clearance.
9. **A–a gradient** = (FiO₂ × (Patm − 6.3 kPa) − PaCO₂/0.8) − PaO₂; normal about age/4 + 4 mmHg; raised in V/Q mismatch, shunt, diffusion impairment; normal in hypoventilation.

## 5. Liver tests

### 5.1 Components

ALT (more liver-specific), AST (also muscle, heart, haemolysis), ALP (liver, bone, placenta, intestine), GGT (liver, enzyme induction by alcohol/drugs), bilirubin (total, conjugated/direct), albumin, PT/INR (synthetic function), bile acids; platelets (portal hypertension); ammonia (limited use).

Typical ranges: ALT 7–40 U/L (up to 35 in women by modern thresholds), AST 10–40, ALP 40–130, GGT 10–60, bilirubin 3–20 µmol/L, albumin 35–50 g/L.

### 5.2 Patterns

| Pattern | Dominant | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Hepatocellular** | ALT/AST ≫ ALP (R ratio = (ALT/ULN) ÷ (ALP/ULN) > 5) | Viral hepatitis (A, B, C, E, EBV, CMV, HSV), alcohol (AST:ALT > 2, AST rarely > 300), MASLD (formerly NAFLD), autoimmune hepatitis, drugs/toxins (paracetamol, isoniazid, amoxicillin–clavulanate, statins, herbal), ischaemia (ALT > 1000 and falling quickly), Wilson disease, haemochromatosis, α1-antitrypsin deficiency, coeliac, hepatic congestion |
| **Cholestatic** | ALP/GGT ≫ ALT (R < 2) | Extrahepatic obstruction (gallstones, pancreatic or biliary cancer, strictures), PBC (AMA, women), PSC (IBD, MRCP), drugs (co-amoxiclav, flucloxacillin, anabolic steroids, chlorpromazine), sepsis, infiltrative disease (sarcoid, TB, lymphoma, amyloid), pregnancy (intrahepatic cholestasis) |
| **Mixed** | R 2–5 | Drug injury, infiltrative |
| **Isolated raised ALP** | Check GGT or ALP isoenzymes | Bone (Paget, metastases, osteomalacia, healing fracture), pregnancy |
| **Isolated raised bilirubin** | Unconjugated | Gilbert syndrome (benign; rises with fasting/illness; other LFTs normal), haemolysis; **conjugated** → liver/biliary disease, Dubin–Johnson, Rotor |
| **Synthetic failure** | Low albumin, prolonged INR not corrected by vitamin K | Cirrhosis, acute liver failure |

**Degree of ALT elevation**: mild (< 5 × ULN: MASLD, alcohol, drugs, chronic viral hepatitis); moderate (5–15×); marked (> 15×) – acute viral hepatitis, drug/toxin, ischaemia, autoimmune; > 1000 U/L: paracetamol, ischaemic hepatitis, acute viral hepatitis (A, B, E, HSV), autoimmune, choledocholithiasis (transient).

### 5.3 Stepwise approach to abnormal LFTs

1. History: alcohol (AUDIT), medications (including supplements, herbal), viral risk, family history, metabolic syndrome, travel, pregnancy.
2. Repeat in 4–6 weeks if mild and asymptomatic (unless synthetic dysfunction or jaundice).
3. First-line: viral serology (HBsAg, anti-HBc, anti-HCV with PCR if positive), ferritin and transferrin saturation, autoantibodies (ANA, ASMA, AMA), immunoglobulins, coeliac serology, caeruloplasmin (< 40 y), α1-antitrypsin, lipids, glucose/HbA1c, TSH, CK (if AST>ALT without liver disease).
4. Ultrasound (fat, biliary dilatation, stones, focal lesions, portal hypertension); elastography/FIB-4 score for fibrosis ((age × AST)/(platelets × √ALT); < 1.3 low risk, > 2.67 high risk (adjust > 65 years)); hepatology referral for fibrosis or unclear cause.
5. Acute liver failure (INR ≥ 1.5 with encephalopathy): emergency; N-acetylcysteine for suspected paracetamol or non-paracetamol ALF, transplant centre contact.

## 6. Cardiac biomarkers

### 6.1 High-sensitivity troponin (hs-cTn)

Rises 2–3 hours after myocardial injury, peaks 24 hours, remains elevated 7–14 days. Use the assay-specific 99th percentile (e.g., hs-cTnT ≈ 14 ng/L, hs-cTnI ≈ 26 ng/L for the general population; sex-specific thresholds exist). **Troponin elevation means myocardial injury, not necessarily MI.** The diagnosis of MI requires a rise and/or fall with ≥ 1 of: ischaemic symptoms, new ischaemic ECG changes, pathological Q waves, imaging evidence of new loss of viable myocardium/wall motion abnormality, or coronary thrombus.

**Rapid rule-out/rule-in algorithms** (ESC 0/1-hour or 0/2-hour): very low initial value and no significant delta ± at least 3 hours after chest pain onset → rule out; high initial value or significant delta → rule in; observe zone → repeat at 3 hours. Always interpret with clinical probability, ECG, and time from onset.

**Non-ischaemic causes of troponin elevation**: myocarditis/pericarditis, tachyarrhythmia, heart failure, pulmonary embolism, sepsis/critical illness, renal failure (chronic mild elevation; look at the delta), stroke/subarachnoid haemorrhage, aortic dissection, Takotsubo, cardiac contusion, defibrillation, strenuous exercise, cardiotoxic chemotherapy, cardiac amyloidosis, aortic stenosis, hypertensive emergency.

**Chronic stable elevation** (no delta) reflects structural disease and carries prognostic weight but not acute MI.

### 6.2 Natriuretic peptides

BNP < 100 pg/mL and NT-proBNP < 300 pg/mL make acute heart failure unlikely (NPV > 95%); NT-proBNP age-adjusted rule-in thresholds in acute dyspnoea: > 450 (< 50 years), > 900 (50–75), > 1800 (> 75). In non-acute outpatient settings: BNP < 35, NT-proBNP < 125 pg/mL exclude HF. Raised in AF, older age, CKD, PE, sepsis, pulmonary hypertension, cirrhosis; **lower in obesity**; sacubitril–valsartan raises BNP (use NT-proBNP).

### 6.3 Others

CK (rhabdomyolysis: > 5× ULN, usually > 5000–10,000 with AKI risk), CK-MB (not needed with hs-troponin), myoglobin (obsolete), LDH (non-specific; haemolysis, tumour lysis, lymphoma, PCP), D-dimer as above.

## 8. Endocrine and metabolic tests

### 8.1 Thyroid function

- **TSH** 0.4–4.0 mU/L (higher upper limit with age). Screening and first-line; interpret with free T4 (FT4, 10–22 pmol/L) and FT3 when needed.
- Primary hypothyroidism: high TSH, low FT4 (subclinical: high TSH, normal FT4 – treat if TSH > 10, symptomatic, pregnancy, TPO antibodies positive with TSH > 4–5 and in younger patients; repeat TSH in 6–12 weeks first). Primary hyperthyroidism: low TSH, high FT4/FT3 (subclinical: low TSH, normal FT4/FT3); Graves (TRAb), toxic nodule/MNG, thyroiditis (low uptake). **Central hypothyroidism**: low/normal TSH with low FT4. **Non-thyroidal illness (sick euthyroid)**: low T3, normal or low TSH, avoid testing in acute illness. Drugs: amiodarone, lithium, heparin (raises FT4 artefactually), biotin (assay interference), steroids/dopamine (lower TSH), metoclopramide, immune checkpoint inhibitors (thyroiditis).
- Pregnancy: trimester-specific reference ranges.

### 8.2 Adrenal and pituitary

- **Cortisol**: 9 a.m. cortisol > 400 nmol/L excludes adrenal insufficiency, < 100 strongly suggests it; intermediate → short Synacthen test (peak ≥ 420–500 nmol/L normal). **Cushing's screening**: overnight 1 mg dexamethasone suppression (cortisol < 50 nmol/L normal), late-night salivary cortisol, 24-h urinary free cortisol; ACTH to localise.
- **Hyperaldosteronism**: aldosterone:renin ratio when hypertension with hypokalaemia, resistant hypertension, adrenal incidentaloma, family history; correct K and interfering drugs.
- **Phaeochromocytoma**: plasma free metanephrines or 24-hour urinary fractionated metanephrines.
- **Prolactin** (note macroprolactin, drugs, hook effect), IGF-1 for acromegaly, LH/FSH/testosterone/oestradiol for hypogonadism (morning testosterone, repeat low values).

### 8.3 Lipids

Total cholesterol, LDL-C, HDL-C, triglycerides, non-HDL-C, Lp(a) once in a lifetime for risk. Non-fasting samples are acceptable. TG > 10 mmol/L (885 mg/dL) → risk of pancreatitis; TG > 5.6 → exclude secondary causes (diabetes, alcohol, hypothyroidism, nephrotic syndrome, drugs). Secondary causes of raised LDL: hypothyroidism, nephrotic syndrome, cholestasis, drugs (steroids, ciclosporin). Targets per risk (ESC/EAS: very-high-risk LDL-C < 1.4 mmol/L [55 mg/dL] and ≥ 50% reduction; extreme risk < 1.0 mmol/L [40 mg/dL]).

### 8.4 Vitamins and others

- **Vitamin B12**: < 148 pmol/L (200 ng/L) deficient; 148–221 borderline → methylmalonic acid or holotranscobalamin; anti-intrinsic factor antibodies (pernicious anaemia). Folate low in poor diet, alcohol, pregnancy, malabsorption, drugs (methotrexate, phenytoin). Treat B12 deficiency before folate in anaemia with neurological features (risk of subacute combined degeneration).
- **25-OH vitamin D**: < 30 nmol/L deficient; 30–50 insufficient (varies); target > 50 nmol/L; test selectively (bone disease, malabsorption, CKD).
- **Uric acid**: not diagnostic of gout; flares occur at normal levels; treat-to-target < 360 µmol/L (< 300 with tophi).
- **Amylase/lipase**: lipase > 3 × ULN supports acute pancreatitis (with typical pain); amylase less specific.
- **Ammonia**: limited utility in encephalopathy.
- **Coeliac**: anti-tTG IgA with total IgA, on a gluten-containing diet.
- **Tumour markers**: used for monitoring, rarely diagnosis (PSA, CA-125, CEA, CA 19-9, AFP, β-hCG); not for screening asymptomatic people (except PSA and AFP in specific protocols).
- **Autoantibodies**: ANA (screen; titre ≥ 1:160 more significant; common in healthy people, low titres in 20–30%), dsDNA, Sm, ENA (Ro, La, RNP, Scl-70, Jo-1), RF and anti-CCP (RA), ANCA (PR3, MPO), complement C3/C4, AMA (PBC), ASMA (AIH), anti-GBM, tTG, TPO antibodies. Order autoantibodies only with pre-test suspicion (chapter 52).

## 9. Urine tests

### 9.1 Dipstick and microscopy

| Finding | Interpretation |
|---|---|
| Leukocyte esterase + nitrite | UTI likely in symptomatic patient; nitrite negative does not exclude (non-nitrate reducing organisms, *Enterococcus*, *Pseudomonas*, dilute urine). **Asymptomatic bacteriuria** (positive culture without symptoms) should not be treated except in pregnancy, before urological procedures with mucosal breach, and some transplant situations; dipsticks in people over 65 are unreliable (chapter 61) |
| Blood (haematuria) | Visible or non-visible; confirm with microscopy (≥ 3 RBC/hpf); dysmorphic RBCs, casts and proteinuria → glomerular; isolated → urological (cystoscopy, imaging); false positive: myoglobin, menstruation, vigorous exercise |
| Protein | Dipstick detects albumin ≥ 0.15–0.3 g/L; quantify by **urine albumin:creatinine ratio (ACR)**: normal < 3 mg/mmol; A2 3–30; A3 > 30; >70 mg/mmol suggests nephrotic range when combined with clinical picture; protein:creatinine ratio (PCR) > 50 mg/mmol (≈ 0.5 g/day), nephrotic > 300 mg/mmol (≈ 3 g/day) |
| Glucose | Hyperglycaemia, SGLT2 inhibitors, renal glycosuria, pregnancy |
| Ketones | DKA, starvation, alcohol, vomiting |
| pH, specific gravity | Concentration ability; urine pH > 5.5 in systemic acidosis suggests distal RTA |
| Casts | RBC casts: glomerulonephritis; WBC casts: pyelonephritis, interstitial nephritis; muddy brown granular casts: ATN; hyaline: benign/prerenal; fatty casts: nephrotic |
| Crystals/eosinophils | Uric acid, oxalate, drug crystals |

### 9.2 Urine electrolytes

**FeNa** = (urine Na × plasma creatinine)/(plasma Na × urine creatinine) × 100: < 1% pre-renal, > 2% ATN; unreliable on diuretics (use **FeUrea** < 35% prerenal), contrast, early obstruction, CKD. Urine osmolality > 500 suggests intact concentrating ability.

### 9.3 Urine drug screens

Immunoassay screens give presumptive results; confirm by GC-MS/LC-MS when consequences are significant. Cross-reactivity (e.g., ibuprofen/naproxen with cannabinoids in some assays, quetiapine with TCAs, fluoroquinolones with opiates, pseudoephedrine with amphetamines). Detection windows vary (cannabis days to weeks).

## 10. Body fluids

### 10.1 Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF)

Normal: clear; opening pressure 10–20 cm H₂O (up to 25 in obesity); WBC 0–5/µL (lymphocytes); protein 0.15–0.45 g/L; glucose ≥ 60% of paired plasma (≥ 2.5 mmol/L); RBC 0.

| Condition | Cells | Protein | Glucose (CSF:plasma ratio) | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Bacterial meningitis** | 100–10,000+, neutrophils | High (1–5 g/L) | Very low (< 0.4) | Gram stain, culture, PCR; lactate > 3.5 mmol/L; opening pressure high |
| **Viral meningitis/encephalitis** | 10–1000, lymphocytes (early neutrophils) | Normal–mildly high | Normal | HSV, VZV, enterovirus PCR |
| **TB meningitis** | 10–500, lymphocytes | High (1–5) | Low | AFB, TB PCR (GeneXpert), ADA; large volume; fibrin web |
| **Cryptococcal** | 0–200 lymphocytes | Mild increase | Low/normal | Very high opening pressure; India ink; CrAg |
| **Fungal, carcinomatous, sarcoid** | Lymphocytes | High | Low | Cytology, flow cytometry |
| **Subarachnoid haemorrhage** | RBC raised; xanthochromia (spectrophotometry ≥ 12 h after onset) | Mild increase | Normal | Compare tube 1 and 4; CT within 6 hours of onset is highly sensitive |
| **Guillain–Barré** | Normal cells | High (albuminocytological dissociation) | Normal | |
| **MS** | Normal/mild lymphocytes | Normal | Normal | Oligoclonal bands (matched serum), raised IgG index |
| **Autoimmune encephalitis** | Mild lymphocytes | Normal | Normal | NMDAR and other antibodies (CSF more sensitive) |

Send: cell count and differential, protein, glucose with paired serum glucose, Gram stain/culture, PCRs per context, cytology, oligoclonal bands, and retain extra tube for later. Contraindications to LP without prior imaging: focal neurological signs, papilloedema, GCS ≤ 12, seizures, immunocompromise, coagulopathy.

### 10.2 Pleural fluid

**Light's criteria** (exudate if ≥ 1): pleural protein/serum protein > 0.5; pleural LDH/serum LDH > 0.6; pleural LDH > 2/3 of the upper limit of serum LDH. In patients on diuretics with borderline results, use serum–pleural protein gradient > 31 g/L (transudate in HF) or albumin gradient > 12 g/L. **Transudates**: heart failure, cirrhosis, nephrotic syndrome, hypoalbuminaemia, peritoneal dialysis, hypothyroidism. **Exudates**: parapneumonic/empyema, malignancy, TB, PE, pancreatitis, rheumatoid, SLE, drugs, post-CABG, chylothorax, asbestos.

Further: pH < 7.2 (with glucose < 3.3, LDH > 1000) in parapneumonic effusion indicates complicated effusion requiring drainage; glucose < 3.3 (rheumatoid, empyema, malignancy, TB); amylase (pancreatitis, oesophageal rupture); triglycerides > 1.2 mmol/L (chylothorax); ADA > 40 U/L supports TB (lymphocytic exudate); cytology (sensitivity ~60% first sample, improves with repeat); NT-proBNP for heart failure; culture and Gram stain.

### 10.3 Ascitic fluid

**SAAG** = serum albumin − ascitic albumin: ≥ 11 g/L (1.1 g/dL) = portal hypertension (cirrhosis, alcoholic hepatitis, heart failure, Budd–Chiari, portal vein thrombosis); < 11 = peritoneal disease (malignancy, TB, pancreatic, nephrotic). Ascitic protein < 25 g/L in portal hypertension increases SBP risk. **Spontaneous bacterial peritonitis (SBP)**: ascitic neutrophils (PMN) ≥ 250/µL → treat (third-generation cephalosporin ± albumin 1.5 g/kg day 1 and 1 g/kg day 3); inoculate culture bottles at the bedside; consider secondary peritonitis if multiple organisms, very high PMN, glucose < 2.8, LDH > upper limit of serum, protein > 10 g/L.

### 10.4 Synovial fluid

| | Normal | Non-inflammatory (OA) | Inflammatory (RA, crystal) | Septic |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| WBC/µL | < 200 | 200–2000 | 2000–50,000 | > 50,000 (often > 100,000) |
| PMN % | < 25 | < 25 | > 50 | > 75–90 |
| Appearance | Clear | Clear/straw | Cloudy | Turbid/purulent |

Gram stain/culture (negative stain does not exclude); crystals under polarised microscopy (MSU: needle-shaped, negatively birefringent, yellow when parallel; CPPD: rhomboid, weakly positive birefringent). Crystals and infection can coexist.

## 11. Choosing, ordering and communicating tests

1. **Ask what decision the result will change.** If none, don't order it.
2. Consider **timing** (troponin, cultures before antibiotics, cortisol at 9 a.m., drug levels at trough, fasting only when needed).
3. **Panels** raise false-positive risk and cost; "routine admission bloods" are low-yield in well patients.
4. Know the **turn-around time** and interpretive comment; order stat only for time-critical decisions.
5. **Repeat tests** only when a change would affect management or to confirm an unexpected result.
6. **Incidental abnormal results** (mildly raised GGT, low-titre ANA, borderline TSH): reassess pre-test probability; avoid cascades of further tests (the "ulysses syndrome").
7. **Document** the result, your interpretation and plan; ensure **results are followed up** (pending at discharge, outpatient).
8. **Communicate** results clearly to patients: what it means, what next, uncertainty, and when to seek help.

## Worked cases

### Case 1: Tired woman with a "normal" Hb

**Presentation.** Ms A., 34, fatigue, hair loss, restless legs, heavy periods. Hb 124 g/L (ref 120–155), MCV 83, ferritin 18 µg/L, TSAT 11%, CRP 3.

**Reasoning.** Haemoglobin is normal, but ferritin < 30 µg/L with low TSAT shows iron deficiency without anaemia, which commonly causes fatigue, restless legs and hair shedding. Menorrhagia is the likely cause. The Hb "normal" range includes patients who have dropped from their baseline (e.g., 145).

**Action.** Oral iron on alternate days (e.g., ferrous sulfate 200 mg every other day) with vitamin C, recheck ferritin and Hb at 8–12 weeks; treat the menorrhagia (gynaecology/tranexamic acid, levonorgestrel IUD); screen for coeliac disease if poor response; consider IV iron if intolerance or malabsorption. In men and postmenopausal women, iron deficiency mandates GI investigation.

**Lessons.** Ferritin finds deficiency before anaemia; treat the cause, not just the number.

### Case 2: Sodium 118 in an elderly woman

**Presentation.** Mrs B., 79, started on a thiazide 2 weeks ago for hypertension, now confused, nauseated, Na 118 mmol/L (previously 138). Euvolaemic clinically. Serum osmolality 248 mOsm/kg, urine osmolality 380, urine Na 55 mmol/L, TSH and cortisol normal.

**Reasoning.** Hypotonic hyponatraemia with inappropriately concentrated urine and urine Na > 30 in euvolaemia; thiazide-induced hyponatraemia (impaired water excretion, often with SIADH-like physiology and a hypovolaemic component). Symptoms moderate (confusion, nausea) but no seizures.

**Action.** Stop the thiazide permanently (record as adverse reaction); avoid hypotonic fluids; if only moderate symptoms: hypertonic saline 150 mL 3% over 20 minutes once may be considered per guideline for moderately severe symptoms, otherwise fluid restriction and careful monitoring; check Na every 2–4 hours; correction target 4–6 mmol/L in the first 24 h, limit ≤ 10 mmol/L (risk factors for osmotic demyelination: thiazide, hypokalaemia, malnutrition). Potassium repletion raises sodium too (count it). Brisk aquaresis after stopping the drug can overcorrect → give desmopressin ± 5% dextrose to re-lower if correction exceeds limits.

**Lessons.** Classify hyponatraemia by osmolality, volume and urine studies; correct slowly.

### Case 3: Raised ALT with an alternative explanation

**Presentation.** Mr C., 45, BMI 33, T2DM, ALT 78, AST 44, GGT 90, ALP normal, bilirubin normal, platelets 210, albumin 42, no alcohol excess (AUDIT 3). Viral serology, autoimmune screen, ferritin 320, TSAT 25%, coeliac negative.

**Reasoning.** Mild hepatocellular pattern in metabolic syndrome: MASLD (metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease) most likely. Key question: fibrosis. FIB-4 = (45 × 44)/(210 × √78) = 1980/(210 × 8.83) = 1.07 (< 1.3 low risk).

**Action.** Ultrasound confirms steatosis. Low FIB-4 → manage in primary care: weight loss 7–10% (diet, exercise), glycaemic control (consider GLP-1 receptor agonist or pioglitazone), statin safe, reduce alcohol, cardiovascular risk management (the main cause of death), repeat FIB-4 every 1–3 years; if FIB-4 intermediate/high → elastography.

**Lessons.** Use non-invasive fibrosis scores; liver enzymes do not reflect fibrosis stage.

### Case 4: Chest pain and a positive troponin that isn't an MI

**Presentation.** Mr D., 72, CKD 3b, hypertensive, presents with 2 days of breathlessness and mild chest discomfort. ECG: left ventricular hypertrophy with strain, no acute changes. hs-cTnT 38 ng/L at 0 h, 40 at 3 h (ULN 14). Echo: LVH, preserved EF. BNP raised, CRP 5.

**Reasoning.** Elevated troponin without significant rise/fall (delta < 20% at 3 hours, < 5 ng/L) and no ischaemic features: **chronic myocardial injury** from LVH and CKD rather than acute MI. Differential: heart failure with preserved EF (BNP), PE (D-dimer not helpful in this setting; use Wells/CTPA if suspicion).

**Action.** Not treated as ACS; manage heart failure, blood pressure, CKD; outpatient cardiology and consider stress imaging/CT coronary angiography for stable symptoms; no heparin/DAPT. Document baseline troponin for future comparison.

**Lessons.** Troponin interpretation depends on delta and context; chronic stable elevation is common in CKD/LVH.

### Case 5: Mixed acid–base disorder

**Presentation.** Mr E., 58, alcohol-dependent, vomiting for 3 days, abdominal pain. ABG: pH 7.42, PaCO₂ 4.4 kPa (33 mmHg), HCO₃⁻ 21, Na 138, K 3.0, Cl 85, lactate 4.5, ketones 5.0 mmol/L, glucose 6.

**Reasoning.** pH is near-normal, so look for mixed disorders. Anion gap = 138 − (85 + 21) = 32 (high; albumin low would raise corrected AG further). Delta gap: ΔAG = 32 − 12 = 20; ΔHCO₃⁻ = 24 − 21 = 3: ratio ≈ 6.7 (> 2) → coexisting **metabolic alkalosis** (vomiting, low chloride). HAGMA from alcoholic ketoacidosis plus lactate; respiratory compensation or alkalosis from alcohol withdrawal/pain. Normal pH from opposing disturbances.

**Action.** Thiamine before glucose, IV 0.9% saline with dextrose and potassium (K 3.0), magnesium and phosphate replacement, monitor for refeeding, treat withdrawal, search for pancreatitis (lipase), treat lactate cause (sepsis?); insulin generally not needed unless glucose high.

**Lessons.** A normal pH does not mean normal acid–base; calculate gaps and deltas.

### Case 6: Pleural effusion in heart failure on diuretics

**Presentation.** Mrs F., 80, large right effusion, on furosemide. Pleural protein 33 g/L (serum 62, ratio 0.53), pleural LDH 180 U/L (serum LDH 250 [ULN 250]; ratio 0.72; 2/3 ULN = 167). Serum–pleural albumin gradient 14 g/L. NT-proBNP high.

**Reasoning.** Light's criteria suggest exudate on two items (protein ratio 0.53 and LDH ratio 0.72 and LDH > 2/3 ULN), but diuresis concentrates pleural fluid and misclassifies up to 25% of cardiac effusions. Albumin gradient > 12 g/L (and pleural NT-proBNP) support a transudate.

**Action.** Treat heart failure; no need for further invasive work-up if the clinical context fits; re-evaluate if the effusion is asymmetric, persistent after diuresis, with fever, pleuritic pain, or weight loss, in which case cytology/thoracoscopy.

**Lessons.** Know the limits of criteria; integrate clinical context.

## Common pitfalls

1. **Treating a spurious result** (haemolysed K, EDTA contamination, pseudohyponatraemia, biotin interference).
2. **Ignoring trends**: a change within the reference range can be significant (creatinine, Hb).
3. **Equating a normal reference range with "disease excluded."**
4. **Ordering large panels** and chasing incidental abnormalities.
5. **Misusing D-dimer** (high-risk patients, inpatients, pregnancy, elderly without age adjustment).
6. **Interpreting ferritin or CRP in isolation** during inflammation.
7. **Correcting hyponatraemia or hypernatraemia too fast.**
8. **Not correcting calcium for albumin.**
9. **Misinterpreting troponin** as "MI" without delta or context.
10. **Using eGFR in AKI** or extremes of body size; overlooking drugs that raise creatinine.
11. **Missing mixed acid–base disorders** by stopping at pH or bicarbonate.
12. **Treating asymptomatic bacteriuria.**
13. **Applying Light's criteria blindly** in diuresed patients.
14. **Failing to take cultures before antibiotics** or to send adequate volume.
15. **Lost results**: no one follows up pending or incidental findings.

## Key points

- A reference interval flags 5% of healthy people; use clinical context, pre-test probability and individual baselines.
- Suspect pre-analytical error when results don't fit; repeat and ask the laboratory.
- Anaemia: classify by MCV and reticulocytes; ferritin needs CRP context; haemolysis and TMA are urgent.
- Sodium disorders need osmolality, volume status, urine studies and slow correction.
- Acid–base: pH → primary disorder → compensation → anion gap → delta ratio.
- LFT patterns (hepatocellular, cholestatic, synthetic) guide next steps; use FIB-4 for fibrosis risk.
- Troponin signals myocardial injury; delta and clinical context diagnose MI. Natriuretic peptides rule out HF better than rule in.
- Fluid analyses (CSF, pleural, ascitic, synovial) have standard criteria; SBP and septic arthritis are emergencies.
- Order tests that will change decisions, and communicate results and uncertainty.

## Self-assessment

**Questions**

1. Why does a panel of 20 tests commonly produce at least one abnormal result in a healthy person?
2. A man aged 62 has Hb 98 g/L, MCV 71 fL, ferritin 9 µg/L. What is the next step?
3. List four causes of spurious hyperkalaemia.
4. A patient has pH 7.28, PaCO₂ 3.0 kPa, HCO₃⁻ 10 mmol/L, Na 140, Cl 100. Calculate the anion gap and name the disorder and causes to consider.
5. How do you recognise hepatocellular vs cholestatic liver injury, and what is the R ratio?
6. What is the minimum evidence required to diagnose MI using hs-troponin?
7. Describe Light's criteria and one limitation.
8. A CSF sample shows 1500 neutrophils/µL, protein 3 g/L, glucose 1.0 mmol/L (plasma 6). What is the diagnosis and the immediate action?
9. What is the maximum safe rate of sodium correction in chronic hyponatraemia, and why?
10. Give three reasons that the ESR may be raised without inflammatory disease.

**Answers**

1. Each test has a 5% chance of falling outside the 95% interval; with 20 independent tests the probability of ≥ 1 abnormal ≈ 1 − 0.95²⁰ ≈ 64%.
2. Iron deficiency anaemia in a man: look for gastrointestinal blood loss (upper and lower endoscopy, coeliac serology), review NSAIDs/anticoagulants, then treat with iron; urgent referral if red flags.
3. Haemolysis in vitro, thrombocytosis/leukocytosis, delayed processing/cold storage, EDTA contamination, prolonged tourniquet/fist clenching, (also familial pseudohyperkalaemia).
4. Anion gap = 140 − (100 + 10) = 30: high anion gap metabolic acidosis with appropriate respiratory compensation (Winter's: 1.5 × 10 + 8 = 23 mmHg ≈ 3.1 kPa). Consider ketoacidosis, lactate, uraemia, toxic alcohols, salicylates; calculate osmolar gap.
5. Hepatocellular: ALT/AST markedly up compared to ALP; cholestatic: ALP/GGT up. R = (ALT/ULN) ÷ (ALP/ULN): > 5 hepatocellular, < 2 cholestatic, 2–5 mixed.
6. Rise and/or fall of troponin with at least one value above the 99th percentile, plus evidence of acute myocardial ischaemia (symptoms, ECG changes, imaging, or thrombus).
7. Exudate if pleural protein/serum > 0.5, pleural LDH/serum LDH > 0.6, or pleural LDH > 2/3 ULN serum; limitation: misclassifies some transudates (e.g., diuresed heart failure) as exudates; use albumin gradient or NT-proBNP.
8. Bacterial meningitis; immediate empirical IV antibiotics (ceftriaxone ± vancomycin, add ampicillin if risk for Listeria), dexamethasone before/with the first dose, cultures/PCR, supportive care and notification as required.
9. ≤ 8–10 mmol/L in 24 hours (≤ 8 in high-risk patients) to avoid osmotic demyelination syndrome.
10. Age, anaemia, pregnancy, renal disease/nephrotic syndrome, paraproteinaemia, obesity, female sex, hypergammaglobulinaemia (any three).
