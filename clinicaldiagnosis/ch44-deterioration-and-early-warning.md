## Chapter 44. Deterioration and Early Warning: Subtle Signs That Precede Collapse

### Learning objectives

After studying this chapter you should be able to:

1. Explain why in-hospital cardiac arrest and unplanned ICU admission are usually preceded by hours of abnormal physiology, and why this deterioration is so often missed.
2. Recognise the earliest and most reliable signs of deterioration: respiratory rate, behaviour and mental state, urine output, capillary refill, and "clinician worry."
3. Use track-and-trigger systems (NEWS2, PEWS, MEOWS) correctly, and understand their limitations and false-reassurance traps.
4. Identify patients who deteriorate despite apparently normal vital signs (older, frail, beta-blocked, young and fit, pregnant, children, immunosuppressed).
5. Organise a rapid response: reassess with ABCDE, escalate with SBAR, involve critical care outreach, and make and communicate ceilings of treatment.
6. Describe the system factors (staffing, monitoring frequency, culture, handover, failure to rescue) that determine whether deterioration is acted on.
7. Apply simple strategies to prevent deterioration and "failure to rescue," including after surgery and at night.

---

### 44.1 The problem: deterioration is usually predictable

Studies of **in-hospital cardiac arrest** repeatedly show that **60–80% of patients have documented abnormal vital signs or other warning features in the 6–24 hours before the arrest,** and many have had **unrecognised or unaddressed deterioration** for hours. The same holds for **unplanned ICU admission** and **unexpected death.** The outcome of in-hospital arrest is poor (survival to discharge about 15–25%, and much lower in non-shockable rhythms and in patients with severe comorbidity), whereas early treatment of deterioration is simple and effective.

The central message is **"failure to rescue,"** not "failure to prevent": the patient's illness is often unavoidable, but the **delay in recognising, responding to and escalating** is not. Preventable harm arises from:

- **Failure to measure** vital signs (especially respiratory rate), or **to record** them.
- **Failure to recognise** the pattern or trend.
- **Failure to act or escalate** (waiting, "let's see how she is in an hour," reluctance to call a senior).
- **Failure of the response** (delay, inexperience, no clear plan, no treatment ceiling).
- **Failure of the system** (understaffing, night-time, weekends, poor handover, hierarchical culture).

This chapter teaches you to **see deterioration early,** to **trust objective trends and your own concern,** and to **act without delay.**

---

### 44.2 The physiology of deterioration

Acute illness (sepsis, haemorrhage, respiratory failure, cardiac failure, metabolic derangement) triggers a stereotyped physiological response designed to preserve **vital organ perfusion and oxygenation:**

1. **Respiratory drive increases** (hypoxia, acidosis, pain, anxiety, fever, sepsis): **tachypnoea precedes** other changes.
2. **Sympathetic activation:** **tachycardia,** vasoconstriction (cool peripheries, prolonged capillary refill, pallor), sweating.
3. **Redistribution of blood flow** away from skin, gut, kidney, and muscle to heart and brain: **reduced urine output,** ileus, mottling, rising lactate.
4. **Compensatory mechanisms** maintain blood pressure until reserve is exhausted. **Hypotension is a late sign** of cardiovascular failure; so is a **fall in conscious level.**
5. **Decompensation:** hypotension, reduced consciousness, rising respiratory rate with fatigue, arrhythmia, acidosis, organ failure, arrest.

The **sequence of vital sign changes** therefore typically is: **respiratory rate ↑ → heart rate ↑ → (temperature change) → urine output ↓ and capillary refill ↑ → behavioural/mental change → blood pressure ↓ → collapse.** The earliest and most valuable signs are those **most often neglected** (respiratory rate, behaviour, urine output).

---

### 44.3 The earliest signs

#### Respiratory rate: the most sensitive and most neglected vital sign

- **A rise in respiratory rate (≥ 21–25/min) is the most sensitive single predictor of serious deterioration** (cardiac arrest, ICU admission, death), outperforming heart rate and blood pressure.
- It is **often estimated, copied forward from a previous set, or recorded as "18"** without counting (digit preference). **Count it for a full 60 seconds** (30 seconds if regular), before touching the patient.
- Causes: hypoxia, pneumonia, PE, pulmonary oedema, sepsis, **metabolic acidosis** (DKA, renal failure, lactic acidosis, salicylates), pain, anxiety, fever, haemorrhage, abdominal catastrophe, hypercapnia (late: slow, shallow).
- **Do not be reassured by a normal SpO₂** (supplemental oxygen can mask respiratory failure, and oximetry does not measure ventilation or acidosis): a patient on oxygen with a respiratory rate of 28 is seriously ill.

#### Heart rate

- Tachycardia (>100) is a non-specific marker but a **sustained rate >110–120** is concerning. **Rising heart rate over hours** is more informative than a single value. **Absence of tachycardia** does not exclude shock in patients on beta-blockers, with pacemakers, in athletes, in older adults, or with neurogenic or hypothermia-related bradycardia. **Bradycardia** with hypotension: heart block, drugs, raised ICP, late hypovolaemia in young people, inferior MI, hypothermia.

#### Blood pressure

- **Late sign.** A systolic BP **<100 mmHg** (or a fall >40 mmHg from baseline) warrants action; **older hypertensive patients** may be in shock at a systolic of 110. **Narrow pulse pressure** (<25% of systolic) suggests reduced stroke volume; **wide pulse pressure** suggests vasodilatation (sepsis).

#### Urine output

- **<0.5 mL/kg/hour (about 30 mL/hour) for 2 consecutive hours** (or <400 mL/day) is an **early marker of renal hypoperfusion and AKI.** Often unmeasured: **measure** in the sick patient; catheterise if needed; check for retention first (bladder scan).

#### Temperature

- **Fever** (>38 °C) and **hypothermia** (<36 °C) both mark serious infection or inflammation; **hypothermia in sepsis carries a worse prognosis.** Absent in many older, immunosuppressed, and neonatal patients.

#### Capillary refill, skin, and peripheries

- **Capillary refill >2–3 seconds,** **cool, mottled or "ashen" skin,** peripheral cooling with a warm core (differential temperature) indicate poor perfusion; mottling spreads from the knees (**mottling score**).

#### Mental state and behaviour: "He's just not himself"

- **New confusion, agitation, drowsiness, restlessness, anxiety ("sense of impending doom"), or change in behaviour** are early signs of hypoxia, hypoperfusion, sepsis, hypoglycaemia, drugs, or metabolic disturbance. **Use AVPU or GCS and consider delirium (4AT/CAM).** In older adults, delirium may be the **only sign** of sepsis or MI. Pay attention to the **family and carers** ("she's not right").

#### Other warning features

- **New or worsening pain** (chest, abdomen, limb), **increasing oxygen requirement,** **rising lactate,** **falling bicarbonate,** **rising creatinine,** **new arrhythmia (AF with fast rate),** **vomiting,** **reduced oral intake,** **abdominal distension, ileus,** **bleeding (drain, wound, per rectum),** **fall in haemoglobin,** **new hypoglycaemia,** **seizure,** **rash,** **inability to complete sentences,** **use of accessory muscles,** and **staff or family worry.**

> **"Gut feeling" or "clinician worry"** (the sense that something is wrong despite normal numbers) is a **validated predictor** of deterioration and a legitimate reason to escalate. Document it and call.

### 44.4 Track-and-trigger systems

**Aggregate weighted early warning scores** combine several vital signs into a single number that triggers escalation. They standardise language, reduce variation, and improve outcomes when **linked to a competent, timely response.**

#### NEWS2 (National Early Warning Score 2)

(Chapter 37.) Seven parameters: respiratory rate, SpO₂ (Scale 1 or 2), supplemental oxygen, systolic BP, pulse, consciousness (new confusion scores 3), temperature. **Response thresholds:**

| NEWS2 | Clinical risk | Response |
|---|---|---|
| **0** | Low | Minimum 12-hourly monitoring |
| **1–4** | Low | Minimum 4–6-hourly; nurse decides if escalation is needed |
| **3 in a single parameter** | Low–medium | **Urgent ward-based response** (review by a clinician competent in acute illness) |
| **5–6** | Medium | **Urgent response:** clinician review within 30 minutes (some trusts 1 hour); consider critical care outreach; increase monitoring to at least hourly |
| **≥7** | High | **Emergency response:** continuous monitoring, immediate review by an experienced team (ICU outreach/rapid response/critical care), consider transfer to a higher level of care |

#### Variants

- **PEWS/PAWS (Paediatric Early Warning Score):** behaviour, cardiovascular, respiratory components; age-specific ranges.
- **MEOWS (Modified Early Obstetric Warning Score):** pregnancy-adjusted ranges (Chapter 39).
- **MEWS, ViEWS, CEWS, qSOFA** (sepsis), **ANTICIPATE, eCART** (electronic), **machine-learning alerts.**
- **Single-parameter trigger systems** ("calling criteria": e.g. RR <5 or >36, SBP <90, HR <40 or >130, SpO₂ <90% despite oxygen, acute change in consciousness, seizure, **any staff or family worry**) used in medical emergency teams (MET) in Australia and elsewhere.

#### Strengths and limitations

- **Strengths:** objective, simple, reproducible, improve recognition and escalation, support communication (the score number is understood by all), and allow monitoring of trends.
- **Limitations (know them):**
  1. **They are only as good as the measurements** (poorly counted respiratory rate, estimated observations, wrong scale for SpO₂).
  2. **False reassurance:** a **low score does not exclude serious illness** (young fit patients compensate until late; patients on beta-blockers; pregnant women; children; immunosuppressed; **those who deteriorate suddenly** (PE, MI, arrhythmia, haemorrhage, anaphylaxis, intracranial event)). Scores do not capture **pain, urine output, lactate, perfusion, behaviour, or specific diagnoses.**
  3. **False alarms** in chronic conditions (COPD with stable low saturations, AF with fast rate, chronic hypotension, anxiety), leading to alert fatigue; **individualise** targets (e.g. SpO₂ Scale 2, adjust parameters with senior agreement and documentation).
  4. **A score is a prompt for a human decision,** not a replacement for assessment. **Trends matter more than snapshots:** a rise of ≥2 points or a persistent upward drift should prompt review even if below the threshold.
  5. **The response depends on the system:** a score that nobody acts on is useless.

### 44.5 Who deteriorates without "looking sick"? High-risk groups and traps

| Group | Why the usual signs mislead |
|---|---|
| **Older adults and frail patients** | Blunted fever and tachycardia; delirium as the presenting sign; "baseline" low BP or high BP; polypharmacy; reduced physiological reserve; hypoactive delirium missed; **sepsis, MI, PE, and GI bleeding may present with falls or confusion** |
| **Patients on beta-blockers, calcium channel blockers, pacemakers** | No tachycardia in shock or hypoglycaemia |
| **Young, fit adults and athletes** | Compensate for up to 30–40% blood volume loss with near-normal BP; sudden collapse |
| **Children and infants** | Compensate until sudden decompensation; **tachycardia, tachypnoea, poor perfusion and behavioural change** are early; **hypotension is pre-terminal;** use age-specific ranges (Chapter 37, 59) and parental concern |
| **Pregnant and postpartum women** | Physiological tachycardia, tachypnoea, low BP can mask sepsis, haemorrhage, pre-eclampsia, PE; **blood loss up to 30–35% before signs;** use MEOWS; "fit women collapse suddenly" |
| **Immunosuppressed and neutropenic** | Fever and inflammatory signs may be absent; rapid progression |
| **Post-operative patients** | Pain, opioids, anaesthesia effects, ileus, and wound losses mask or mimic complications; **anastomotic leak, bleeding, PE, MI, AKI, sepsis** present on days 2–7; "just post-op" is an anchoring bias |
| **Patients with chronic respiratory disease (COPD)** | Baseline abnormalities (SpO₂ 88–92%); hypercapnia and exhaustion present with a **falling** respiratory rate and drowsiness; **a "normal" PaCO₂ in an asthmatic is ominous** |
| **Patients with spinal cord injury, neuropathy, diabetes** | Absent pain; autonomic dysfunction; silent MI and peritonitis |
| **Patients with learning disability, dementia, communication problems** | Pain and distress expressed as behaviour; use observational tools |
| **Patients on opioids, sedatives** | Respiratory depression with a "normal" rate that falls; **sedation score and capnography** more sensitive than SpO₂ (especially on oxygen) |
| **Patients with sleep apnoea, obesity** | Post-operative respiratory events at night |
| **Language barrier** | Underreporting and misunderstanding |
| **Patients with "do not attempt CPR" status** | Often **under-treated** for reversible deterioration: a DNACPR order does **not** mean "do not treat" |

#### Sudden deterioration despite a stable pattern

Some catastrophic events occur abruptly: **pulmonary embolism, massive haemorrhage, arrhythmia, anaphylaxis, tension pneumothorax, intracranial haemorrhage, aortic dissection, acute coronary syndrome, airway obstruction.** The response is the same: **ABCDE, call for help, treat the cause.**

### 44.6 What to do when you are worried: the response

#### Step 1: Don't leave the patient; get the facts

- **Re-measure** vital signs yourself (especially respiratory rate), **look at the patient** (colour, effort, sweating, interaction), **ask the patient how they feel,** **review the trend** (observation chart, fluid balance, drains), **check glucose,** **review medications** (opioids, antihypertensives, anticoagulants, sedatives, insulin), **review the plan and reasons for admission,** **check for a treatment escalation plan/resuscitation status.**

#### Step 2: ABCDE and treat as you go (Chapter 37)

- **A:** airway patent? **B:** respiratory rate, SpO₂, chest, oxygen (titrate), nebulisers, CPAP; **C:** HR, BP, capillary refill, JVP, urine output, **IV access, fluid bolus, bloods (gas, lactate, FBC, U&E, cultures, troponin, crossmatch), ECG;** **D:** AVPU/GCS, pupils, glucose, seizures; **E:** full exposure (wounds, drains, rash, calves, abdomen, back, lines), temperature.
- **Initiate immediate treatment** (oxygen, fluids, antibiotics, drugs) while calling for help.

#### Step 3: Escalate using SBAR

- **Call early,** not when "sure." **Use the NEWS2 or other score** and **state your concern explicitly** ("I'm worried"). **Call the senior responsible clinician, rapid response/MET, or critical care outreach** according to the trigger. **Do not accept "she's probably fine" if you remain concerned;** use the **chain of command** (escalate to the next senior, with "I need you to come now," or the two-challenge rule). Follow up if the response is delayed.

> **SBAR example:** *"I'm calling about Mr Rossi, bed 14, 3 days after bowel resection. **S:** his NEWS2 has risen from 2 to 7 in 3 hours; respiratory rate 28, pulse 118, systolic BP 94, new confusion, urine output 20 mL in 2 hours. **B:** day 3 post anterior resection, on PCA morphine, previously stable. **A:** I think he's septic with a possible anastomotic leak or chest source; he looks unwell, abdomen is distended and tender; lactate 3.6; I've started oxygen, given a fluid bolus and taken cultures. **R:** I need you to review him now; I'd like to discuss CT, antibiotics, and ICU outreach."*

#### Step 4: Define the plan and the ceiling

- **Diagnosis and treatment:** immediate therapy, investigations, source control, monitoring frequency (increase to hourly or continuous).
- **Level of care:** ward vs HDU/ICU; **critical care outreach review within 30–60 minutes** for NEWS2 ≥5–7.
- **Treatment escalation plan (TEP):** **document** whether the patient is for resuscitation, intubation, ICU, NIV, dialysis, etc., after discussion with the patient or family where possible. **DNACPR ≠ do not treat:** patients with DNACPR orders should receive all appropriate treatment for **reversible** deterioration unless otherwise agreed.
- **Communicate** with the patient and family (honestly, with compassion), the nursing team, and the on-call team; **hand over** using a structured format.

#### Step 5: Reassess and follow through

- **Reassess after every intervention** (usually within 15–30 minutes); if the patient does not improve, **escalate again.** **Review at defined intervals** and **document** (times, interventions, discussions). **Avoid anchoring** ("it's just post-operative pain").

### 44.7 Critical care outreach, rapid response and teamwork

- **Rapid response teams (RRT/MET) and critical care outreach teams (CCOT)** bring critical care skills to the ward, offering early assessment, treatment, advice, and triage to ICU.
- **Effective use** requires: **clear activation criteria** (including "worried" as a criterion), **a culture that rewards calling,** **no penalty for "false alarms,"** and **the ward team remains involved** (the RRT is a resource, not a replacement).
- **Evidence:** RRTs reduce non-ICU cardiac arrests (and probably some deaths), but effects on overall mortality are uncertain: the **afferent limb** (detection and calling) is the weakest part of the chain: **failure to activate** is more important than team performance.
- **Leadership and communication:** one team leader, defined roles, closed-loop communication, SBAR, explicit statement of the problem and plan, and a **debrief.**

---

### 44.8 System factors and "failure to rescue"

Common contributors identified in investigations:

1. **Inadequate monitoring:** infrequent observations; observations not taken at night; no continuous monitoring in high-risk patients; **unmeasured respiratory rate;** equipment faults.
2. **Poor recognition:** misinterpretation of trends; **normalisation** of abnormal values ("she's always tachycardic"); failure to appreciate the **combination** of parameters; **anchoring on a benign diagnosis.**
3. **Delayed escalation:** hierarchy (reluctance to disturb seniors), fear of being wrong, inexperience, belief that "it can wait until the morning round," **no named responsible clinician,** **weekend and night effects** (reduced staffing, delays in blood tests and imaging).
4. **Inadequate response:** junior clinicians without support; no plan; delay in antibiotics, fluids, or oxygen; failure to review after intervention; inappropriate "wait and see."
5. **Communication failures:** poor handover; unclear **treatment escalation plans;** lack of family involvement.
6. **Organisational:** under-resourced wards, outliers (patients on the wrong ward), delays in ICU admission, inadequate education, no feedback.

**Mitigation:** standardised observation and escalation protocols, **electronic early warning with alerts** (with appropriate tuning), dedicated outreach teams, **sepsis and AKI bundles,** safety huddles, **handover tools (SBAR, I-PASS),** structured **post-operative pathways,** **patient and family-activated escalation** (e.g. Martha's Rule: the right of patients and families to request an urgent independent review), **audits of deterioration and cardiac arrests,** education (ALS, ABCDE, simulation), **psychological safety** and learning from errors.

### 44.9 Special settings and examples

#### Post-operative patients

- **Common time courses:** **day 0–1:** bleeding, pain, hypoxia (atelectasis, opioids), arrhythmia, hypotension (blood loss, anaesthesia), AKI; **day 2–7:** sepsis, anastomotic leak, wound infection, pneumonia, PE, MI, delirium, ileus; **later:** abscess, DVT/PE, line infection.
- **Tools:** enhanced recovery pathways, **early mobilisation,** **multimodal analgesia** (opioid-sparing), **post-operative continuous pulse oximetry and capnography in high-risk patients** (sleep apnoea, high opioid doses), **monitoring of fluid balance and drains,** **prompt response to rising lactate, falling urine output, or tachycardia,** **CT for suspected leak.**
- **"Post-op pain" and "ileus" are diagnoses of exclusion** in a patient who is tachycardic and tachypnoeic.

#### Nights and weekends

- Higher mortality for out-of-hours admissions and for post-operative patients in some systems. **Safeguards:** senior availability, outreach cover, **fewer low-priority tasks,** **explicit handover of "watcher" patients** (illness severity: **watcher** category in I-PASS), **anticipatory care plans** ("if A happens, do B"), **time-critical drugs and tests available.**

#### Children and obstetric units

- **PEWS and MEOWS** with parental or family concern as a trigger; **sepsis and haemorrhage pathways**; "fit and well women/children can deteriorate quickly."

#### Mental health and learning-disability units

- Physical health deterioration is under-recognised (e.g. antipsychotic-related NMS, clozapine myocarditis, constipation, aspiration, sepsis); apply early warning scores.

#### Community, primary care, and care homes

- **Tools:** **RESTORE2 (Recognise Early Soft Signs, Take Observations, Respond, Escalate)** in care homes: soft signs (change in behaviour, eating, drinking, mobility, continence, skin colour; "something is not right"); **NEWS2 in the community and ambulances;** **safety-netting** (Chapter 73); **telephone triage.**

### 44.10 Cardiac arrest prevention and treatment escalation

- **Prevention:** the best treatment for cardiac arrest is **preventing it:** recognise and treat deterioration; identify patients for whom arrest is expected and discuss **resuscitation preferences in advance** (with senior clinicians, patients and families, using clear, compassionate communication); implement **ReSPECT**-type or local **treatment escalation plans.**
- **If arrest occurs:** immediate **CPR and defibrillation** (Chapter 37), call the resuscitation team, identify and treat reversible causes (4 Hs/4 Ts), **post-resuscitation care** and **debrief.**
- **After events:** review contributing factors; **audit** early warning scores in the 24 hours before arrest; share learning.

---

### 44.11 Communication with patients and families

- **Tell patients and families** what is happening in plain language; **invite concerns** and **encourage them to speak up** if the patient seems different ("What would worry you?"); **provide contacts** for escalating concerns (e.g. patient-activated rapid response); **involve families in decisions about treatment limits;** **be honest about prognosis and uncertainty;** **record** conversations and decisions.
- **Respond to family concerns** as seriously as to monitoring data: relatives often detect subtle behaviour changes before staff do.

### 44.12 Worked cases

#### Case 1: Respiratory rate: the early signal

*A 72-year-old woman, day 2 after a laparoscopic cholecystectomy, is tired and "a bit short of breath." Observations from the HCA: RR 18 (recorded at 06:00 and 10:00), HR 96, BP 118/66, SpO₂ 94% on 2 L oxygen, temperature 37.4 °C. NEWS2 = 4. The nurse counts the respiratory rate at 26 and notes she is mildly confused. NEWS2 now 8.*

**Reasoning.** The **estimated respiratory rate** hid early deterioration: a measured rate of 26 plus new confusion, tachycardia, and oxygen requirement indicate **possible pneumonia, PE, bile leak with sepsis, or MI.**

**Action.** ABCDE, increase oxygen, blood gas and lactate, ECG, CXR, bloods and cultures, IV fluids, urgent senior review, **antibiotics if sepsis,** CTPA or CT abdomen as appropriate, critical care outreach, discuss ceiling of care.

#### Case 2: The beta-blocked patient

*A 68-year-old man on bisoprolol, 3 days after a hemicolectomy, has pulse 82, BP 108/60, RR 22, temperature 37.8 °C, lactate 3.4, urine output 15 mL/hour, mild abdominal distension.*

**Reasoning.** **Absent tachycardia** due to beta-blockade; the **respiratory rate, urine output, lactate, and abdominal signs** indicate sepsis or leak.

**Action.** Treat as sepsis: cultures, IV antibiotics within the hour, fluids, urgent surgical review and CT, outreach, monitor hourly.

#### Case 3: The young fit patient

*A 24-year-old rugby player with a ruptured spleen after a tackle has HR 112, BP 118/74, RR 22, looks well and talks normally; FAST positive.*

**Reasoning.** **Compensated class II haemorrhage;** hypotension will appear late and abruptly.

**Action.** Treat as haemorrhage: IV access, cross-match, FAST/CT, surgical/IR review, early blood if worsening; do not be reassured by normal BP.

#### Case 4: Sepsis in an older patient with "normal" vitals

*An 86-year-old man in a care home with dementia is "less alert" and refuses food for 24 hours. Temperature 36.2 °C, pulse 88, BP 126/70, RR 20. The carer reports he is "not himself." On transfer, 4AT is 8, lactate 3.1, WBC 17, chest X-ray shows consolidation.*

**Reasoning.** **Hypoactive delirium and sepsis** (pneumonia) with a normal NEWS2 apart from consciousness: **clinician and carer worry were the only warning.**

**Action.** Antibiotics, fluids, oxygen as needed, review medications, treat delirium (non-drug measures), discuss goals of care with the family.

#### Case 5: Treatment ceiling and DNACPR

*An 80-year-old woman with advanced heart failure and a DNACPR order has increasing breathlessness, RR 32, SpO₂ 86%, crackles, HR 130. The night team says "she's DNACPR, so we'll just keep her comfortable."*

**Reasoning.** DNACPR applies to **cardiopulmonary resuscitation,** not to treatment of reversible deterioration. Acute pulmonary oedema is treatable and may allow her to recover.

**Action.** **Treat:** sit up, oxygen, IV furosemide, nitrate, consider NIV (if consistent with her wishes and escalation plan), assess precipitant; discuss with the patient/family and senior clinician what treatments are appropriate; document a clear **treatment escalation plan** (e.g. for ward-based treatment and NIV, not ICU).

#### Case 6: Post-operative night-time deterioration

*A 58-year-old man, 36 hours after a total knee replacement, has been on PCA morphine and sleeping. At 03:00, RR 8, SpO₂ 90% on 2 L, snoring, difficult to rouse (sedation score 3).*

**Reasoning.** **Opioid-induced respiratory depression** with sleep apnoea.

**Action.** Stimulate, open airway, **stop PCA,** oxygen via mask, bag-valve-mask if needed, **naloxone in small titrated doses (0.1–0.4 mg),** call the on-call doctor/ICU outreach, review analgesia plan (regional/multimodal), continuous monitoring (SpO₂ and capnography) thereafter.

#### Case 7: Family concern

*A mother says her 14-month-old, admitted with a respiratory infection, "isn't right: more sleepy and not feeding." PEWS = 3, then 4 after 1 hour. The registrar is reluctant ("he's coughing, he's fine"). The nurse activates the family-concern escalation.*

**Reasoning.** **Parental concern and rising PEWS** are valid triggers; **hypoxia, exhaustion, or sepsis** are possible.

**Action.** Senior paediatric review, ABCDE, SpO₂, blood gas, glucose, oxygen/HFNC, consider antibiotics, PICU liaison; parents informed.

### 44.13 Common pitfalls

- **Estimating or copying the respiratory rate.**
- **Believing the SpO₂** while ignoring the respiratory rate and work of breathing, especially on oxygen.
- **Dismissing new confusion** as "dementia" or "UTI" without a full assessment.
- **Waiting for hypotension.**
- **Reassurance from a low early warning score** in high-risk groups.
- **Not looking at trends** and fluid balance (urine output).
- **Anchoring** on "post-operative pain" or "chronic condition."
- **Delay in escalating** (waiting for the next ward round); **reluctance to call seniors;** accepting a poor response.
- **Treating DNACPR as "do not treat."**
- **No treatment escalation plan** or ceiling of care.
- **Failing to reassess** after treatment.
- **Ignoring relatives' or carers' concerns.**
- **Inadequate monitoring at night,** after opioids, or after surgery.
- **Failing to learn** from cardiac arrests and near misses.

---

### 44.14 Key points

- Most cardiac arrests and unplanned ICU admissions are preceded by hours of physiological deterioration: **recognition and response, not prevention of illness, is the lever.**
- The earliest signs: **rising respiratory rate, new confusion or behaviour change, reduced urine output, poor perfusion, rising heart rate;** hypotension and reduced consciousness are late.
- **Count the respiratory rate** for a full minute; do not rely on SpO₂ alone.
- Use NEWS2/PEWS/MEOWS with their thresholds, but know their limits: **trends, clinical judgement, and worry matter;** be especially vigilant in older, frail, beta-blocked, young fit, pregnant, paediatric, post-operative, and immunosuppressed patients.
- When worried: **ABCDE, treat as you go, escalate early (SBAR), define the level of care and treatment ceiling, and reassess.**
- DNACPR is not "do not treat"; document escalation plans and discuss them with patients and families.
- System design (monitoring, outreach, culture, handover, family-activated escalation) determines whether deterioration is rescued.

### 44.15 Self-assessment

1. Which vital sign is the most sensitive early predictor of deterioration and how should it be measured?
   *Answer: Respiratory rate; count it for a full minute (or 30 seconds if regular) by observation, not estimated or copied.*

2. Give four reasons why a patient may deteriorate despite a low early warning score.
   *Answer (any four): beta-blockers/pacemakers masking tachycardia; young fit adults compensating until late; older frail adults with atypical signs; pregnancy; children; immunosuppression; post-operative analgesia masking signs; sudden events (PE, arrhythmia, haemorrhage); poorly measured observations.*

3. What does a NEWS2 of 7 or more require?
   *Answer: Emergency response: immediate review by a team with critical care competencies (rapid response/outreach), continuous monitoring, and consideration of transfer to a higher level of care.*

4. State the meaning of DNACPR and what treatment should continue.
   *Answer: Do not attempt cardiopulmonary resuscitation in cardiac arrest; it does not preclude treatment of reversible deterioration (oxygen, antibiotics, fluids, NIV as appropriate), which should follow the documented treatment escalation plan.*

5. Describe the elements of an SBAR escalation call.
   *Answer: Situation (who, what concern, current observations), Background (relevant history, admission reason, treatments), Assessment (what you think is wrong, severity), Recommendation (what you need and by when).*

6. Why should relatives' concerns be treated as clinical information?
   *Answer: Family and carers often notice subtle behavioural and physiological changes (a validated predictor of deterioration); acting on them reduces missed deterioration.*
