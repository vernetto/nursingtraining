## Chapter 43. Metabolic Emergencies

### Learning objectives

After studying this chapter you should be able to:

1. Diagnose and manage diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA) and the hyperosmolar hyperglycaemic state (HHS), including fluid, insulin and potassium management, and recognise euglycaemic DKA.
2. Recognise and treat hypoglycaemia in diabetic and non-diabetic patients.
3. Manage life-threatening electrolyte disorders: hyperkalaemia, hypokalaemia, hyponatraemia, hypernatraemia, hypercalcaemia, hypocalcaemia and magnesium disorders, and avoid overcorrection.
4. Interpret acid–base disturbances (anion gap, delta gap, osmolar gap, Winter's formula) and identify the cause of metabolic acidosis, including toxic alcohol and lactic acidosis.
5. Recognise and treat thyroid storm, myxoedema coma and adrenal crisis.
6. Take a structured approach to the poisoned patient, and know the key antidotes and indications for dialysis.
7. Recognise and manage hypothermia, heat stroke, rhabdomyolysis and tumour lysis syndrome.

---

### 43.1 General approach

Metabolic emergencies often present non-specifically (confusion, drowsiness, vomiting, dyspnoea, weakness, arrhythmia, seizures, coma). The approach:

1. **ABCDE** and resuscitate first (Chapter 37). **Glucose, ECG and venous/arterial blood gas** in every sick patient with altered consciousness or suspected metabolic disturbance.
2. **Measure and interpret:** U&E (Na, K, creatinine), **glucose, ketones (beta-hydroxybutyrate), calcium (corrected), magnesium, phosphate, bicarbonate, chloride, lactate, osmolality,** LFTs, CK, thyroid function, cortisol, toxicology, paracetamol and salicylate levels.
3. **Treat life-threatening abnormalities immediately** (hyperkalaemia with ECG changes, severe hypoglycaemia, symptomatic hyponatraemia, DKA with shock), then diagnose and treat the cause.
4. **Correct deficits at the safe rate:** rapid changes in sodium, osmolality, glucose and potassium cause harm (osmotic demyelination, cerebral oedema, arrhythmia).
5. **Monitor closely and frequently,** and prevent recurrence (education, medication review).

# Part A: Diabetic Emergencies

### 43.2 Diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA)

**DKA** is a life-threatening state of **absolute or relative insulin deficiency** with raised counter-regulatory hormones, leading to **hyperglycaemia, ketosis, and metabolic acidosis.** It occurs mainly in type 1 diabetes (also type 2, ketosis-prone, pancreatic diabetes, and with SGLT2 inhibitors). Mortality <1% in specialist care but higher in older adults and with delay.

#### Precipitants ("the 5 Is" and others)

**I**nfection (the commonest, 30–40%), **I**nsulin omission or error (pump failure), **I**nfarction (MI, stroke), **I**ntercurrent illness (pancreatitis), **I**atrogenic/drugs (steroids, antipsychotics, SGLT2 inhibitors, thiazides), new diagnosis of diabetes, pregnancy, trauma, surgery, alcohol, eating disorders, psychological stress and non-adherence.

#### Presentation

Polyuria, polydipsia, weight loss, nausea, vomiting, **abdominal pain** (may mimic acute abdomen), **Kussmaul respiration (deep sighing),** **acetone ("pear-drop") breath,** dehydration (dry mucosa, tachycardia, hypotension), drowsiness or coma, hypothermia (sepsis), signs of precipitant. **Euglycaemic DKA** (glucose <14 mmol/L / 250 mg/dL, sometimes near normal): **SGLT2 inhibitors,** pregnancy, prolonged fasting, alcohol, bariatric surgery, insulin pump failure: **check ketones in any unwell patient on SGLT2 inhibitors.**

#### Diagnosis (ADA/JBDS; follow local definitions)

| | **DKA** |
|---|---|
| **Glucose** | >11 mmol/L (200 mg/dL) or known diabetes (may be normal in euglycaemic DKA) |
| **Ketones** | **Capillary or serum β-hydroxybutyrate ≥3.0 mmol/L** (or urine ketones ≥2+) |
| **Acidosis** | **Venous pH <7.3 and/or bicarbonate <15 mmol/L** |

**Severity (ADA):** mild (pH 7.25–7.30; bicarbonate 15–18), moderate (pH 7.00–7.24; bicarbonate 10–<15), severe (pH <7.00; bicarbonate <10; stupor/coma). Anion gap raised (>12). **Corrected sodium** (add 0.3 mmol/L per 1 mmol/L glucose above normal, i.e. about 1.6 mmol/L per 100 mg/dL); **initial K may be normal or high despite total body depletion** (acidosis and insulin deficiency shift K out; total deficit 3–5 mmol/kg).

Other tests: FBC (leucocytosis is common even without infection), U&E, creatinine, osmolality, amylase/lipase (often raised nonspecifically), LFTs, troponin, ECG (hyper/hypokalaemia, MI), chest X-ray, cultures, urinalysis, pregnancy test, HbA1c, serum phosphate and magnesium.

#### Management (summarised from JBDS/ADA; follow local protocol)

**Priorities: fluids, insulin, potassium, find and treat the precipitant, monitor.**

1. **Resuscitate (ABCDE),** two IV lines, **monitoring (ECG, SpO₂, BP, hourly urine output), catheter if severe.**
2. **Fluids (the first priority):** isotonic saline (0.9% NaCl) or balanced crystalloid:
   - **Hypotension (SBP <90 mmHg):** 500 mL bolus, repeat as needed; seek senior help; consider other causes of shock (sepsis, MI).
   - **Adults (JBDS):** 1 L over 1 hour, then 1 L over 2 hours, 1 L over 2 hours, 1 L over 4 hours, 1 L over 4 hours, 1 L over 6 hours (adjust to clinical status, avoiding overload in heart failure and renal disease). **(ADA:** 15–20 mL/kg/hour in the first hour, then 4–14 mL/kg/hour depending on sodium and hydration.)
   - **Add 10% glucose** (125 mL/hour) when glucose falls **below 14 mmol/L (250 mg/dL)** to continue insulin without hypoglycaemia while clearing ketones.
3. **Insulin:** start a **fixed-rate IV insulin infusion at 0.1 units/kg/hour** (soluble/regular insulin) **after the first fluid bolus and after confirming K ≥3.5 mmol/L (ADA: ≥3.3);** if K <3.3 (3.5), give potassium first (and hold insulin) to avoid arrhythmia. **Continue basal (long-acting) insulin** at the usual dose. **Aim:** ketones falling by ≥0.5 mmol/L/hour (or bicarbonate rising by ≥3 mmol/L/hour) and glucose falling by 3 mmol/L/hour; **avoid rapid falls in glucose** (cerebral oedema in children; aim <5 mmol/L/hour). Increase the infusion by 1 unit/hour if the targets are not met.
4. **Potassium:**
   - **K >5.5 mmol/L:** no potassium, recheck hourly.
   - **K 3.5–5.5 mmol/L:** add **40 mmol/L** potassium chloride to each litre (20–30 mmol/hour maximum via peripheral line per local policy), aiming K 4–5.
   - **K <3.5 mmol/L:** senior review; replace potassium (higher rates with ECG monitoring), hold insulin until K ≥3.3–3.5.
   - **Monitor K hourly initially** (hypokalaemia is the commonest preventable cause of death).
5. **Bicarbonate:** **not routinely given;** consider only for **pH <6.9** (ADA) (or <7.0 with haemodynamic compromise or life-threatening hyperkalaemia): 50–100 mmol over 1–2 hours. Evidence of benefit is lacking and it may worsen hypokalaemia and cause paradoxical CSF acidosis.
6. **Phosphate, magnesium:** replace if <0.3–0.5 mmol/L (respiratory failure, haemolysis, cardiac dysfunction); not routine.
7. **Monitoring:** capillary glucose and ketones hourly; **venous gas at 60 minutes, 2 hours, then every 2 hours;** U&E (K) hourly then 2–4-hourly; fluid balance; neurological status.
8. **Treat precipitants:** antibiotics for infection, ECG/troponin, etc.
9. **VTE prophylaxis** (LMWH) in all.
10. **Resolution (JBDS):** **ketones <0.6 mmol/L and pH >7.3** (bicarbonate >15). **Transition to subcutaneous insulin** once resolved and the patient is eating and drinking: give subcutaneous short-acting insulin with meals and **continue the IV insulin for 30–60 minutes after the first subcutaneous dose** (to avoid rebound), long-acting insulin continued.
11. **Referral to the diabetes team** for education, review of insulin technique, sick-day rules, ketone testing, psychological support.

#### Complications

Hypokalaemia, hypoglycaemia, cerebral oedema (**children** and young adults: headache, vomiting, reduced consciousness, bradycardia, hypertension within 4–12 hours of treatment; treat with mannitol 0.5–1 g/kg or 3% saline, reduce fluids), ARDS, AKI, VTE, hyperchloraemic acidosis (non-gap) during recovery (normal), rhabdomyolysis, mucormycosis (rare), gastric stasis.

#### Special groups

- **Children and adolescents:** use paediatric protocols (fluids at about 10 mL/kg boluses only if shocked; slower rehydration over 48 hours; insulin 0.05–0.1 units/kg/hour started 1 hour after fluids).
- **Pregnancy:** DKA at lower glucose, fetal risk; obstetric involvement.
- **SGLT2-inhibitor DKA:** stop the drug, prolonged ketosis, may need dextrose with insulin for longer.

### 43.3 Hyperosmolar hyperglycaemic state (HHS)

**HHS** is a state of **severe hyperglycaemia, hyperosmolality and dehydration without significant ketoacidosis,** typically in **older adults with type 2 diabetes** (also younger patients with obesity). Mortality 5–20% (higher than DKA) because of age, comorbidities and delay.

- **Precipitants:** infection (pneumonia, UTI, sepsis), MI, stroke, drugs (steroids, diuretics, atypical antipsychotics), poor adherence, dehydration, dementia, nursing-home residents (impaired thirst), TPN.
- **Diagnosis (JBDS):** **glucose >30 mmol/L (540–600 mg/dL), effective osmolality >320 mOsm/kg** [2 × (Na + K) + glucose, + urea in total osmolality; effective = 2 × Na + glucose], **hypovolaemia, ketones <3.0 mmol/L, pH >7.3, bicarbonate >15 mmol/L;** altered consciousness (osmolality >350 mOsm/kg), seizures, focal deficits, **profound dehydration (fluid deficit 100–220 mL/kg).**
- **Management:**
  1. **Fluids first:** 0.9% NaCl (1 L over the first hour in adults if SBP is low; guided by cardiac status and sodium), aiming for **positive fluid balance of 3–6 L by 6 hours** and the rest over 24–48 hours; **the fall in osmolality should be 3–8 mOsm/kg/hour** and sodium should not fall by more than 10 mmol/L in 24 hours (risk of cerebral oedema or central pontine myelinolysis). Switch to 0.45% saline if sodium rises or fails to fall despite adequate volume.
  2. **Insulin: delay** (unless ketonaemia present) until fluid resuscitation has begun and glucose is no longer falling by >5 mmol/L/hour with fluids alone: then **fixed-rate 0.05 units/kg/hour** (as osmolality falls too rapidly with high doses).
  3. **Potassium** replacement as for DKA; magnesium and phosphate as needed.
  4. **Anticoagulation:** **prophylactic (or full-dose, per JBDS) LMWH** (high thrombosis risk).
  5. **Treat precipitant,** foot care (pressure ulcers), nutrition.
  6. **Monitoring:** hourly glucose, osmolality/sodium, K; neurological status.
- **Resolution:** osmolality <315 mOsm/kg, hydration, mental state improvement; then transition to subcutaneous insulin or oral agents.

### 43.4 Hypoglycaemia

#### Definitions (ADA/ISPAD)

- **Level 1:** glucose <4.0 mmol/L (70 mg/dL) and ≥3.0 mmol/L (54 mg/dL): alert value.
- **Level 2:** <3.0 mmol/L (54 mg/dL): clinically significant.
- **Level 3 (severe):** altered mental or physical status requiring assistance.

**Whipple's triad** (for diagnosis in non-diabetic patients): symptoms of hypoglycaemia, low plasma glucose measured at the time, and relief when glucose is raised.

#### Symptoms

**Autonomic:** sweating, tremor, palpitations, hunger, anxiety, pallor. **Neuroglycopenic:** confusion, irritability, blurred vision, slurred speech, behavioural change (mimics intoxication), drowsiness, seizures, focal neurological deficits (mimics stroke), coma. **Hypoglycaemia unawareness** (repeated episodes, beta-blockers, autonomic neuropathy, tight control).

#### Causes

- **In diabetes:** **insulin and sulfonylureas (and meglitinides)** (most common), missed or delayed meals, exercise, alcohol, renal impairment (reduced insulin clearance), dosing errors, drug interactions (quinolones, co-trimoxazole, beta-blockers), gastroparesis, pump malfunction.
- **In non-diabetic patients:** **drugs** (quinine, quinolones, salicylates, beta-blockers, pentamidine, disopyramide, accidental/factitious insulin or sulfonylurea), **alcohol and starvation,** **sepsis,** **critical illness,** **liver failure,** **adrenal insufficiency, hypopituitarism,** **insulinoma and other tumours (non-islet cell tumour hypoglycaemia: IGF-II),** **post-bariatric surgery (dumping, nesidioblastosis),** **autoimmune hypoglycaemia,** inborn errors, **reactive hypoglycaemia** (post-prandial), malaria (especially in children and pregnancy), renal failure, heart failure.

#### Treatment

| Situation | Action |
|---|---|
| **Conscious, able to swallow** | **15–20 g fast-acting carbohydrate** (3–4 glucose tablets, 150–200 mL fruit juice, or glucose gel); **recheck in 15 minutes** and repeat if <4 mmol/L; follow with a longer-acting carbohydrate (biscuits, sandwich) |
| **Unable to swallow or unconscious (IV access available)** | **IV glucose:** **10% glucose 150–200 mL over 15 minutes** (or 20% glucose 75–100 mL); avoid 50% glucose in peripheral veins when possible (phlebitis, extravasation injury) in adults, but use if no alternative; **in children: 2 mL/kg of 10% glucose.** Recheck glucose in 10 minutes, repeat if needed, then infusion (10% glucose at 100 mL/hour) |
| **No IV access** | **Glucagon 1 mg IM or SC** (0.5 mg if <25 kg or <6–8 years); response depends on hepatic glycogen stores (less effective in alcohol, starvation, liver disease, sulfonylurea overdose); intranasal glucagon (3 mg) or oral glucose gel in the buccal cavity as alternatives |
| **Sulfonylurea hypoglycaemia (prolonged)** | **Continuous glucose infusion,** admission for observation (up to 24–72 hours, longer in renal impairment), **octreotide** (50 µg SC every 8 hours) to suppress insulin secretion in recurrent hypoglycaemia despite glucose; check for interactions |
| **Alcohol, malnutrition** | Give **thiamine** before or with glucose |
| **Post-hypoglycaemia** | Identify cause, adjust therapy, review sick-day rules, driving advice; hypoglycaemia unawareness: relaxation of targets, structured education, continuous glucose monitoring, sensor-augmented pumps |

#### Evaluation of unexplained hypoglycaemia (non-diabetic)

During spontaneous hypoglycaemia (or a supervised fast), measure **glucose, insulin, C-peptide, proinsulin, β-hydroxybutyrate, sulfonylurea/meglitinide screen,** and **insulin antibodies:** high insulin and C-peptide with low β-hydroxybutyrate suggest endogenous hyperinsulinism (insulinoma, sulfonylurea, post-bariatric); high insulin with low C-peptide suggests exogenous insulin; low insulin with high β-hydroxybutyrate suggests starvation, adrenal insufficiency, liver disease, or sepsis. Imaging (CT, MRI, endoscopic ultrasound, PET) for insulinoma.

# Part B: Electrolyte and Acid–Base Emergencies

### 43.5 Hyperkalaemia

**Definition:** serum K⁺ >5.5 mmol/L (mild 5.5–5.9; moderate 6.0–6.4; **severe ≥6.5**); **ECG changes** and rate of rise matter more than the absolute value.

#### Causes

- **Reduced excretion:** **AKI and CKD,** **drugs** (ACE inhibitors, ARBs, spironolactone/eplerenone, trimethoprim, NSAIDs, heparin, calcineurin inhibitors, potassium supplements, potassium-sparing diuretics, amiloride), **adrenal insufficiency,** type 4 RTA.
- **Shift from cells:** **acidosis,** insulin deficiency/DKA, beta-blockers, **rhabdomyolysis, tumour lysis,** haemolysis, burns, tissue ischaemia, suxamethonium, digoxin toxicity, hyperosmolality, exercise.
- **Increased intake:** supplements, salt substitutes, blood transfusions, penicillin G.
- **Pseudohyperkalaemia:** **haemolysed sample,** thrombocytosis, leucocytosis, fist clenching, delayed processing; check by repeat sample (whole-blood potassium on a gas analyser).

#### Clinical and ECG features

Weakness, paralysis, paraesthesia, palpitations, nausea, but often **asymptomatic until arrhythmia.** **ECG progression:** **peaked (tented) T waves** → **flattened P waves, prolonged PR** → **widened QRS** → **sine-wave pattern** → **VF/asystole.** ECG may be normal at K ≥7; the absence of ECG changes does not exclude danger.

#### Management (stepwise: protect, shift, remove, treat the cause)

| Step | Therapy |
|---|---|
| **1. Protect the myocardium (if ECG changes or K ≥6.5)** | **Calcium gluconate 10% 10–30 mL IV over 5–10 minutes** (or **calcium chloride 10% 5–10 mL via a central line**), **repeat after 5–10 minutes if ECG changes persist;** onset within minutes, lasts 30–60 minutes; **continuous ECG monitoring;** caution in digoxin toxicity (traditionally contraindicated; evidence limited: give slowly if life-threatening) |
| **2. Shift potassium into cells** | **Insulin 10 units (soluble) IV with 25 g glucose** (50 mL of 50% glucose, or 250 mL of 10% glucose) over 15–30 minutes; **monitor glucose hourly for at least 6 hours** (hypoglycaemia is common, especially in CKD: consider 5 units, or no glucose if glucose >14); **salbutamol 10–20 mg nebulised** (additive effect; avoid in ischaemic heart disease with tachyarrhythmia); **sodium bicarbonate 50–100 mmol** IV only in **metabolic acidosis** (modest effect otherwise) |
| **3. Remove potassium** | **Loop diuretic** (furosemide 40–80 mg IV) if the patient is not anuric or hypovolaemic; **potassium binders:** **sodium zirconium cyclosilicate 10 g three times daily up to 48 hours** (rapid onset about 1 hour), **patiromer** (slower), **calcium resonium** (slow, less effective, risk of bowel necrosis); **haemodialysis** for severe or refractory hyperkalaemia, oliguric AKI, or ongoing release (rhabdomyolysis, tumour lysis) |
| **4. Treat the cause** | Stop offending drugs, treat AKI, adrenal crisis (hydrocortisone), acidosis, relieve obstruction; low-potassium diet; review RAAS inhibitors (reintroduce with binders where indicated) |
| **5. Monitor** | ECG and K every 1–2 hours until stable; recheck at 6 hours for rebound |

### 43.6 Hypokalaemia

**Definition:** K⁺ <3.5 mmol/L (mild 3.0–3.4; moderate 2.5–2.9; **severe <2.5**).

- **Causes:** **GI loss** (vomiting, diarrhoea, laxatives, fistulae), **renal loss** (loop and thiazide diuretics, hyperaldosteronism (primary), Cushing, hypomagnesaemia, Bartter/Gitelman syndromes, licorice, DKA/osmotic diuresis, RTA, amphotericin, aminoglycosides), **shift into cells** (insulin, beta-agonists, alkalosis, hypokalaemic periodic paralysis, thyrotoxic periodic paralysis, refeeding), **reduced intake.**
- **Features:** muscle weakness, cramps, constipation/ileus, rhabdomyolysis, polyuria, palpitations; **ECG:** flattened T waves, **U waves,** ST depression, prolonged QT/PR, arrhythmias (torsades de pointes, VT, VF), especially with digoxin or other QT-prolonging drugs.
- **Management:**
  - **Correct magnesium** (refractory hypokalaemia persists until Mg is replaced).
  - **Oral potassium chloride** (40–80 mmol/day in divided doses) for mild-to-moderate cases; **IV potassium chloride** for severe hypokalaemia, symptoms, or arrhythmia: **central line preferred, maximum 20 mmol/hour (10 mmol/hour peripherally; concentration ≤40 mmol/L peripherally)** with continuous ECG; recheck every 1–2 hours.
  - **Find the cause** (urine K and chloride, acid–base status, renin and aldosterone, blood pressure): hypertension with hypokalaemia suggests mineralocorticoid excess; normotensive: diuretics, GI, Bartter/Gitelman.

### 43.7 Hyponatraemia

**Definition:** serum Na⁺ <135 mmol/L (mild 130–134; moderate 125–129; **severe <125; profound <120**). **Hypotonic hyponatraemia** (low serum osmolality, <275 mOsm/kg) is the clinically important form. **Exclude** pseudohyponatraemia (hyperlipidaemia, hyperproteinaemia: normal measured osmolality), **hyperglycaemia** (translocational: Na falls ~2.4 mmol/L per 5.6 mmol/L (100 mg/dL) glucose rise), mannitol, radiocontrast.

#### Classification and causes

| Volume status | Causes | Urine Na (spot) |
|---|---|---|
| **Hypovolaemic** | GI losses (vomiting, diarrhoea), burns, third-spacing; **diuretics** (thiazides), adrenal insufficiency, cerebral salt wasting, salt-wasting nephropathy | Non-renal loss: <20–30 mmol/L; renal loss: >30 |
| **Euvolaemic** | **SIADH** (CNS disorders, lung disease/cancer (small cell), drugs (SSRIs, carbamazepine, oxcarbazepine, cyclophosphamide, vincristine, desmopressin, MDMA), pain, nausea, post-operative), **hypothyroidism, adrenal insufficiency, primary polydipsia, low solute intake** (beer potomania, tea-and-toast), reset osmostat | >30 mmol/L (SIADH); low in polydipsia/low solute (urine osmolality <100) |
| **Hypervolaemic** | **Heart failure, cirrhosis, nephrotic syndrome, CKD** | <20 mmol/L (hypervolaemic non-renal), >20 in CKD |

**Diagnostic work-up:** serum osmolality, glucose, urea, creatinine, lipids; **urine osmolality** (<100 mOsm/kg suggests primary polydipsia or low solute intake; >100 indicates ADH-mediated), **urine sodium and osmolality,** uric acid (low in SIADH), TSH, cortisol, volume assessment, medication review, chest imaging, brain imaging if neurological features.

**SIADH criteria:** hypotonic hyponatraemia, **inappropriately concentrated urine (osmolality >100 mOsm/kg), urine Na >30 mmol/L,** clinical euvolaemia, normal thyroid, adrenal and renal function, no diuretics.

#### Clinical features

Depend on **severity and rate of fall.** **Moderate:** nausea, headache, confusion, lethargy, unsteadiness, falls. **Severe:** vomiting, seizures, reduced consciousness, coma, respiratory arrest, **cerebral oedema** (non-cardiogenic pulmonary oedema, brain herniation). **Acute (<48 hours)** is more dangerous (marathon runners, MDMA, post-operative, psychogenic polydipsia, hypotonic fluid administration, thiazides, colonoscopy preparation, desmopressin); **chronic (>48 hours):** adapted brain, risk of osmotic demyelination with overly rapid correction.

#### Management

| Situation | Action |
|---|---|
| **Severe symptoms** (seizures, coma, vomiting, cardiorespiratory distress) | **Hypertonic saline (3% NaCl) 150 mL IV over 20 minutes (or 2 mL/kg),** repeat once or twice until the Na rises by **5 mmol/L (or symptoms improve);** check Na every 20–60 minutes; **limit correction to 10 mmol/L in the first 24 hours and 8 mmol/L in each subsequent 24 hours** (limit to **≤8 mmol/L/day** in patients at **high risk of osmotic demyelination:** alcohol use disorder, malnutrition, liver disease, hypokalaemia, Na <105); treat seizures with benzodiazepines |
| **Moderate symptoms** (nausea, confusion) | Single 3% saline bolus (e.g. 150 mL) or slower infusion, search for the cause; avoid rapid overcorrection |
| **Chronic, asymptomatic or mild** | **Treat the cause;** hypovolaemic: **0.9% saline;** SIADH: **fluid restriction (e.g. 500–1,000 mL/day),** salt tablets, **urea 15–30 g/day,** loop diuretic plus salt, **tolvaptan** (selected, risk of overcorrection and liver injury), treat underlying cause (stop offending drug); hypervolaemic: fluid and sodium restriction, loop diuretics, treat heart failure/cirrhosis; adrenal insufficiency/hypothyroidism: replace hormones; **primary polydipsia:** fluid restriction |
| **Monitoring** | Na every 2–4 hours (hourly for acute, 6-hourly for chronic after the first 24 hours); **urine output** (a sudden brisk water diuresis signals impending overcorrection as ADH suppresses, e.g. after volume repletion or stopping a thiazide) |
| **Overcorrection (rise >10–12 mmol/L in 24 hours)** | **Relower the sodium:** **desmopressin 2–4 µg IV every 6–8 hours** plus **5% dextrose** (3 mL/kg/hour) to bring Na back toward the safe limit, with frequent monitoring (within 24 hours of overcorrection) |
| **Avoid** | Hypotonic fluids; **rapid correction;** **fluid restriction in hypovolaemic hyponatraemia;** hypertonic saline in hypervolaemic overload unless life-threatening |

**Osmotic demyelination syndrome (ODS):** 2–6 days after rapid correction: dysarthria, dysphagia, quadriparesis, pseudobulbar palsy, locked-in syndrome, seizures, behavioural change; MRI shows pontine and extrapontine lesions; mostly irreversible; prevention is key.

### 43.8 Hypernatraemia

**Definition:** serum Na⁺ >145 mmol/L (severe >160). Always indicates **hyperosmolality and cellular dehydration.**

- **Causes:** **water deficit:** inadequate intake (older adults, infants, impaired thirst or access, dementia, ICU), **insensible losses** (fever, burns, sweating), **GI losses** (osmotic diarrhoea, lactulose), **renal losses** (diabetes insipidus (central or nephrogenic), osmotic diuresis in hyperglycaemia, mannitol, loop diuretics, post-obstructive diuresis), **sodium excess** (hypertonic saline, sodium bicarbonate, salt ingestion, primary hyperaldosteronism, Cushing).
- **Features:** thirst, irritability, lethargy, confusion, seizures, coma, hyperreflexia, muscle twitching; intracranial haemorrhage (shrinking brain); signs of dehydration.
- **Management:**
  1. **Replace volume deficits first** (isotonic saline in hypovolaemic shock).
  2. **Calculate the free water deficit** = TBW × (Na/140 − 1), where **TBW** (total body water) = 0.6 × weight (men), 0.5 (women), 0.5 (older men), 0.45 (older women); **correct slowly:** **fall in Na ≤10 mmol/L per 24 hours (about 0.5 mmol/L/hour)** (faster in acute hypernatraemia <48 hours, up to 1 mmol/L/hour) to avoid cerebral oedema; **route:** oral or enteral water preferred; IV 5% dextrose (or 0.45% saline) if oral is not possible; monitor Na every 4–6 hours.
  3. **Treat the cause:** desmopressin in central DI, thiazides/NSAIDs/correcting calcium and potassium in nephrogenic DI, stop lithium, control glucose, manage fever.

### 43.9 Disorders of calcium and magnesium

#### Hypercalcaemia

- **Definition:** **corrected serum calcium >2.6 mmol/L (10.5 mg/dL);** **severe >3.5 mmol/L (14 mg/dL)** or symptomatic.
- **Causes:** **primary hyperparathyroidism** (commonest in outpatients: mildly raised calcium, raised or inappropriately normal PTH) and **malignancy** (commonest in inpatients: PTH-related peptide (squamous cell cancers), osteolytic metastases (breast, lung, myeloma), lymphoma (calcitriol), humoral); vitamin D excess, **granulomatous disease (sarcoidosis, TB),** thiazides, lithium, immobilisation, milk-alkali syndrome, thyrotoxicosis, adrenal insufficiency, vitamin A, familial hypocalciuric hypercalcaemia.
- **Clinical ("bones, stones, abdominal groans, psychic moans"):** polyuria, polydipsia, dehydration, constipation, nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, pancreatitis, kidney stones, confusion, depression, drowsiness, coma, bone pain, hypertension, short QT, bradyarrhythmias, **AKI.**
- **Investigations:** corrected calcium, **PTH,** vitamin D (25-OH and 1,25-OH), phosphate, albumin, U&E, ALP, protein electrophoresis, PTHrP, chest X-ray, imaging for malignancy, ECG.
- **Treatment of severe hypercalcaemia:**
  1. **Aggressive IV rehydration:** 0.9% saline 3–4 L over 24 hours (caution in heart failure and renal failure), restoring urine output.
  2. **Bisphosphonate** after rehydration: **zoledronic acid 4 mg IV over ≥15 minutes** (adjust for renal function) (or pamidronate 60–90 mg); onset 2–4 days, lasts weeks; risk of renal impairment, osteonecrosis of the jaw (rare), hypocalcaemia.
  3. **Calcitonin 4–8 IU/kg SC or IM every 6–12 hours** (rapid onset within hours, tachyphylaxis within 48 hours) for severe cases.
  4. **Denosumab 120 mg SC** for bisphosphonate-refractory or renal failure.
  5. **Glucocorticoids** (prednisolone 40–60 mg or hydrocortisone) for **vitamin D-mediated hypercalcaemia** (lymphoma, sarcoidosis, vitamin D toxicity).
  6. **Loop diuretics** only after volume repletion if fluid overload.
  7. **Haemodialysis** for severe hypercalcaemia with renal failure or cardiac failure.
  8. **Treat the cause:** parathyroidectomy (primary hyperparathyroidism: symptomatic, calcium >0.25 mmol/L above normal, age <50, reduced eGFR, osteoporosis), cinacalcet, oncological treatment; stop thiazides, vitamin D, calcium supplements.

#### Hypocalcaemia

- **Definition:** corrected calcium <2.2 mmol/L (8.5 mg/dL); symptomatic usually <1.9 mmol/L or ionised <1.0.
- **Causes:** **hypoparathyroidism** (post-thyroidectomy/parathyroidectomy, autoimmune, DiGeorge, radiation), **vitamin D deficiency** (malnutrition, malabsorption, lack of sunlight, liver and kidney disease), **CKD** (hyperphosphataemia), **hypomagnesaemia, acute pancreatitis,** rhabdomyolysis, tumour lysis, citrate in transfusions, **pseudohypoparathyroidism,** drugs (bisphosphonates, denosumab, cinacalcet, loop diuretics, phenytoin, foscarnet), sepsis, massive transfusion, hungry bone syndrome, alkalosis (decreases ionised calcium).
- **Clinical:** perioral and acral paraesthesia, muscle cramps, **tetany** (carpopedal spasm, laryngospasm, stridor), **Chvostek sign** (facial twitch on tapping the facial nerve), **Trousseau sign** (carpal spasm with BP cuff inflation), seizures, prolonged **QT interval,** arrhythmias, hypotension, heart failure, irritability, anxiety, cataracts (chronic).
- **Treatment:**
  - **Symptomatic/severe or QT prolongation:** **10 mL of 10% calcium gluconate (2.25 mmol) IV over 10 minutes** in 50–100 mL dextrose or saline with cardiac monitoring, repeat as needed; then **infusion** (e.g. 100 mL of 10% calcium gluconate in 1 L of 5% glucose or saline at 50–100 mL/hour) titrated to calcium; **correct magnesium** (magnesium sulphate); **avoid calcium infusion through the same line as bicarbonate or phosphate;** caution with digoxin.
  - **Maintenance:** oral calcium and **active vitamin D** (alfacalcidol or calcitriol) for hypoparathyroidism; cholecalciferol for vitamin D deficiency.

#### Magnesium

- **Hypomagnesaemia** (<0.7 mmol/L): causes: PPIs, loop and thiazide diuretics, alcohol, diarrhoea, malabsorption, refeeding, diabetes, amphotericin, cisplatin, aminoglycosides, calcineurin inhibitors; features: neuromuscular excitability, tetany, seizures, arrhythmia (**torsades de pointes**), refractory hypokalaemia and hypocalcaemia. **Treatment:** **magnesium sulphate 2 g (8 mmol) IV over 10–20 minutes** for torsades and severe/symptomatic cases (then 4–6 g/24 hours infusion), oral supplements for chronic.
- **Hypermagnesaemia** (>1.0–1.5 mmol/L): AKI/CKD with magnesium-containing laxatives and antacids, **pre-eclampsia treatment (magnesium sulphate),** lithium; features: loss of deep tendon reflexes (first sign), hypotension, bradycardia, respiratory depression, coma, cardiac arrest. **Treatment:** stop magnesium; **calcium gluconate 10–20 mL IV** (antagonises); fluids and loop diuretics; **dialysis** for severe or renal failure.

#### Phosphate

- **Hypophosphataemia** (<0.8 mmol/L; severe <0.3): refeeding syndrome, alcoholism, DKA treatment, respiratory alkalosis, malnutrition, hyperparathyroidism; causes muscle weakness, respiratory failure, haemolysis, rhabdomyolysis, cardiac dysfunction; replace orally or IV (phosphate 9–18 mmol over 6–12 hours) with monitoring.
- **Hyperphosphataemia:** CKD, tumour lysis, rhabdomyolysis, hypoparathyroidism; phosphate binders, dialysis.

### 43.10 Acid–base disorders (focus on metabolic acidosis)

#### Stepwise interpretation

1. **pH:** acidaemia (<7.35) or alkalaemia (>7.45).
2. **PaCO₂** (respiratory) and **HCO₃⁻** (metabolic): which explains the pH?
3. **Compensation:** expected PaCO₂ in metabolic acidosis (**Winter's formula:** **PaCO₂ = 1.5 × HCO₃⁻ + 8 ± 2**); if the measured PaCO₂ is higher: concurrent respiratory acidosis (tiring, inadequate ventilation); lower: respiratory alkalosis (e.g. salicylates, sepsis).
4. **Anion gap (AG)** = Na⁺ − (Cl⁻ + HCO₃⁻) (normal 8–12 mmol/L without K⁺); **correct for albumin** (add 2.5 mmol/L for each 10 g/L fall in albumin below 40).
5. **Delta ratio** (ΔAG/ΔHCO₃⁻): >2 suggests concurrent metabolic alkalosis; <1 suggests concurrent normal-anion-gap acidosis.
6. **Osmolar gap** (measured − calculated osmolality; calculated = 2Na + glucose + urea; normal <10): raised in toxic alcohol ingestion, ketoacidosis, lactic acidosis, renal failure.

#### High anion gap metabolic acidosis (HAGMA)

Mnemonic **GOLDMARK:** **G**lycols (ethylene glycol, propylene glycol), **O**xoproline (pyroglutamic acid: chronic paracetamol use, malnutrition, sepsis), **L**-lactate, **D**-lactate (short bowel), **M**ethanol, **A**spirin (salicylate), **R**enal failure (uraemia), **K**etoacidosis (diabetic, alcoholic, starvation).

- **Lactic acidosis:** **type A** (tissue hypoperfusion: shock, hypoxia, seizures, severe anaemia, CO poisoning, limb ischaemia); **type B** (**metformin-associated lactic acidosis (MALA)** with AKI, **thiamine deficiency,** liver failure, malignancy (lymphoma), linezolid, nucleoside analogues, propofol infusion syndrome, adrenaline, salbutamol, alcohol, inborn errors, cyanide). **Treat the cause;** **bicarbonate** only for pH <7.1–7.2 with AKI (BICAR-ICU suggests benefit in severe acidaemia with AKI); **dialysis** in MALA.
- **Toxic alcohols (methanol, ethylene glycol):** presentation hours after ingestion; **high anion and osmolar gaps** early (the gaps change as the parent compound is metabolised, so a normal osmolar gap does not exclude late poisoning); **methanol:** visual disturbance ("snowstorm"), optic neuropathy, basal ganglia necrosis; **ethylene glycol:** calcium oxalate crystals, AKI, hypocalcaemia, neurological features. **Treatment:** **fomepizole 15 mg/kg IV loading, then 10 mg/kg every 12 hours** (or ethanol), **folate** (methanol) or **thiamine and pyridoxine** (ethylene glycol), **bicarbonate,** **haemodialysis** (severe acidosis, visual symptoms, renal failure, high levels).
- **Salicylate poisoning:** mixed respiratory alkalosis and HAGMA (see 43.13).
- **Alcoholic ketoacidosis:** vomiting, abdominal pain, alcohol binge; ketones (β-hydroxybutyrate), glucose normal or low, high anion gap; **treat with IV saline + dextrose + thiamine** (give thiamine first); potassium, magnesium, phosphate replacement; **no insulin** unless hyperglycaemic.
- **Starvation ketosis, uraemia.**

#### Normal anion gap (hyperchloraemic) metabolic acidosis

GI bicarbonate loss (**diarrhoea,** fistulae, ileostomy, ureteric diversion), **renal tubular acidosis,** **large-volume 0.9% saline infusion,** carbonic anhydrase inhibitors (acetazolamide), early renal failure, hypoaldosteronism; **urine anion gap** (Na + K − Cl): negative suggests GI loss, positive suggests renal tubular acidosis.

#### Metabolic alkalosis

**Vomiting or NG suction, diuretics** (contraction alkalosis), **hypokalaemia, hyperaldosteronism, Cushing,** milk-alkali, bicarbonate administration, post-hypercapnia. **Saline-responsive** (urinary Cl <20 mmol/L: volume contraction) vs **saline-resistant** (urinary Cl >20: mineralocorticoid excess, severe hypokalaemia). Treat: **IV 0.9% saline with potassium,** stop diuretics, acetazolamide, treat the cause.

#### Respiratory acidosis and alkalosis

See Chapter 40 (respiratory acidosis in type 2 failure); **respiratory alkalosis:** anxiety, pain, hypoxia, PE, pneumonia, asthma, sepsis, pregnancy, salicylates, liver failure, high altitude, mechanical ventilation.

# Part C: Endocrine Emergencies

### 43.11 Thyroid storm and myxoedema coma

#### Thyroid storm (thyrotoxic crisis)

- **Life-threatening exacerbation of thyrotoxicosis** (mortality 10–30%) precipitated by **infection, surgery, trauma, iodine load (contrast), parturition, MI, DKA, withdrawal of antithyroid drugs, radioiodine treatment, pulmonary embolism,** or other stressors in a patient with Graves' disease or toxic nodular goitre.
- **Features:** **hyperpyrexia (>38.5–41 °C), marked tachycardia (>140) or atrial fibrillation,** heart failure, agitation, delirium, psychosis, seizures, coma, vomiting, diarrhoea, jaundice, sweating, tremor, exophthalmos, goitre. **Burch–Wartofsky Point Scale** (≥45 highly suggestive); free T4/T3 high (often not markedly different from uncomplicated thyrotoxicosis), TSH suppressed.
- **Management (ICU):**
  1. **Supportive:** oxygen, fluids, **active cooling** (paracetamol; **avoid aspirin** which displaces T4 from binding proteins), glucose, correction of electrolytes.
  2. **Beta-blockade:** **propranolol 60–80 mg orally every 4–6 hours** (or 0.5–1 mg IV slowly, repeated; also blocks peripheral T4→T3 conversion), **esmolol infusion** in ICU; caution in heart failure (use with monitoring; consider digoxin or diltiazem if contraindicated).
  3. **Thionamide:** **propylthiouracil 500–1,000 mg loading dose then 250 mg every 4 hours** (preferred: also blocks peripheral conversion) **or methimazole 20 mg every 4–6 hours** (oral, NG, or rectal).
  4. **Iodine** (potassium iodide solution/Lugol's iodine, 5 drops (about 0.25 mL) every 6 hours) **at least 1 hour after the thionamide** (block hormone release).
  5. **Corticosteroids:** **hydrocortisone 100 mg IV every 8 hours** (reduces conversion, treats relative adrenal insufficiency).
  6. **Cholestyramine** (reduces enterohepatic recirculation), lithium, plasmapheresis, or thyroidectomy in refractory cases.
  7. **Treat precipitant,** anticoagulate AF.

#### Myxoedema coma

- **Severe decompensated hypothyroidism** (mortality 30–60%): older women, winter, precipitated by **infection, cold exposure, drugs (sedatives, amiodarone, lithium), stroke, MI, surgery, non-adherence,** in a patient with long-standing untreated hypothyroidism.
- **Features:** **hypothermia, reduced consciousness (stupor, coma),** bradycardia, hypotension, **hypoventilation with CO₂ retention,** hyponatraemia, hypoglycaemia, dry skin, periorbital oedema, macroglossia, delayed reflexes, pleural and pericardial effusions, seizures. **TSH high (low in central hypothyroidism), free T4 very low.**
- **Management (ICU):** **IV levothyroxine** (loading 200–400 µg, then 1.6 µg/kg/day (50–100 µg daily) IV; lower doses in elderly or cardiac disease), **± IV liothyronine (T3) 5–20 µg** then 2.5–10 µg every 8 hours (cardiac monitoring), **IV hydrocortisone 100 mg every 8 hours** (give before thyroid hormone: coexisting adrenal insufficiency), ventilatory support, **passive rewarming** (avoid active rewarming: vasodilatation and hypotension), cautious fluids (hyponatraemia, hypoglycaemia), treat infection and precipitants.

### 43.12 Adrenal crisis

(Chapter 38.) **Hydrocortisone 100 mg IV immediately, then 200 mg/24 hours,** **IV saline and glucose,** treat precipitant; do not wait for cortisol results. **Prevention:** sick-day rules (double the oral dose for fever, triple for severe illness; IM hydrocortisone 100 mg in vomiting), steroid emergency cards.

# Part D: Poisoning and Environmental Emergencies

### 43.13 The poisoned patient

#### General principles

1. **ABCDE and resuscitate;** most deaths in poisoning result from airway compromise, respiratory depression, hypotension, arrhythmia, seizures, and hyperthermia. **Supportive care is the mainstay.**
2. **Identify the toxin:** history (substance, amount, time, route, intent, co-ingestants), collateral history, **pill bottles,** scene; **toxidromes;** **consult a poison information service** (national poison centre or TOXBASE).
3. **Investigations:** glucose, ECG (QRS, QTc), blood gas (acid–base), U&E, LFTs, CK, osmolality, lactate, **paracetamol and salicylate levels in every intentional overdose,** co-oximetry, drug screens (limited and slow; do not delay treatment), pregnancy test, anion and osmolar gaps.
4. **Decontamination:** **activated charcoal 50 g** (1 g/kg in children) if the patient presents **within 1 hour of ingestion** (up to 4–6 hours for sustained-release or anticholinergic ingestions) of a charcoal-adsorbed toxin and has a **protected airway;** **not** for caustics, hydrocarbons, alcohols, iron, lithium, metals (poorly adsorbed). **Gastric lavage** and **induced emesis** are rarely indicated. **Whole bowel irrigation** for sustained-release preparations, iron, body packers. **Skin and eye decontamination.**
5. **Enhanced elimination:** **multiple-dose activated charcoal** (carbamazepine, theophylline, phenobarbital, dapsone), **urinary alkalinisation** (salicylates, methotrexate), **haemodialysis** (toxic alcohols, salicylates, lithium, theophylline, metformin-associated acidosis, valproate, carbamazepine in severe cases), haemoperfusion.
6. **Antidotes:** see table.
7. **Observation and disposition,** **psychiatric assessment** for self-harm (risk assessment, safety, safeguarding), **never leave a patient who has taken an intentional overdose without assessment.**

### 43.14 Temperature emergencies

#### Hypothermia

- **Definition:** core temperature <35 °C: **mild 32–35 °C** (shivering, tachycardia, confusion), **moderate 28–32 °C** (shivering stops, bradycardia, hypotension, stupor, ECG: **Osborn (J) waves,** arrhythmias), **severe <28 °C** (coma, dilated pupils, VF/asystole, apparent death).
- **Causes:** exposure (cold, wet, wind, immersion), **alcohol and drugs,** elderly, malnutrition, **sepsis,** hypothyroidism, hypoglycaemia, adrenal insufficiency, stroke, burns, spinal injury, intoxication, outdoor sleeping, neonates.
- **Management:** **handle gently** (arrhythmia risk), **remove wet clothing, insulate, prevent further heat loss,** assess ABC (check the pulse for 60 seconds), **continuous ECG monitoring,** **rewarming:**
  - **Passive** (blankets) for mild.
  - **Active external** (forced-air warming, heated blankets) for moderate.
  - **Active internal (core) rewarming** for severe or arrested patients: warmed IV fluids (38–42 °C), warm humidified oxygen, bladder/pleural/peritoneal lavage, **extracorporeal rewarming (ECMO or cardiopulmonary bypass)** for **cardiac arrest.**
  - **Treat the cause** (glucose, thyroid/adrenal hormones, antibiotics), correct electrolytes.
  - **Cardiac arrest in hypothermia:** "**no one is dead until warm and dead**": continue CPR, **limit defibrillation to three shocks until temperature >30 °C,** withhold adrenaline until >30 °C (then at longer intervals), **transfer to an ECMO-capable centre;** **serum potassium >12 mmol/L** in avalanche burial suggests non-survivable.
  - **Afterdrop and rewarming shock:** monitor closely; fluid resuscitation.
  - **Neonatal hypothermia:** skin-to-skin, warming.

#### Heat illness and hyperthermia

- **Heat exhaustion:** weakness, dizziness, nausea, headache, temperature <40 °C, normal mentation; treat with cooling, fluids, rest.
- **Heat stroke:** **core temperature >40 °C with CNS dysfunction** (confusion, seizures, coma): **classic** (older adults, heat waves, no sweating) or **exertional** (athletes, military, sweating, rhabdomyolysis); **multi-organ failure** (AKI, DIC, liver failure, rhabdomyolysis, ARDS, cardiac dysfunction).
- **Treatment: cool first, transport second:** **immediate cold-water immersion (1–15 °C) or ice-water immersion** (most effective; cool to 39 °C within 30 minutes), or evaporative cooling (fan, spray), ice packs to axillae/groin/neck, cold IV fluids; **avoid antipyretics (ineffective);** benzodiazepines for shivering/seizures; fluids (guided; avoid over-hydration with hyponatraemia), monitor CK, electrolytes, renal function, coagulation; ICU.
- **Drug-induced hyperthermia and other syndromes:** **malignant hyperthermia** (after volatile anaesthetics/suxamethonium; **dantrolene 2.5 mg/kg IV,** repeated; cooling; hyperkalaemia treatment; stop triggers), **neuroleptic malignant syndrome** (antipsychotics; rigidity; dantrolene, bromocriptine), **serotonin syndrome, anticholinergic/sympathomimetic toxicity, thyroid storm** (Chapter 21).

### 43.15 Rhabdomyolysis and tumour lysis syndrome

#### Rhabdomyolysis

- **Muscle breakdown with release of myoglobin, CK, potassium, phosphate, uric acid.** **Causes:** **trauma and crush injury,** prolonged immobilisation ("long lie"), **exertion** (marathon, military recruits, intense gym), seizures, **heat stroke,** **drugs** (statins (especially with fibrates, macrolides), cocaine, amphetamines, alcohol, antipsychotics (NMS), propofol), **infections** (influenza, COVID-19), metabolic myopathies, hypokalaemia, hypophosphataemia, ischaemia (compartment syndrome, arterial occlusion), electrical injury.
- **Features:** muscle pain, swelling, weakness, **dark (tea-coloured) urine** (dipstick positive for blood but no RBCs on microscopy), fever; **CK >5 × upper limit (often >5,000–100,000 U/L),** hyperkalaemia, hyperphosphataemia, early hypocalcaemia (later rebound hypercalcaemia), raised uric acid, **AKI** (myoglobin tubular injury), acidosis, **DIC, compartment syndrome.**
- **Management:** **early aggressive IV fluids** (isotonic saline or balanced solution, e.g. 1–1.5 L/hour initially, then 200–300 mL/hour to target **urine output 200–300 mL/hour** (3 mL/kg/hour) in severe rhabdomyolysis; monitor for overload), **correct hyperkalaemia** (as above) **and avoid calcium unless symptomatic hypocalcaemia or life-threatening hyperkalaemia** (calcium deposition), **treat compartment syndrome (fasciotomy),** stop causative drugs, **dialysis** for refractory hyperkalaemia, acidosis, oliguric AKI or volume overload; bicarbonate and mannitol have limited evidence.

#### Tumour lysis syndrome (TLS)

- **Rapid tumour cell death (spontaneous or after chemotherapy, targeted therapy, steroids, radiotherapy)** in **high-turnover haematological malignancies** (Burkitt lymphoma, ALL, AML with high WBC, high-grade lymphomas, bulky disease, high LDH) and occasionally solid tumours; releases potassium, phosphate, nucleic acids (uric acid); **hyperkalaemia, hyperphosphataemia, hypocalcaemia, hyperuricaemia, AKI** (urate and calcium phosphate crystals), arrhythmia, seizures, death.
- **Prevention (high-risk patients):** **vigorous hydration** (3 L/m²/day) before chemotherapy, **rasburicase** (0.2 mg/kg IV daily; check G6PD first; interferes with uric acid measurement unless samples are put on ice) or allopurinol (lower risk), frequent monitoring (every 4–6 hours for 24–72 hours of U&E, K, calcium, phosphate, uric acid, LDH, creatinine).
- **Treatment:** hydration, **rasburicase,** treat hyperkalaemia, hypocalcaemia only if symptomatic, phosphate binders, **early dialysis** for refractory abnormalities or oliguria; ICU/nephrology involvement.

### 43.16 Worked cases

#### Case 1: DKA in type 1 diabetes

*A 19-year-old woman with type 1 diabetes, with 2 days of vomiting and abdominal pain, has RR 32 (deep), HR 124, BP 96/58, dry mucosa, drowsy but rousable. Glucose 31 mmol/L, β-hydroxybutyrate 6.8 mmol/L, venous pH 7.08, bicarbonate 7, K 5.2, Na 130 (corrected 138).*

**Reasoning.** **Moderate–severe DKA,** probably precipitated by a viral illness and insulin reduction.

**Action.** IV 0.9% NaCl (500 mL bolus then 1 L over the next hour), after the first hour start **fixed-rate insulin 0.1 U/kg/hour,** add **40 mmol KCl/L** once K <5.5, add 10% glucose when glucose <14, hourly glucose and ketones, venous gas and K hourly, LMWH, antibiotics if indicated, continue basal insulin, transition to subcutaneous insulin when ketones <0.6 and pH >7.3, diabetes team review.

#### Case 2: Hyperosmolar hyperglycaemic state

*A 78-year-old nursing-home resident, with 5 days of reduced intake and fever, is drowsy and dehydrated. Glucose 52 mmol/L, ketones 0.4, pH 7.34, bicarbonate 20, Na 152, K 4.6, creatinine 190, osmolality 365 mOsm/kg.*

**Reasoning.** **HHS** precipitated by infection.

**Action.** Fluids first (0.9% NaCl 1 L over 1 hour, then adjusted; target 3–6 L positive by 6 hours), delay insulin until glucose stops falling (then 0.05 U/kg/hour), potassium monitoring, **LMWH,** treat infection, monitor osmolality and sodium (avoid fall >10 mmol/L/24 hours), foot and pressure care.

#### Case 3: Severe hyperkalaemia

*A 68-year-old man with CKD stage 4 on ramipril and spironolactone, with a diarrhoeal illness, presents weak. HR 42, BP 100/60. K 7.4 mmol/L, creatinine 520 (baseline 250). ECG: peaked T waves, absent P waves, wide QRS.*

**Reasoning.** **Life-threatening hyperkalaemia from AKI on CKD with RAAS inhibitors.**

**Action.** **Calcium gluconate 10% 30 mL IV over 10 minutes** (repeat if ECG unchanged), **insulin 10 units + 25 g glucose** (hourly glucose), **salbutamol 10–20 mg nebulised,** **sodium zirconium cyclosilicate,** stop ramipril/spironolactone, fluids if hypovolaemic, **urgent nephrology for haemodialysis,** repeat K hourly.

#### Case 4: Acute symptomatic hyponatraemia

*A 28-year-old woman at a music festival, after taking MDMA and drinking 6 L of water, has a headache, vomiting, then a seizure. Na 118 mmol/L; serum osmolality low; urine osmolality 480.*

**Reasoning.** **Acute severe symptomatic hyponatraemia** (water intoxication with ADH excess from MDMA), cerebral oedema.

**Action.** **3% saline 150 mL IV over 20 minutes** (repeat up to twice) to raise Na by about 5 mmol/L; lorazepam for seizure; check Na every 20–60 minutes; **limit correction to ≤10 mmol/L/24 hours;** monitor urine output (water diuresis); ICU.

#### Case 5: Hypercalcaemia of malignancy

*A 64-year-old woman with breast cancer and bone metastases presents with confusion, constipation, polyuria, and vomiting. Corrected calcium 3.9 mmol/L, creatinine 180, PTH suppressed.*

**Reasoning.** **Severe hypercalcaemia of malignancy** with AKI and dehydration.

**Action.** **IV 0.9% saline 3–4 L/24 hours,** then **zoledronic acid 4 mg IV (renal dose adjustment)** or denosumab if eGFR very low, **calcitonin** for rapid effect, monitor Ca/K/Mg, treat cancer, stop calcium and vitamin D supplements.

#### Case 6: Paracetamol overdose

*A 19-year-old woman reports taking 30 × 500 mg paracetamol tablets 6 hours ago after an argument; she has nausea only. Paracetamol level at 6 hours is above the treatment line. ALT normal.*

**Reasoning.** **Significant paracetamol overdose;** early treatment prevents hepatotoxicity.

**Action.** **IV N-acetylcysteine** (3-bag regimen) immediately, recheck INR/ALT/creatinine at the end of the infusion, extend NAC if abnormal, **psychiatric assessment** and safeguarding, liver unit referral if ALF.

#### Case 7: Salicylate toxicity

*A 35-year-old man with a history of depression is drowsy and hyperventilating with tinnitus, vomiting and temperature 38.6 °C. ABG: pH 7.36, PaCO₂ 2.2 kPa, HCO₃⁻ 9. Anion gap 24. Salicylate level 5.8 mmol/L (80 mg/dL).*

**Reasoning.** **Moderate–severe salicylate poisoning** (mixed respiratory alkalosis and metabolic acidosis).

**Action.** IV sodium bicarbonate to alkalinise urine (pH 7.5–8), potassium replacement, glucose, fluids, **haemodialysis** (level near the threshold with altered mental state and acidosis), avoid intubation unless essential (use high minute ventilation), serial levels.

#### Case 8: Thyroid storm

*A 35-year-old woman with untreated Graves' disease develops fever 40 °C, HR 168 (AF), agitation, vomiting and jaundice after pneumonia. TSH <0.01, free T4 very high.*

**Reasoning.** **Thyroid storm** precipitated by infection.

**Action.** ICU; cooling with paracetamol; propranolol (or esmolol); **propylthiouracil 500–1,000 mg load;** iodine 1 hour after thionamide; **hydrocortisone 100 mg q8h;** treat pneumonia; fluids; anticoagulation for AF.

#### Case 9: Heat stroke

*A 22-year-old soldier collapses during a marathon in hot weather, confused and combative, T 41.6 °C, HR 150, BP 100/60; CK 38,000; dark urine.*

**Reasoning.** **Exertional heat stroke with rhabdomyolysis.**

**Action.** **Immediate cold-water immersion** to 39 °C, fluids, benzodiazepines for agitation, monitor K, Ca, creatinine, coagulation, ICU, aggressive hydration for rhabdomyolysis, renal replacement if needed.

#### Case 10: Methanol poisoning

*A 45-year-old man who drank an unknown illicit spirit 12 hours ago has headache, visual blurring ("snowfield"), nausea, and tachypnoea. pH 7.12, HCO₃⁻ 6, anion gap 30, osmolar gap 28.*

**Reasoning.** **Toxic alcohol ingestion (methanol).**

**Action.** **Fomepizole 15 mg/kg IV** (or ethanol), **IV bicarbonate,** folinic/folic acid, **urgent haemodialysis** (visual symptoms, severe acidosis), ophthalmology; call poison centre.

### 43.17 Common pitfalls

- **Not checking capillary glucose and ketones** in unwell patients; stopping insulin in DKA when glucose normalises (continue insulin with dextrose until ketones clear).
- **Giving insulin before potassium is known** in DKA; **under-monitoring potassium.**
- **Treating hyperglycaemia in HHS aggressively with insulin and large saline volumes too fast** (osmolality falls too quickly).
- **Missing euglycaemic DKA** on SGLT2 inhibitors; no ketone testing.
- **Delaying calcium in hyperkalaemia with ECG changes;** forgetting to monitor glucose after insulin–glucose.
- **Correcting hyponatraemia too fast** (osmotic demyelination); fluid-restricting hypovolaemic hyponatraemia; using hypotonic fluids in at-risk patients.
- **Treating hypokalaemia without checking magnesium.**
- **Mistaking pseudohyperkalaemia (haemolysis) for true hyperkalaemia** or treating a "normal ECG" casually.
- **Missing a toxic ingestion** (no paracetamol and salicylate levels); not asking about co-ingestants; delaying NAC.
- **Intubating salicylate-poisoned patients** without maintaining hyperventilation.
- **Giving flumazenil or physostigmine indiscriminately.**
- **Missing high-anion-gap acidosis causes** (toxic alcohols, lactate) and not calculating the osmolar gap.
- **Re-warming hypothermic patients roughly** or abandoning resuscitation early.
- **Treating heat stroke with antipyretics or delaying cooling.**
- **Calcium for asymptomatic hypocalcaemia in rhabdomyolysis.**
- **Forgetting G6PD testing before rasburicase** or hydration before chemotherapy in TLS-risk patients.
- **Missing adrenal crisis, thyroid storm, myxoedema coma** because the presentation is non-specific.

### 43.18 Key points

- **DKA:** fluids, then insulin (fixed rate 0.1 U/kg/h), potassium (hold insulin if K <3.3–3.5), add glucose when <14 mmol/L, treat the precipitant, monitor hourly; bicarbonate only if pH <6.9; **SGLT2 inhibitors cause euglycaemic DKA.**
- **HHS:** fluids first and slow correction; delayed low-dose insulin; thromboprophylaxis.
- **Hypoglycaemia:** oral glucose if able, IV glucose or IM glucagon if not; sulfonylurea hypoglycaemia needs prolonged observation; thiamine with glucose in alcohol misuse.
- **Hyperkalaemia:** calcium (protect), insulin–glucose and salbutamol (shift), binders, diuretics, dialysis (remove); treat the cause.
- **Hyponatraemia:** classify by tonicity and volume; 3% saline for severe symptoms; limit correction to ≤10 mmol/L/24 hours (≤8 in high risk); relower if overshoot.
- **Hypercalcaemia:** saline, bisphosphonate, calcitonin; **hypocalcaemia:** IV calcium gluconate and correct magnesium.
- **Acid–base:** use pH, PaCO₂, HCO₃⁻, anion gap, delta ratio, Winter's formula and osmolar gap; recognise toxic alcohols, salicylates, lactic acidosis.
- **Poisoning:** supportive care first; paracetamol and salicylate levels; antidotes; poison centre; dialysis indications.
- **Temperature:** hypothermia (gentle rewarming, ECMO for arrest), heat stroke (immediate cold immersion), rhabdomyolysis (fluids), TLS (hydration and rasburicase).

### 43.19 Self-assessment

1. State the diagnostic criteria for DKA and the order of key interventions.
   *Answer: Glucose >11 mmol/L (or known diabetes), ketones ≥3 mmol/L (or urine ++), pH <7.3/bicarbonate <15; fluids first, insulin infusion 0.1 U/kg/h once K ≥3.3–3.5, potassium replacement, add glucose at <14 mmol/L, treat the precipitant, monitor hourly.*

2. List the steps in managing severe hyperkalaemia with ECG changes.
   *Answer: Calcium gluconate IV; insulin with glucose; nebulised salbutamol; consider bicarbonate if acidotic; potassium binders, loop diuretic, dialysis; stop precipitating drugs; monitor ECG and K.*

3. What are the correction limits for sodium in hyponatraemia, and the rescue strategy for overcorrection?
   *Answer: ≤10 mmol/L in the first 24 hours (≤8 in high-risk patients); relower with desmopressin and 5% dextrose if exceeded.*

4. Calculate the anion gap and interpret: Na 140, Cl 100, HCO₃⁻ 10.
   *Answer: AG = 140 − (100 + 10) = 30: high anion gap metabolic acidosis (lactate, ketones, toxic alcohols, uraemia, salicylates); calculate osmolar gap and check ketones and lactate.*

5. When is N-acetylcysteine indicated in paracetamol overdose, and how is the decision made?
   *Answer: Based on a paracetamol level ≥4 hours after a single acute ingestion plotted on the treatment nomogram; treat immediately for staggered or unknown-time ingestion, late presentation, or evidence of hepatic injury.*

6. How is heat stroke treated?
   *Answer: Immediate rapid cooling (cold-water immersion or evaporative cooling), fluids, benzodiazepines for agitation, monitoring for rhabdomyolysis, AKI, DIC, and liver failure; antipyretics are ineffective.*
