## Chapter 26. Nausea, Vomiting, Diarrhea and Constipation

### Learning objectives

After studying this chapter you should be able to:

1. Explain the mechanisms of nausea and vomiting, and use the timing, content and associated features to narrow the differential.
2. Recognise the metabolic consequences and dangerous causes of vomiting, and choose antiemetics by mechanism.
3. Classify diarrhoea by duration and mechanism, and distinguish infectious, inflammatory, malabsorptive and functional causes.
4. Assess dehydration, identify red flags in diarrhoea, and decide who needs testing, antibiotics or admission.
5. Evaluate chronic diarrhoea in a stepwise way, including coeliac disease, inflammatory bowel disease, microscopic colitis, pancreatic insufficiency and drug effects.
6. Define constipation, identify secondary causes and alarm features, and manage faecal impaction and overflow.
7. Apply these approaches in older adults, children, pregnant women and patients on multiple medications.

# Part A: Nausea and Vomiting

### 26.1 Mechanisms

**Nausea** is the unpleasant sensation of an urge to vomit; **vomiting (emesis)** is the forceful expulsion of gastric contents through the mouth, mediated by a brainstem reflex; **retching** is the rhythmic respiratory movement without expulsion; **regurgitation** is effortless return of contents into the mouth, without nausea; **rumination** is repeated effortless regurgitation of recently eaten food, which is re-chewed or swallowed.

Vomiting is coordinated by the **vomiting centre** in the medulla, which receives input from:

- the **chemoreceptor trigger zone** (area postrema, outside the blood–brain barrier) responding to drugs, toxins and metabolic disturbances (dopamine D₂, serotonin 5-HT₃, and NK1 receptors);
- the **vestibular system** (histamine H₁ and muscarinic receptors; motion sickness, labyrinthitis);
- the **gastrointestinal tract** via vagal and sympathetic afferents (5-HT₃ receptors; distension, irritation, obstruction);
- **higher cortical centres** (anxiety, smell, sight, anticipation, pain);
- **raised intracranial pressure** and meningeal irritation.

Understanding the receptor targets allows rational selection of antiemetics (26.5).

### 26.2 Causes: a systematic framework

| Category | Examples |
|---|---|
| **Gastrointestinal: luminal and mucosal** | Gastroenteritis (viral, bacterial, toxin), food poisoning, gastritis, peptic ulcer disease, *H. pylori*, pill oesophagitis |
| **Gastrointestinal: obstruction and motility** | Gastric outlet obstruction (peptic stricture, tumour, pyloric stenosis in infants), small bowel obstruction, large bowel obstruction, volvulus, gastroparesis (diabetes, post-surgical, drugs), intestinal pseudo-obstruction, constipation, achalasia, Zenker diverticulum |
| **Hepatobiliary and pancreatic** | Biliary colic, cholecystitis, hepatitis, pancreatitis, hepatic failure |
| **Intra-abdominal inflammation** | Appendicitis, peritonitis, diverticulitis, inflammatory bowel disease |
| **Genitourinary** | Renal colic, pyelonephritis, urinary retention, ovarian torsion |
| **Pregnancy** | Nausea and vomiting of pregnancy, hyperemesis gravidarum, pre-eclampsia/HELLP, acute fatty liver |
| **Central nervous system** | Raised intracranial pressure (tumour, haemorrhage, hydrocephalus), meningitis, migraine, concussion, stroke (cerebellar), vestibular disease (labyrinthitis, Ménière, vestibular neuritis), motion sickness |
| **Metabolic and endocrine** | Diabetic ketoacidosis, uraemia, hypercalcaemia, hyponatraemia, adrenal insufficiency (Addisonian crisis), hyperthyroidism, hyperparathyroidism |
| **Drugs and toxins** | Chemotherapy, opioids, digoxin toxicity, antibiotics (erythromycin, metronidazole), NSAIDs, iron, SSRIs, theophylline, levodopa, oral contraceptives, alcohol, cannabis (cannabinoid hyperemesis), lithium, radiation therapy, poisoning (paracetamol, salicylate, carbon monoxide, mushrooms) |
| **Cardiac and others** | Inferior myocardial infarction, heart failure (hepatic and gut congestion), severe pain |
| **Infections outside the gut** | Sepsis, otitis media, urinary tract infection, pneumonia (children), meningitis, hepatitis |
| **Psychiatric and functional** | Anxiety, depression, bulimia nervosa, anorexia nervosa, cyclic vomiting syndrome, functional dyspepsia, rumination syndrome, psychogenic vomiting |

### 26.3 History and examination

#### Key features of the history

- **Timing and pattern:**
  - **Early morning vomiting:** pregnancy, raised ICP, alcohol, uraemia.
  - **Immediately after meals:** gastric ulcer, gastritis, psychogenic, bulimia.
  - **Vomiting hours after eating, undigested food:** gastric outlet obstruction or gastroparesis.
  - **Episodic, stereotyped attacks with symptom-free intervals:** cyclic vomiting syndrome, abdominal migraine, cannabinoid hyperemesis (relief with hot showers).
  - **Acute onset:** infection, toxin, obstruction, drug.
  - **Chronic:** motility disorder, metabolic disease, cancer, functional.
- **Content:**
  - **Undigested food:** achalasia, oesophageal diverticulum, gastric outlet obstruction.
  - **Bile (green or yellow):** obstruction distal to the ampulla of Vater, or repeated vomiting.
  - **Faeculent (smelly, brown):** distal bowel obstruction, gastrocolic fistula.
  - **Blood:** bright red or coffee-ground (haematemesis, Chapter 27), after retching (Mallory–Weiss tear).
  - **Projectile vomiting** without nausea: pyloric stenosis (infants), raised ICP.
- **Relationship to pain:** relief of pain by vomiting (peptic disease, gastric outlet obstruction); vomiting preceding pain (gastroenteritis) vs pain preceding vomiting (appendicitis, surgical conditions).
- **Associated symptoms:** abdominal pain, diarrhoea, fever, headache, neck stiffness, vertigo or tinnitus, visual change, weight loss, dysphagia, jaundice, polyuria and thirst (DKA, hypercalcaemia), urinary symptoms, possibility of pregnancy, dehydration symptoms.
- **Medications and substances:** new drugs, chemotherapy, opioids, alcohol, cannabis, supplements, accidental ingestion.
- **Exposures:** sick contacts, shared meals, travel, water, reheated rice, seafood.
- **Psychosocial history:** stress, eating disorder, bulimia (dental erosion, parotid swelling, Russell sign (calluses on knuckles)).

#### Examination

- **Vital signs:** tachycardia, hypotension (volume depletion), fever, tachypnoea (acidosis).
- **Hydration assessment:** mucous membranes, skin turgor, capillary refill, JVP, postural BP, urine output (Chapter 10).
- **Abdomen:** distension, tenderness, peritonism, mass, hernias, bowel sounds, succussion splash (gastric outlet obstruction), visible peristalsis.
- **Neurological:** neck stiffness, papilloedema, focal deficits, nystagmus, cerebellar signs.
- **Other:** thyroid, ENT (otitis), chest, hepatomegaly, jaundice, signs of eating disorder.
- **Urine dipstick and pregnancy test** in women of reproductive age.

#### Red flags

- Haematemesis or melaena
- Severe or localised abdominal pain, peritonism, distension, absent flatus (obstruction)
- Persistent or bilious vomiting
- Dehydration, shock, altered consciousness
- Neurological signs, severe headache, neck stiffness (meningitis, raised ICP, SAH)
- Weight loss, dysphagia (cancer)
- Jaundice
- Diabetes with vomiting (DKA)
- Pregnancy with vomiting (hyperemesis, HELLP)
- Immunosuppression, elderly, infants (especially bilious vomiting)

### 26.4 Complications and investigations

#### Complications of vomiting

| Complication | Mechanism |
|---|---|
| **Dehydration, hypovolaemia, AKI** | Fluid loss |
| **Hypochloraemic, hypokalaemic metabolic alkalosis** | Loss of gastric HCl; volume depletion triggers aldosterone-driven renal potassium loss |
| **Hyponatraemia** | Water intake in excess of solute |
| **Aspiration pneumonia** | Reduced consciousness |
| **Mallory–Weiss tear** | Mucosal tear at the gastro-oesophageal junction |
| **Boerhaave syndrome** | Full-thickness oesophageal rupture |
| **Dental erosion, oesophagitis** | Chronic acid exposure |
| **Wernicke encephalopathy** | Thiamine deficiency in prolonged vomiting (hyperemesis, alcohol, bariatric surgery) |
| **Malnutrition, weight loss** | |

#### Investigations (based on context)

- **Bedside:** glucose, urine ketones and pregnancy test, ECG (if risk or electrolyte disturbance).
- **Blood:** U&E, bicarbonate, chloride, glucose, calcium, magnesium, liver tests, lipase, FBC, CRP, thyroid function, cortisol if suspected adrenal insufficiency, toxicology and drug levels (digoxin, lithium, paracetamol, salicylate), blood gas.
- **Imaging:** abdominal X-ray or CT for obstruction; ultrasound for biliary and pelvic disease; CT/MRI head if neurological signs; contrast studies and endoscopy for chronic vomiting.
- **Gastric emptying study** (scintigraphy) for suspected gastroparesis.
- **Upper endoscopy:** gastric outlet obstruction, ulcer, malignancy.
- **Specific:** *H. pylori* testing, coeliac serology.

### 26.5 Treatment: antiemetics by mechanism

First: **treat the cause** and **correct fluid and electrolyte disturbances** (IV fluids with potassium as needed).

| Class | Examples | Site | Best for | Cautions |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Dopamine (D₂) antagonists** | Metoclopramide, domperidone, prochlorperazine, haloperidol, droperidol | CTZ, gut | Drug- and metabolic-induced nausea, gastroparesis (prokinetic effect of metoclopramide and domperidone), post-operative | Extrapyramidal reactions (metoclopramide, prochlorperazine; especially young people), QT prolongation (domperidone, haloperidol, droperidol); metoclopramide contraindicated in complete mechanical obstruction |
| **5-HT₃ antagonists** | Ondansetron, granisetron | Gut, CTZ | Chemotherapy, radiotherapy, post-operative, gastroenteritis (especially in children) | QT prolongation, constipation, headache |
| **Antihistamines (H₁)** | Cyclizine, promethazine, dimenhydrinate | Vomiting centre, vestibular | Vestibular, motion sickness, raised ICP, bowel obstruction, pregnancy (promethazine, doxylamine-pyridoxine) | Sedation, anticholinergic effects; avoid cyclizine in severe heart failure |
| **Anticholinergics** | Hyoscine (scopolamine) | Vestibular, vomiting centre | Motion sickness, bowel obstruction, secretions | Dry mouth, confusion in older people |
| **NK1 antagonists** | Aprepitant, fosaprepitant | CTZ | Chemotherapy-induced (especially highly emetogenic regimens, delayed phase) | Drug interactions (CYP3A4) |
| **Corticosteroids** | Dexamethasone | Unknown | Chemotherapy, raised ICP, bowel obstruction, post-operative | Glucose, insomnia, mood |
| **Benzodiazepines** | Lorazepam | Cortex | Anticipatory nausea, anxiety | Sedation |
| **Cannabinoids** | Nabilone, dronabinol | CNS | Refractory chemotherapy nausea | Sedation, dysphoria |
| **Olanzapine** | | Multiple | Refractory chemotherapy, palliative care | Sedation, weight gain |
| **Ginger, acupressure, vitamin B6** | | | Pregnancy nausea (mild) | |

**Choose according to the cause:**

- **Gastroenteritis (children):** a single dose of ondansetron may facilitate oral rehydration.
- **Gastroparesis:** metoclopramide (short-term; maximum 12 weeks because of tardive dyskinesia), erythromycin, domperidone; dietary changes.
- **Raised ICP or vestibular:** cyclizine, with treatment of the cause.
- **Bowel obstruction:** cyclizine, haloperidol, hyoscine butylbromide, octreotide; avoid prokinetics.
- **Opioid-induced:** haloperidol or metoclopramide.
- **Chemotherapy:** guideline-based combinations.
- **Pregnancy:** see below.

#### Pregnancy: nausea and hyperemesis

- **Nausea and vomiting of pregnancy** affects most pregnant women, typically peaking at 9–10 weeks and resolving by about 16–20 weeks. First-line: dietary changes, ginger, vitamin B6 with or without doxylamine; then antihistamines (promethazine, cyclizine), then prochlorperazine, metoclopramide, ondansetron (consider the risks and benefits).
- **Hyperemesis gravidarum:** persistent severe vomiting with weight loss >5% of pre-pregnancy weight, dehydration, ketonuria and electrolyte disturbance. Admit for IV fluids with potassium, thiamine supplementation (to prevent Wernicke encephalopathy), antiemetics, thromboprophylaxis. Exclude other causes (UTI, thyrotoxicosis, molar pregnancy, multiple pregnancy, gastrointestinal causes).
- **Late pregnancy vomiting:** think pre-eclampsia/HELLP, acute fatty liver, cholestasis, appendicitis, pancreatitis.

---

### 26.6 Cyclic vomiting syndrome and cannabinoid hyperemesis

- **Cyclic vomiting syndrome:** stereotypical episodes of intense nausea and vomiting lasting hours to days, with return to baseline between episodes; association with migraine; triggers (stress, infection, lack of sleep); diagnosis by exclusion and criteria; treatment: abortive (ondansetron, sumatriptan, sedation), prophylaxis (amitriptyline, topiramate).
- **Cannabinoid hyperemesis syndrome:** chronic heavy cannabis use, cyclic vomiting, compulsive hot showers or baths providing temporary relief, resolution only after cessation. Consider in any patient with cyclic vomiting and cannabis use; treat with IV fluids, haloperidol or topical capsaicin, and sustained cessation.

# Part B: Diarrhoea

### 26.7 Definition and classification

**Diarrhoea** is generally defined as **three or more loose or watery stools in 24 hours** (or stool weight >200 g/day). Patients may use the word to mean frequency, urgency, incontinence or loose consistency, so clarify. The **Bristol Stool Chart** (types 6 and 7 are loose) provides a common language.

**By duration:**

- **Acute:** <14 days (most often infectious).
- **Persistent:** 14–30 days.
- **Chronic:** >4 weeks.

**By mechanism:**

| Type | Mechanism | Features | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Osmotic** | Poorly absorbed solutes retain water | Stops with fasting; stool osmotic gap >125 mOsm/kg | Lactose intolerance, laxatives (magnesium, lactulose, PEG), sorbitol/fructose, malabsorption |
| **Secretory** | Active intestinal secretion of fluid and electrolytes | Large volume, watery, persists with fasting, nocturnal; osmotic gap <50 | Cholera, enterotoxigenic *E. coli*, VIPoma, carcinoid, bile acid diarrhoea, microscopic colitis, stimulant laxatives, villous adenoma |
| **Inflammatory / exudative** | Mucosal damage with blood, pus, mucus, protein | Blood, pus, tenesmus, abdominal pain, fever; raised faecal calprotectin | Ulcerative colitis, Crohn's disease, *Shigella*, *Campylobacter*, *Salmonella*, invasive *E. coli*, *C. difficile*, CMV colitis, ischaemic colitis, radiation, cancer |
| **Malabsorptive (steatorrhoea)** | Impaired digestion or absorption of fat and nutrients | Bulky, pale, offensive, floating, greasy stools; weight loss; deficiency states | Coeliac disease, chronic pancreatitis, pancreatic cancer, cystic fibrosis, SIBO, bile acid deficiency, Whipple disease, giardiasis, short bowel |
| **Altered motility** | Rapid transit | Small, urgent stools, relation to meals or stress | IBS-D, hyperthyroidism, diabetic autonomic neuropathy, post-vagotomy, drugs (prokinetics) |
| **Overflow** | Liquid stool passing around impacted faeces | Older adults, constipation history, soiling | Faecal impaction |
| **Factitious** | Covert laxatives | Psychiatric history, healthcare workers | |

Many diarrhoeas have mixed mechanisms.

---

### 26.8 Acute diarrhoea

#### Common causes

| Cause | Features |
|---|---|
| **Viral gastroenteritis** (norovirus, rotavirus, adenovirus, astrovirus) | Sudden onset, vomiting predominant (norovirus), watery stool, low fever, outbreaks (care homes, cruise ships, schools), lasts 1–3 days |
| **Preformed toxin food poisoning** (*Staphylococcus aureus*, *Bacillus cereus*, *Clostridium perfringens*) | Onset within 1–6 hours (*S. aureus*, *B. cereus* emetic type; reheated rice) or 8–16 hours (*C. perfringens*; meat dishes); vomiting and cramps; self-limited; many affected after a shared meal |
| ***Campylobacter*** | Poultry, unpasteurised milk; fever, crampy pain, bloody diarrhoea; the most common bacterial cause in many countries; complications: reactive arthritis, Guillain–Barré syndrome |
| ***Salmonella*** (non-typhoidal) | Eggs, poultry; fever, diarrhoea, can cause bacteraemia in vulnerable patients |
| ***Shigella*** | Dysentery (blood and mucus, tenesmus, fever), person-to-person spread |
| **Shiga toxin-producing *E. coli* (STEC, e.g. O157:H7)** | Undercooked beef, unpasteurised products; bloody diarrhoea without high fever; complication: **haemolytic uraemic syndrome (HUS)** (children and older adults) in the 1st–2nd week: renal failure, haemolytic anaemia, thrombocytopenia |
| **Travellers' diarrhoea** (enterotoxigenic *E. coli* commonly) | Within days of arrival in a developing region; watery; self-limited |
| ***Clostridioides difficile*** | Recent antibiotics (clindamycin, cephalosporins, fluoroquinolones), hospitalisation, PPIs, older age; watery, foul-smelling diarrhoea, cramps, fever, leucocytosis; severe: ileus, megacolon, shock |
| **Parasitic** (*Giardia*, *Cryptosporidium*, *Entamoeba histolytica*) | Water exposure, travel, immunosuppression; prolonged watery diarrhoea (*Giardia*: bloating, greasy stools, weight loss), amoebic dysentery and liver abscess (*E. histolytica*) |
| **Cholera** | Profuse rice-water stool, rapid dehydration; endemic and epidemic areas |
| **Drug-related** | Antibiotics, metformin, laxatives, magnesium antacids, colchicine, chemotherapy, NSAIDs, PPIs |
| **Non-infective** | Ischaemic colitis, first presentation of IBD, diverticulitis, faecal impaction with overflow, appendicitis (diarrhoea in some), hyperthyroidism |

#### Assessment

- **Duration and severity:** number of stools, volume, blood or mucus, nocturnal, fever, vomiting, pain, urine output, weight loss.
- **Dehydration:** thirst, dry mucosa, reduced skin turgor, tachycardia, postural hypotension, low urine output; in children, additional signs (Chapter 59).
- **Exposures:** travel, food, water, antibiotics, healthcare contact, sick contacts, animals, sexual contact (proctitis in MSM), occupational exposure.
- **Risk groups:** young children, older adults, immunosuppressed (HIV, transplant, chemotherapy), pregnancy, food handlers, healthcare workers, care home residents.

#### Red flags (admit, investigate, escalate)

- Blood in the stool, high fever (>38.5 °C), severe abdominal pain
- Signs of dehydration or sepsis
- Age >70 or frail, or immunosuppression
- Recent antibiotics or hospitalisation (*C. difficile*)
- Persistent diarrhoea >7 days
- Neurological symptoms (botulism, Guillain–Barré)
- Passing very little urine, confusion
- Systemic illness (HUS: pallor, bruising, oliguria)
- Public health risk (food handlers, outbreaks)

#### Investigations

Most acute watery diarrhoea in a well, immunocompetent patient needs **no investigation.** Test when:

- **Stool culture/PCR panel:** bloody diarrhoea, fever, severe illness, immunocompromised, persistent, outbreak, food handlers, recent travel (also consider parasites).
- ***C. difficile* testing:** diarrhoea (≥3 loose stools/24 h) in anyone with risk factors (recent antibiotics, hospitalisation). Use a two-step algorithm (glutamate dehydrogenase/PCR plus toxin) according to local policy.
- **FBC, U&E, creatinine, CRP, lactate,** blood cultures if unwell or febrile.
- **Stool ova and parasites, *Giardia* antigen, *Cryptosporidium*:** persistent or travel-related.
- **STEC testing** in bloody diarrhoea: and monitor for HUS (FBC, creatinine, film for schistocytes).
- **Abdominal imaging** for suspected megacolon or complications.
- **Sigmoidoscopy** if the diagnosis is unclear (pseudomembranes in *C. difficile*, colitis).

#### Management

1. **Rehydration:** oral rehydration solution (ORS) is the cornerstone, even in moderate dehydration; IV fluids if severe dehydration, shock, ileus, or intolerance.
2. **Continue feeding;** avoid prolonged starvation.
3. **Antimotility drugs (loperamide)** may be used for non-bloody, afebrile diarrhoea in adults; **avoid** in bloody diarrhoea, fever, suspected STEC, *C. difficile*, and in children.
4. **Antibiotics are not routinely needed.** Indicated for: severe or invasive disease (high fever, bloody diarrhoea, sepsis), travellers' diarrhoea with severe symptoms, shigellosis, cholera, *C. difficile* (oral vancomycin or fidaxomicin; metronidazole in some settings), immunocompromised patients, specific parasites (metronidazole or tinidazole for *Giardia*; nitazoxanide for *Cryptosporidium*). **Avoid antibiotics in suspected STEC** because they may increase the risk of HUS. Follow local resistance data (e.g. *Campylobacter* resistance to fluoroquinolones).
5. **Infection control:** hand hygiene with soap and water (alcohol gel does not kill *C. difficile* spores), isolation, exclusion from work or school for specified periods.
6. **Zinc and ORS for children** in low-resource settings (Chapter 59).
7. **Public health notification** for notifiable diseases.

### 26.9 Chronic diarrhoea

Chronic diarrhoea (>4 weeks) is a diagnostic challenge because it spans many mechanisms. Organise by **stool pattern** and **clinical context.**

#### Questions to ask

- Onset (sudden, gradual), course, nocturnal diarrhoea (organic cause more likely than IBS), blood or mucus, steatorrhoea, urgency or incontinence, relationship to food, abdominal pain, weight loss.
- Drugs and supplements (metformin, PPIs, SSRIs, NSAIDs, magnesium, laxatives, colchicine, sorbitol-containing foods, artificial sweeteners).
- Surgery (cholecystectomy: bile acid diarrhoea; ileal resection; gastric surgery; bowel resection), radiation, travel, immunosuppression, HIV.
- Diet: lactose, fructose, gluten, alcohol, caffeine.
- Family history: IBD, coeliac disease, colorectal cancer.
- Extra-intestinal features: skin rashes (dermatitis herpetiformis, pyoderma gangrenosum), arthritis, uveitis, oral ulcers, mouth sores, flushing (carcinoid), tremor and sweating (thyrotoxicosis), neuropathy (diabetes), eczema.

#### Common causes of chronic diarrhoea by pattern

| Pattern | Likely causes |
|---|---|
| **Watery, non-bloody, large volume** | Microscopic colitis, bile acid diarrhoea, medications, chronic infection (*Giardia*), carbohydrate malabsorption, endocrine tumours (VIPoma, gastrinoma, carcinoid), thyrotoxicosis, laxative abuse, diabetes |
| **Fatty (steatorrhoea)** | Coeliac disease, pancreatic exocrine insufficiency (chronic pancreatitis, cystic fibrosis, pancreatic cancer), SIBO, small bowel mucosal disease (Crohn's, Whipple, lymphoma), Giardia, bile salt deficiency |
| **Inflammatory (blood, mucus, pain, raised calprotectin)** | Ulcerative colitis, Crohn's disease, infectious colitis (including TB, CMV), ischaemic colitis, radiation colitis, colorectal cancer |
| **Functional** | IBS-D, functional diarrhoea |
| **Overflow** | Faecal impaction, especially in older adults, drug-induced constipation |

#### Stepwise evaluation

1. **History and examination (including rectal examination);** look for weight loss, anaemia, clubbing, aphthous ulcers, abdominal masses, perianal disease, thyroid enlargement.
2. **First-line tests:** FBC, ferritin, B12, folate, U&E, calcium, albumin, liver tests, CRP, TSH, **coeliac serology** (tissue transglutaminase IgA with total IgA), HbA1c, **faecal calprotectin** (or lactoferrin) to differentiate inflammatory from functional, stool cultures and parasites, *C. difficile* if risk.
3. **Stool tests:** faecal fat, **faecal elastase** (pancreatic insufficiency; may be falsely low in watery stool), faecal occult blood or FIT, stool osmotic gap (secretory vs osmotic), laxative screen (if factitious suspected).
4. **Endoscopy:**
   - **Colonoscopy with ileal intubation and multiple biopsies** (even if mucosa appears normal) for chronic watery diarrhoea (microscopic colitis), raised calprotectin, alarm features, age >50, anaemia.
   - **Upper endoscopy with duodenal biopsies** for suspected coeliac disease (confirm with biopsies in adults) or malabsorption.
5. **Further tests as indicated:** hydrogen breath tests (lactose, fructose, SIBO), **SeHCAT scan** or serum 7α-hydroxy-4-cholesten-3-one (C4) or empirical trial of a bile acid sequestrant for suspected bile acid diarrhoea (common after cholecystectomy, ileal disease, or in IBS-D), CT or MR enterography for small bowel disease, capsule endoscopy, hormone assays (VIP, gastrin, chromogranin A, 24-hour urinary 5-HIAA), HIV testing.
6. **Trial of therapy** (gluten-free diet only after diagnostic testing, bile acid sequestrant, loperamide, dietary changes) when indicated.

#### Key conditions

- **Coeliac disease:** autoimmune enteropathy triggered by gluten. Presents with diarrhoea, bloating, weight loss, iron deficiency anaemia, osteoporosis, mouth ulcers, dermatitis herpetiformis, infertility, elevated transaminases, fatigue, and is often atypical or silent. Check tTG-IgA while the patient is eating gluten; confirm with duodenal biopsy (and consider HLA-DQ2/DQ8 in doubtful cases). Treat with a lifelong gluten-free diet; monitor for complications (refractory disease, enteropathy-associated T-cell lymphoma).
- **Microscopic colitis:** chronic watery non-bloody diarrhoea, often in older women, associated with NSAIDs, PPIs, SSRIs, and autoimmune disease; endoscopically normal; diagnosis requires biopsy (lymphocytic or collagenous); budesonide is effective.
- **Inflammatory bowel disease:** ulcerative colitis (continuous colonic inflammation from the rectum, bloody diarrhoea) and Crohn's disease (any part, transmural, skip lesions, perianal disease, strictures, fistulae, weight loss). Raised calprotectin; endoscopy, imaging, histology (Chapter 47).
- **Bile acid diarrhoea:** watery, often urgent, post-prandial; after cholecystectomy; responds to cholestyramine, colesevelam.
- **Exocrine pancreatic insufficiency:** steatorrhoea, weight loss, fat-soluble vitamin deficiency; faecal elastase low; treat with pancreatic enzyme replacement.
- **SIBO:** bloating, gas, diarrhoea in patients with anatomical or motility abnormalities; breath test or empirical antibiotic.
- **Lactose intolerance:** bloating, gas, diarrhoea after dairy; improvement with exclusion.
- **IBS-D:** diagnosis by Rome IV criteria after exclusion of mimics (Chapter 25).
- **Chronic infections** (*Giardia*, *Cryptosporidium*, *C. difficile*, CMV, TB, Whipple disease) in susceptible patients.
- **Drug-induced diarrhoea:** the most underdiagnosed cause; review all medications.
- **Neuroendocrine tumours:** carcinoid syndrome (flushing, wheeze, right heart valve disease), VIPoma (watery diarrhoea, hypokalaemia, achlorhydria), medullary thyroid cancer, gastrinoma (Zollinger–Ellison syndrome; refractory ulcers).
- **Endocrine:** hyperthyroidism, Addison disease, diabetes.
- **Colorectal cancer, villous adenoma.**

# Part C: Constipation

### 26.10 Definition and classification

**Constipation** is infrequent, difficult or incomplete evacuation. Patients may mean hard stools, straining, infrequency (<3 per week), a sense of incomplete evacuation, bloating or the need for manual help. **Rome IV criteria for functional constipation** (symptoms present for the last 3 months, onset ≥6 months before diagnosis): two or more of the following in >25% of defecations: straining; lumpy or hard stools (Bristol 1–2); a sense of incomplete evacuation; a sense of anorectal obstruction; manual manoeuvres to facilitate defecation; fewer than three spontaneous bowel movements per week; loose stools rarely present without laxatives; insufficient criteria for IBS-C.

#### Classification

- **Primary (functional) constipation:**
  - **Normal-transit constipation** (most common; often related to low fibre, fluid, activity, or stool perception).
  - **Slow-transit constipation** (colonic inertia; young women).
  - **Defecatory disorders** (dyssynergic defecation: paradoxical contraction of the pelvic floor and anal sphincter; rectocele; rectal intussusception; descending perineum).
- **Secondary constipation:** drugs, metabolic, neurological, structural, and others (below).

---

### 26.11 Secondary causes

| Category | Examples |
|---|---|
| **Drugs** | **Opioids** (most common), anticholinergics (tricyclics, antipsychotics, antihistamines, oxybutynin), calcium channel blockers (verapamil), iron, calcium and aluminium antacids, ondansetron, diuretics, anticonvulsants, clonidine, vinca alkaloids, 5-HT₃ antagonists, bile acid sequestrants, laxative overuse |
| **Metabolic and endocrine** | Hypothyroidism, hypercalcaemia, hypokalaemia, diabetes, uraemia, porphyria, pregnancy |
| **Neurological** | Parkinson disease, multiple sclerosis, stroke, spinal cord injury, autonomic neuropathy, Hirschsprung disease, Chagas disease, dementia |
| **Structural** | **Colorectal cancer**, strictures (diverticular, ischaemic, anastomotic), extrinsic compression, rectocele, anal fissure or stenosis, volvulus |
| **Psychological** | Depression, eating disorders, anxiety, abuse history |
| **Systemic** | Scleroderma, amyloidosis, dermatomyositis |
| **Lifestyle** | Low fibre and fluid intake, immobility, ignoring the urge to defecate |

#### Faecal impaction and overflow

In older, immobile or neurologically impaired patients, hard stool accumulates in the rectum. Consequences: **overflow diarrhoea** (liquid stool seeping around the mass), faecal incontinence, urinary retention, abdominal pain, confusion, delirium, and rarely stercoral ulceration with perforation. Diagnosis: **digital rectal examination** (hard stool in the rectum) and sometimes abdominal X-ray. Treatment: rectal measures (glycerin or bisacodyl suppositories, mini-enemas, manual evacuation) followed by oral laxatives (high-dose macrogol), then maintenance.

---

### 26.12 Assessment of constipation

#### History

- Duration; stool frequency and form (Bristol); straining; incomplete evacuation; need for manual manoeuvres; urge and ability to defecate; blood or mucus; abdominal pain and bloating; nocturnal symptoms; weight loss; recent change in bowel habit; previous laxative use and response.
- **Drugs and supplements**, diet (fibre, fluid), activity, mobility, toilet access and privacy, psychological factors, pregnancy.
- Past history: surgery, obstetric trauma, neurological and endocrine disorders.
- Family history of colorectal cancer or IBD.

#### Red flags

- **New-onset or changed bowel habit after age 50** (cancer risk)
- Rectal bleeding or blood in the stool, anaemia, iron deficiency
- Unintentional weight loss
- Abdominal mass
- Family history of colorectal cancer or IBD
- Progressive symptoms, nocturnal symptoms, pain
- Vomiting, distension and absolute constipation (obstruction)
- Neurological symptoms (cord compression, cauda equina)
- Thin, pencil-like stool (rectal lesion)

#### Examination

- **Abdomen:** distension, faecal loading (palpable mass in left iliac fossa), masses, tenderness.
- **Perianal inspection** (fissure, haemorrhoids, prolapse, perineal descent, scars, soiling).
- **Digital rectal examination** (stool in the rectum, anal tone, squeeze, push effort (paradoxical contraction suggests dyssynergia), masses, tenderness, prostate).
- **Neurological examination** if indicated.
- Thyroid, signs of systemic disease.

#### Investigations

- **Not needed** in most young patients without red flags: start with lifestyle changes and laxative therapy.
- **Basic blood tests** if secondary causes are suspected: FBC, glucose, TSH, calcium, U&E.
- **Colonoscopy or CT colonography** for red flags and age-related risk.
- **Specialist tests for refractory constipation:** colonic transit study (radio-opaque markers), anorectal manometry, balloon expulsion test, defecography (barium or MR), consider biopsy for Hirschsprung (rectal) or neuropathy.

---

### 26.13 Management of constipation

1. **Treat or modify causes:** review and reduce constipating drugs; correct hypothyroidism, hypercalcaemia, hypokalaemia.
2. **Lifestyle:** adequate fluids; gradual increase in dietary fibre (soluble fibre, such as ispaghula/psyllium, is better tolerated), physical activity, regular toilet routine after meals (gastrocolic reflex), proper posture (feet raised on a stool), unhurried and private toilet access.
3. **Laxatives (stepwise):**
   - **Bulk-forming:** ispaghula, methylcellulose (need adequate fluid; may worsen bloating or obstruction).
   - **Osmotic:** **macrogol (polyethylene glycol)** (first-line in many guidelines), lactulose, magnesium salts (avoid in renal impairment).
   - **Stimulants:** senna, bisacodyl, sodium picosulfate (for short-term, or regular use when osmotic agents alone are inadequate).
   - **Stool softeners:** docusate (limited evidence).
   - **Secretagogues:** linaclotide, lubiprostone (IBS-C and chronic constipation).
   - **Prokinetic 5-HT₄ agonist:** prucalopride (chronic constipation unresponsive to laxatives in women).
   - **Opioid-induced constipation:** prophylactic stimulant ± osmotic laxative; peripherally acting μ-opioid receptor antagonists (naloxegol, methylnaltrexone, naldemedine).
4. **Rectal measures:** suppositories (glycerin, bisacodyl), enemas, manual evacuation in impaction.
5. **Biofeedback** for dyssynergic defecation (effective; first-line).
6. **Surgery** (rare): for selected patients with slow-transit constipation or structural abnormalities.
7. **Avoid long-term reliance on stimulants** without review; address expectations.

---

### 26.14 Special populations

- **Older adults:** high prevalence of constipation, polypharmacy and reduced mobility; impaction, overflow, delirium. Diarrhoea is frequently overflow; *C. difficile* is more common and severe; dehydration develops quickly; drugs (including laxatives) cause electrolyte disturbance.
- **Children:** see Chapter 59. Vomiting with bile requires urgent surgical assessment; diarrhoea with dehydration is life-threatening in infants; constipation with withholding is common, and Hirschsprung disease must be considered if delayed passage of meconium (>48 hours) or severe constipation from birth.
- **Pregnancy:** nausea and vomiting, constipation (progesterone, iron), diarrhoea (infection).
- **Cancer patients:** chemotherapy-induced nausea and vomiting (anticipatory, acute, delayed), opioid-induced constipation, malignant bowel obstruction, radiation-induced diarrhoea, immune checkpoint inhibitor colitis (presenting with diarrhoea; treat with steroids), neutropenic enterocolitis.
- **Palliative care:** prophylactic antiemetics and laxatives with opioids; bowel obstruction management.
- **HIV and immunosuppressed:** chronic diarrhoea from *Cryptosporidium*, *Microsporidia*, CMV, MAC, *Isospora*.

### 26.15 Worked cases

#### Case 1: Vomiting and metabolic alkalosis

*A 65-year-old man has had 3 months of early satiety, postprandial fullness, weight loss of 8 kg, and for 2 weeks vomiting undigested food eaten 8–12 hours earlier, without bile. He has epigastric distension with a succussion splash. Blood tests: Na 134, K 2.8 mmol/L, Cl 80, HCO₃ 38, creatinine 160 µmol/L.*

**Reasoning.** Vomiting of old, undigested, non-bilious food, succussion splash and weight loss: **gastric outlet obstruction** (peptic stricture or gastric/pancreatic malignancy). The biochemistry shows **hypochloraemic, hypokalaemic metabolic alkalosis** with pre-renal AKI.

**Action.** IV 0.9% saline with potassium replacement, correct dehydration, nasogastric tube decompression, PPI, thiamine, upper endoscopy with biopsy, CT staging; endoscopic or surgical treatment depending on the cause. Avoid prokinetics in complete obstruction.

#### Case 2: Hyperemesis gravidarum

*A 26-year-old woman at 9 weeks' gestation has had 3 weeks of severe vomiting, cannot keep fluids down, has lost 5 kg (8% of her weight), and has ketonuria. HR 110, BP 98/60. K 3.0. She is dizzy and drowsy.*

**Reasoning.** Persistent vomiting with weight loss >5%, dehydration and ketonuria: **hyperemesis gravidarum.** Exclude UTI, thyroid disease, molar pregnancy, multiple pregnancy, and other causes. Watch for **Wernicke encephalopathy.**

**Action.** Admit; IV fluids with potassium (avoid dextrose-containing fluids before giving thiamine), thiamine, antiemetic ladder (cyclizine, ondansetron, metoclopramide as appropriate), thromboprophylaxis, ultrasound, nutritional support if prolonged.

#### Case 3: Bloody diarrhoea and a falling platelet count

*A 6-year-old child ate an undercooked burger at a barbecue. Four days later: severe abdominal cramps and bloody diarrhoea without high fever. On day 7 she is pale, lethargic, and has not passed urine for 12 hours. Hb 78 g/L, platelets 45 × 10⁹/L, creatinine 190 µmol/L, film shows fragmented red cells.*

**Reasoning.** Bloody diarrhoea after undercooked beef, then pallor, oliguria, anaemia, thrombocytopenia and AKI: **haemolytic uraemic syndrome** following **STEC infection.**

**Action.** Admit to paediatric nephrology; careful isotonic fluid management, monitor electrolytes, blood pressure, and neurological status; transfuse if needed; dialysis if indicated; **no antibiotics or antimotility agents**; report to public health.

#### Case 4: *C. difficile*

*A 77-year-old woman, discharged after treatment for pneumonia with co-amoxiclav and clarithromycin, has had 6 days of watery foul-smelling diarrhoea, now 10 times a day, lower abdominal pain, fever 38.3 °C, and WBC 19 × 10⁹/L, creatinine 130 µmol/L (baseline 80). Toxin and PCR positive for C. difficile.*

**Reasoning.** Antibiotic exposure, hospitalisation and age: ***C. difficile* infection**, moderate to severe (raised WBC and creatinine).

**Action.** Stop the offending antibiotic if possible, avoid antimotility agents and unnecessary PPIs, start oral vancomycin (or fidaxomicin), isolate, enteric precautions, fluid resuscitation. Monitor for fulminant disease (hypotension, ileus, megacolon; add IV metronidazole and surgical review). Discuss recurrence prevention (fidaxomicin, bezlotoxumab, faecal microbiota therapy).

#### Case 5: Chronic diarrhoea and iron deficiency

*A 34-year-old woman has had 2 years of bloating, loose stools, and fatigue; she has iron-deficiency anaemia unresponsive to oral iron, mouth ulcers, and osteopenia. Weight is down 4 kg. Her sister has type 1 diabetes. tTG-IgA is strongly positive; duodenal biopsy shows villous atrophy.*

**Reasoning.** **Coeliac disease** (a common cause of unexplained iron deficiency and malabsorption, frequently missed for years).

**Action.** Dietitian referral and strict gluten-free diet, correct deficiencies (iron, folate, B12, vitamin D, calcium), bone density assessment, vaccinations (pneumococcal for hyposplenism), screening of first-degree relatives, follow-up serology.

#### Case 6: Chronic watery diarrhoea in an older woman

*A 68-year-old woman has had 6 months of watery, non-bloody diarrhoea, 6–8 times a day, including nocturnal, without weight loss or blood. She takes lansoprazole and an NSAID. Calprotectin is mildly raised; colonoscopy appears normal; random biopsies show a thickened subepithelial collagen band.*

**Reasoning.** Chronic watery diarrhoea in a woman over 60, on a PPI and NSAID, with normal endoscopic appearance: **collagenous colitis (microscopic colitis).**

**Action.** Stop NSAIDs and review PPI; start budesonide (oral) with high response; consider maintenance in relapsing disease; exclude coeliac disease and bile acid diarrhoea if the response is incomplete.

#### Case 7: Constipation with a red flag

*A 63-year-old man reports 4 months of worsening constipation alternating with small loose stools, a sensation of incomplete evacuation, bright red blood on the stool, and 5 kg weight loss. Hb is 98 g/L, ferritin low. Rectal examination reveals a firm mass in the lower rectum.*

**Reasoning.** New bowel habit change, bleeding, weight loss and iron-deficiency anaemia over age 50 = **colorectal cancer** until proven otherwise; the rectal mass indicates a rectal primary.

**Action.** Urgent colonoscopy and biopsy, CT staging, MRI pelvis, colorectal MDT; do not prescribe laxatives as a substitute for investigation.

#### Case 8: Opioid-induced constipation and overflow

*A 82-year-old woman on regular codeine for osteoarthritis presents with 3 days of "diarrhoea," lower abdominal discomfort, confusion, and urinary retention. Examination: distended abdomen, faecal masses in the left iliac fossa; digital rectal examination: rectum full of hard stool with watery stool leaking around it.*

**Reasoning.** **Faecal impaction with overflow** (opioid-induced), causing delirium and urinary retention.

**Action.** Treat the impaction (suppositories, enemas, manual evacuation if needed), then macrogol-based regimen, addition of a stimulant laxative, review of opioid dose and non-opioid analgesia, address mobility and toileting; check for infection and electrolyte problems. Avoid antidiarrhoeal drugs.

### 26.16 Common pitfalls

- **Treating symptoms** (antiemetics, antidiarrhoeals, laxatives) before looking for the cause.
- **Using metoclopramide in bowel obstruction.**
- **Missing DKA, adrenal crisis, raised ICP, MI or pregnancy** in a vomiting patient.
- **Ignoring potassium and acid–base disturbance** in persistent vomiting.
- **Giving glucose before thiamine** in prolonged vomiting or alcohol misuse.
- **Prescribing antibiotics for uncomplicated gastroenteritis** or in suspected STEC.
- **Giving loperamide in bloody diarrhoea, fever or** *C. difficile.*
- **Missing coeliac disease,** microscopic colitis, bile acid diarrhoea and drug-induced diarrhoea in chronic diarrhoea.
- **Labelling IBS** in the presence of red flags or without simple tests.
- **Mistaking overflow diarrhoea for infectious diarrhoea** in older patients; failing to do a rectal examination.
- **Under-treating constipation in patients on opioids.**
- **Attributing new constipation in an older patient to age** without considering colorectal cancer.
- **Forgetting infection control** (hand hygiene with soap, isolation, notification).

---

### 26.17 Key points

- Nausea and vomiting have many causes beyond the gut: metabolic, neurological, drug-related, pregnancy and cardiac; use timing, content and associated features to narrow the list.
- Vomiting can cause hypochloraemic hypokalaemic alkalosis and Wernicke encephalopathy; correct fluids and electrolytes and give thiamine when indicated.
- Select antiemetics by mechanism; avoid prokinetics in obstruction.
- Acute diarrhoea is usually self-limited; rehydrate; test and treat selectively; avoid antimotility drugs and antibiotics in bloody diarrhoea, suspected STEC, and *C. difficile.*
- Think of *C. difficile* after antibiotics or hospitalisation.
- Chronic diarrhoea: classify by stool pattern, check calprotectin and coeliac serology, consider microscopic colitis, bile acid diarrhoea, pancreatic insufficiency, SIBO and drugs; colonoscopy with biopsies when watery diarrhoea is unexplained.
- Constipation: look for drugs, metabolic, neurological and structural causes; red flags for cancer; digital rectal examination; stepwise laxatives; treat impaction; do not miss overflow.

### 26.18 Self-assessment

1. What acid–base and electrolyte disturbance is typical of prolonged vomiting and why?
   *Answer: Hypochloraemic, hypokalaemic metabolic alkalosis; loss of gastric HCl raises bicarbonate, and volume depletion with secondary hyperaldosteronism promotes renal potassium loss.*

2. List four situations where antibiotics for diarrhoea are indicated and one in which they should be avoided.
   *Answer: Severe invasive disease/sepsis, shigellosis, cholera, *C. difficile*, immunocompromised patients, specific parasites; avoid in suspected STEC (HUS risk).*

3. Describe the stool characteristics and two causes of steatorrhoea.
   *Answer: Pale, bulky, greasy, offensive, floating stools; coeliac disease and pancreatic exocrine insufficiency (also SIBO, Crohn's, cystic fibrosis).*

4. Give the Rome IV criteria for functional constipation (in brief).
   *Answer: Two or more of: straining, lumpy/hard stool, incomplete evacuation, anorectal obstruction sensation, manual manoeuvres, <3 spontaneous bowel movements/week, in >25% of defecations, over ≥3 months with onset ≥6 months earlier, loose stools rarely without laxatives.*

5. Why is a rectal examination essential in an older patient with "diarrhoea"?
   *Answer: To detect faecal impaction with overflow, which looks like diarrhoea but requires clearing the impaction, not antidiarrhoeals.*

6. Name three causes of chronic watery diarrhoea with a normal colonic appearance on colonoscopy.
   *Answer: Microscopic colitis, bile acid diarrhoea, drug-induced diarrhoea (also coeliac disease, endocrine causes, laxative abuse).*
