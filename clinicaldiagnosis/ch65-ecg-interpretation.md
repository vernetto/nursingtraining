# Chapter 65. ECG Interpretation

## Learning objectives

After studying this chapter you should be able to:

1. Explain how the 12-lead ECG is generated, what each lead "sees", and how to check technical quality (calibration, lead placement, artefact).
2. Apply a systematic, repeatable method to every ECG: rate, rhythm, axis, intervals, P wave, QRS, ST segment, T wave, U wave, comparison with old tracings.
3. Diagnose sinus rhythms, atrial and junctional arrhythmias, ventricular arrhythmias and AV blocks, and distinguish narrow-complex from wide-complex tachycardia.
4. Recognise bundle branch blocks, fascicular blocks, pre-excitation and ventricular hypertrophy.
5. Identify the ECG of acute coronary syndromes: STEMI, its equivalents (posterior MI, de Winter, Wellens, left main, Sgarbossa criteria in LBBB), and NSTEMI mimics.
6. Recognise the ECG signature of pericarditis, pulmonary embolism, hyperkalaemia and other electrolyte disorders, hypothermia, Brugada, long QT, and drug effects.
7. Decide urgency: which ECG findings require immediate action (defibrillation, pacing, reperfusion, calcium) and which need outpatient follow-up.
8. Integrate the ECG with the clinical context and avoid over-reading automated interpretations.

> **Note on thresholds.** Voltage, interval and ST-elevation criteria follow the Fourth Universal Definition of MI and AHA/ACC/ESC consensus statements at the time of writing. Machine interpretation is a prompt, not a diagnosis.

---

## 1. Basics: what the ECG records

### 1.1 Electrophysiology in brief

The ECG records the **net electrical vector** of cardiac depolarisation and repolarisation, projected onto each lead axis. A wave of depolarisation moving **towards** a positive electrode produces an upward deflection; moving **away**, a downward deflection.

Conduction sequence: sinoatrial (SA) node (60–100/min) → atrial myocardium (**P wave**) → AV node (delay: **PR segment**) → His bundle → right and left bundle branches (left splits into anterior and posterior fascicles) → Purkinje fibres → ventricular myocardium (**QRS complex**) → repolarisation (**ST segment, T wave**, ± U wave). Subsidiary pacemakers: AV junction (40–60/min), ventricle (20–40/min).

### 1.2 The leads

| Group | Leads | Region viewed | Coronary artery (typical) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Inferior | II, III, aVF | Inferior wall | RCA (80–90%), LCx |
| Lateral | I, aVL, V5, V6 | Lateral wall | LCx, LAD diagonals |
| Septal | V1, V2 | Septum | LAD |
| Anterior | V3, V4 | Anterior wall | LAD |
| Right ventricle | V1, V4R | RV | Proximal RCA |
| Posterior | V7–V9 (reciprocal ST depression V1–V3) | Posterior wall | RCA, LCx |

Limb leads are bipolar (I, II, III) and augmented unipolar (aVR, aVL, aVF) in the frontal plane; V1–V6 are in the horizontal plane. **aVR** looks at the right shoulder and the cavity of the left ventricle: normally negative QRS, P and T waves.

### 1.3 Technical quality

- Standard calibration: **25 mm/s** (1 small square 0.04 s, 1 large square 0.2 s) and **10 mm/mV**.
- **Limb lead reversal** (right arm/left arm): inverted P-QRS-T in lead I with positive aVR; common error mistaken for dextrocardia (in true dextrocardia, R-wave progression is reversed across the chest leads).
- **Misplaced chest electrodes** (V1/V2 too high: pseudo-RBBB pattern, loss of R progression).
- **Artefacts**: tremor (mimics AF/flutter), loose lead (baseline wander), 50/60 Hz interference, movement; Parkinson tremor mimicking flutter.
- Check patient identity, date, time, and compare with previous ECGs.

## 2. A systematic method

Always read in the same order, even when the abnormality seems obvious; otherwise a second problem is missed.

1. **Rate**: 300 ÷ number of large squares between R-R; or 1500 ÷ small squares; for irregular rhythm count QRS complexes in a 6-second strip × 10.
2. **Rhythm**: Is there a P wave before every QRS? Is the P-QRS relationship constant? Regular or irregular? Narrow (< 0.12 s = 3 small squares) or wide QRS?
3. **Axis**: normal −30° to +90°. Quadrant method: lead I and aVF both positive = normal; I positive, aVF negative = left axis deviation (LAD; check lead II: if positive, within normal up to −30°); I negative, aVF positive = right axis deviation (RAD); both negative = extreme (northwest) axis.
4. **Intervals**: PR 0.12–0.20 s (3–5 small squares); QRS < 0.12 s; QT: measure and correct (QTc, below).
5. **P waves**: morphology, duration (< 0.12 s), amplitude (< 2.5 mm), P mitrale (broad notched in II), P pulmonale (tall peaked in II), biphasic in V1 (left atrial enlargement).
6. **QRS**: width, voltage (hypertrophy criteria, low voltage), Q waves (pathological: ≥ 0.04 s wide or > 25% of R wave height, or any Q in V1–V3; small septal q in I, aVL, V5–V6 normal), R-wave progression (transition at V3–V4), delta wave.
7. **ST segment**: elevation, depression, morphology (concave "saddle", convex "tombstone", downsloping, horizontal), reciprocal change; J point.
8. **T waves**: inverted (normal in aVR, V1, III; V2–V3 in some), hyperacute, peaked, flattened.
9. **QT/U waves**: QTc; U waves (prominent in hypokalaemia).
10. **Compare** with a previous ECG; **correlate** with the patient.

**QTc**: Bazett (QT/√RR) overcorrects at high heart rates and undercorrects at low; Fridericia (QT/∛RR) is preferred at HR > 90 or < 60. Prolonged: men > 450 ms, women > 460 ms (some use 470); > 500 ms or an increase > 60 ms carries high torsades risk. In wide QRS, subtract the QRS excess or use JT interval.

## 3. Rhythm disorders

### 3.1 Sinus rhythms

- **Normal sinus rhythm**: P before each QRS, upright in I and II, inverted in aVR, rate 60–100.
- **Sinus bradycardia** (< 60): athletes, sleep, hypothyroidism, raised ICP, drugs (β-blockers, digoxin, verapamil/diltiazem, amiodarone), inferior MI, sick sinus.
- **Sinus tachycardia** (> 100): pain, fever, hypovolaemia, anaemia, hypoxia, PE, thyrotoxicosis, anxiety, drugs; **treat the cause** (rarely above ~150 in adults unless extreme; a fixed rate of 150 suggests flutter with 2:1 block).
- **Sinus arrhythmia**: rate varies with breathing; normal in the young.
- **Sick sinus syndrome**: bradycardia, sinus pauses, sinoatrial exit block, tachy-brady syndrome; permanent pacing if symptomatic.

### 3.2 Atrial rhythms

| Rhythm | Key ECG features | Clinical points |
|---|---|---|
| **Premature atrial complex (PAC)** | Early, abnormal P wave, narrow QRS (or aberrant), non-compensatory pause | Benign; triggers: caffeine, stress |
| **Atrial fibrillation (AF)** | Irregularly irregular, no distinct P waves, fibrillatory baseline | Commonest sustained arrhythmia; stroke risk (CHA₂DS₂-VASc, chapter 70); rate vs rhythm control; AF with fast rates + wide QRS (pre-excitation) is dangerous |
| **Atrial flutter** | Sawtooth flutter waves (~300/min) best seen in II, III, aVF, V1; ventricular rate often 150 (2:1 block) or 75 (4:1) | Typical (cavotricuspid isthmus-dependent, counter-clockwise: negative sawtooth in inferior leads); ablation highly effective; anticoagulate like AF |
| **Atrial tachycardia** | Regular narrow tachycardia with abnormal P waves before QRS (rate 130–250), may have AV block (digoxin toxicity) | Focal or multifocal |
| **Multifocal atrial tachycardia (MAT)** | ≥ 3 P-wave morphologies, irregular, rate > 100 | COPD, hypoxia, theophylline; treat the underlying lung disease and hypoxia |
| **AVNRT / AVRT (SVT)** | Regular narrow tachycardia 150–250, no visible P or retrograde P after QRS (pseudo-r' in V1 in AVNRT; RP interval in AVRT) | Abrupt onset/offset; vagal manoeuvres, adenosine 6 → 12 mg rapid IV push |

### 3.3 Wide-complex tachycardia (WCT): assume VT until proven otherwise

QRS ≥ 0.12 s at rate > 100. Causes: **ventricular tachycardia** (VT, ~80% in unselected patients; > 90% if structural heart disease or prior MI), SVT with aberrancy or bundle branch block, pre-excited tachycardia (antidromic AVRT, AF with accessory pathway), hyperkalaemia, drug toxicity (flecainide, TCA), paced rhythm.

**Features favouring VT**: AV dissociation (P waves independent of QRS; capture and fusion beats), QRS > 0.14 s (RBBB-like) or > 0.16 s (LBBB-like), extreme axis (northwest), concordance (all chest leads positive or negative), RS interval > 100 ms in a precordial lead, Brugada algorithm, lead aVR criteria (initial R wave or Vi/Vt ≤ 1), history of MI or structural heart disease. Clinical clues: history of coronary disease or cardiomyopathy strongly supports VT. **Haemodynamic stability does not exclude VT.**

**Management**: unstable (hypotension, shock, chest pain, heart failure, altered consciousness) → **synchronised DC cardioversion** (120–200 J biphasic); pulseless → defibrillation and ACLS. Stable: 12-lead ECG, IV access, correct K/Mg, consider IV amiodarone 300 mg over 10–60 minutes, procainamide, lidocaine; avoid verapamil/diltiazem in undiagnosed WCT (can cause collapse in VT or pre-excited AF). Adenosine only if regular monomorphic WCT is probably SVT with aberrancy and no pre-excitation.

### 3.4 Other ventricular rhythms

- **Premature ventricular complexes (PVCs)**: wide, bizarre QRS, no preceding P, compensatory pause; bigeminy, couplets; frequent PVCs (> 10–15% burden) can cause cardiomyopathy; high-risk if with structural disease or R-on-T.
- **Monomorphic VT**: scar-related (post-MI); **polymorphic VT**: ischaemia, electrolytes, drugs; **torsades de pointes**: polymorphic VT with twisting QRS around the baseline in prolonged QT; treat with IV magnesium 2 g, correct K, stop QT-prolonging drugs, overdrive pacing/isoprenaline for bradycardia-dependent; **ventricular fibrillation**: chaotic, no QRS → immediate defibrillation. **Accelerated idioventricular rhythm** (rate 60–110 after reperfusion): usually benign.
- **Idioventricular escape rhythm**: wide, regular, 20–40/min in complete heart block.

### 3.5 AV conduction disease

| Block | ECG | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **First-degree** | PR > 0.20 s, every P conducted | Usually benign; drugs, vagal tone, inferior MI, Lyme, myocarditis |
| **Second-degree Mobitz I (Wenckebach)** | Progressive PR lengthening until a dropped QRS; grouped beating, shortening R-R | AV nodal; often benign (inferior MI, drugs, young athletes); atropine if symptomatic |
| **Second-degree Mobitz II** | Constant PR, sudden dropped QRS, usually wide QRS | Infranodal; risk of progression to complete block → pacing |
| **2:1 block** | Every other P conducted | Cannot distinguish type I/II: wide QRS and exercise response help |
| **High-grade (advanced) AV block** | ≥ 2 consecutive non-conducted P waves | Pacing |
| **Third-degree (complete)** | AV dissociation; atrial rate > ventricular rate; narrow escape (junctional, 40–60) or wide (ventricular, 20–40) | Causes: inferior MI (often transient), anterior MI (poor prognosis), fibrosis, Lyme, sarcoid, congenital, drugs (digoxin, β-blockers, CCB), amyloid; atropine, transcutaneous or transvenous pacing, permanent pacemaker |

### 3.6 Pre-excitation

**Wolff–Parkinson–White (WPW)**: short PR (< 0.12 s), delta wave (slurred upstroke), wide QRS, secondary ST–T changes. Risk: AF with rapid conduction through the accessory pathway (irregular wide-complex tachycardia up to 250/min) → degenerates to VF. **Avoid AV nodal blockers (adenosine, verapamil, diltiazem, β-blockers, digoxin) in pre-excited AF**; use procainamide or DC cardioversion. Definitive treatment: catheter ablation.

## 4. Conduction abnormalities and axis

### 4.1 Bundle branch blocks (QRS ≥ 0.12 s)

| | Right bundle branch block (RBBB) | Left bundle branch block (LBBB) |
|---|---|---|
| V1 | rSR′ ("rabbit ears", M-shape) | Deep QS or rS |
| V6, I | Wide slurred S wave | Broad notched R wave ("M"), no septal q |
| ST–T | Secondary ST depression and T inversion V1–V3 | Discordant ST–T changes (opposite to QRS) |
| Significance | Can be normal; PE, RV strain, congenital, cor pulmonale, Brugada mimic | Almost always pathological: ischaemic, hypertensive, cardiomyopathy, aortic stenosis; **new LBBB with ischaemic symptoms** → treat as possible MI per local pathway |

Incomplete block: QRS 0.10–0.12 s. **LBBB masks MI**: use the **Sgarbossa criteria** (concordant ST elevation ≥ 1 mm in any lead scores 5; concordant ST depression ≥ 1 mm in V1–V3 scores 3; excessively discordant ST elevation ≥ 25% of the S-wave depth scores 2 [modified Smith–Sgarbossa]; score ≥ 3 specific for occlusion MI). Also Cabrera sign and the Chapman sign are weak signs. Paced rhythm similarly requires adapted criteria.

### 4.2 Fascicular blocks

- **Left anterior fascicular block (LAFB)**: left axis deviation (−45° to −90°), qR in I and aVL, rS in II, III, aVF; QRS < 0.12.
- **Left posterior fascicular block (LPFB)**: right axis deviation, rS in I and aVL, qR in III and aVF; exclude RV hypertrophy and lateral MI.
- **Bifascicular block**: RBBB + LAFB (or LPFB). **Trifascicular block** conceptually: bifascicular + first-degree AV block (not a true diagnosis of trifascicular disease); syncope with bifascicular block → consider EP study/pacing.

### 4.3 Axis deviation: causes

- **Left axis deviation**: LAFB, inferior MI, LVH, WPW (left-sided pathway), hyperkalaemia, pacing, emphysema.
- **Right axis deviation**: RVH, acute PE, lateral MI, LPFB, COPD, dextrocardia, lead reversal, normal in young thin patients (up to +110°), WPW.

## 5. Chamber enlargement and hypertrophy

- **Left atrial enlargement**: P > 0.12 s notched in II ("P mitrale"), terminal negative component in V1 > 1 mm wide and deep.
- **Right atrial enlargement**: P > 2.5 mm in II, III, aVF ("P pulmonale").
- **Left ventricular hypertrophy (LVH)**: Sokolow–Lyon (S in V1 + R in V5 or V6 > 35 mm); Cornell (R in aVL + S in V3 > 28 mm men, > 20 mm women); R in aVL > 11 mm; with **strain pattern** (asymmetric ST depression and T inversion in V5–V6, I, aVL). Voltage criteria are less sensitive in obesity; echo confirms.
- **Right ventricular hypertrophy**: dominant R in V1 (R/S > 1), right axis deviation, deep S in V5–V6, strain pattern in V1–V3, P pulmonale; causes: pulmonary hypertension, mitral stenosis, pulmonary stenosis, congenital heart disease.
- **Low voltage** (QRS < 5 mm in all limb leads or < 10 mm in chest leads): pericardial effusion, obesity, emphysema, hypothyroidism, infiltrative disease (amyloid), pleural effusion; **electrical alternans** (beat-to-beat QRS amplitude change) indicates tamponade.

## 6. Myocardial ischaemia and infarction

### 6.1 Evolution of STEMI

1. **Hyperacute T waves** (minutes): broad, tall, symmetric T waves, often earlier than ST elevation.
2. **ST elevation** (minutes to hours): convex or straight ("tombstone"), with reciprocal depression in opposite leads.
3. **Q waves** (hours to days): pathological, may or may not resolve.
4. **T wave inversion** (days): ST returns to baseline.
5. **Chronic**: persistent Q waves ± ST elevation (aneurysm).

### 6.2 Diagnostic criteria for ST-elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI)

New ST elevation at the J point in **two contiguous leads** of ≥ 1 mm, except V2–V3: ≥ 2 mm in men ≥ 40 years, ≥ 2.5 mm in men < 40, ≥ 1.5 mm in women. Plus clinical context. The definition of "contiguous": lead groups above.

| Territory | Leads with ST elevation | Reciprocal changes | Culprit |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Anterior (extensive)** | V1–V6, I, aVL | II, III, aVF | Proximal LAD |
| **Septal** | V1–V2 (V3) | | LAD |
| **Anterolateral** | V3–V6, I, aVL | | LAD/diagonal |
| **Inferior** | II, III, aVF | I, aVL | RCA (ST elevation III > II), LCx (II > III) |
| **Right ventricular** | ST elevation in V1 and right-sided leads V4R; with inferior MI | | Proximal RCA; avoid nitrates and diuretics; fluids |
| **Lateral** | I, aVL, V5–V6 | | LCx, diagonal |
| **Posterior** | ST **depression** V1–V3 with tall R waves, upright T waves; elevation in V7–V9 (≥ 0.5 mm) | | LCx/RCA; treat as STEMI |

### 6.3 STEMI equivalents and high-risk patterns ("occlusion MI")

- **Posterior MI** (above).
- **De Winter T waves**: upsloping ST depression at the J point with tall, symmetric T waves in V1–V6, ST elevation in aVR; indicates proximal LAD occlusion.
- **Wellens syndrome**: pain-free biphasic (type A) or deeply inverted (type B) T waves in V2–V3 in a patient with recent chest pain, normal troponin or minimally elevated; critical proximal LAD stenosis. **Avoid stress testing; urgent angiography.**
- **Left main/multivessel disease**: widespread ST depression (≥ 6 leads, maximal in V4–V6) with ST elevation in aVR (≥ 1 mm and > V1): left main or triple-vessel disease, or diffuse subendocardial ischaemia; urgent angiography if ongoing pain or instability.
- **Hyperacute T waves** without ST elevation (early occlusion).
- **LBBB/paced rhythm with Sgarbossa-positive** changes.
- **Isolated ST elevation in aVR with ST depression elsewhere**: see above.
- **Terminal QRS distortion**, ST elevation in V1–V2 with ST depression in V5–V6 (consider RV infarct, PE).
- Persistent ischaemic pain with a non-diagnostic ECG: repeat ECGs every 15–30 minutes, posterior and right-sided leads; discuss early angiography. Roughly 25–30% of NSTEMI patients have an occluded culprit artery ("occlusion MI") not meeting STEMI criteria.

### 6.4 NSTEMI/unstable angina patterns

Horizontal or downsloping ST depression ≥ 0.5 mm in ≥ 2 contiguous leads; dynamic T wave inversion (≥ 1 mm in leads with prominent R wave); transient ST elevation. A normal ECG does not exclude ACS: initial ECG is normal in about 30% of NSTEMI.

### 6.5 Mimics of ST elevation

| Mimic | Distinguishing features |
|---|---|
| **Benign early repolarisation** | Young, concave ST elevation with notched J point (V2–V5), tall T, stable on serial ECGs, no reciprocal depression |
| **Acute pericarditis** | Diffuse concave ST elevation (I, II, III, aVF, V2–V6) with **PR depression** (and PR elevation in aVR), no reciprocal depression except aVR/V1 |
| **LV aneurysm** | Persistent ST elevation with Q waves, small T (T/QRS ratio < 0.36 in V1–V4 differentiates from acute) |
| **LVH / LBBB** | Discordant ST–T proportionate to QRS |
| **Brugada pattern** | Coved ST elevation ≥ 2 mm with T inversion in V1–V2 (type 1) |
| **Hyperkalaemia** | With peaked T, wide QRS |
| **Takotsubo** | Anterior ST elevation or T inversion; apical ballooning on echo; normal coronary arteries |
| **Myocarditis** | Mimics pericarditis or MI; raised troponin |
| **Hypothermia** | J (Osborn) waves |
| **Pulmonary embolism, acute cor pulmonale** | ST elevation V1/aVR |
| **Cardiac injury** | Contusion, post-cardioversion |

### 6.6 Q waves and old infarction

Pathological Q waves in ≥ 2 contiguous leads (≥ 0.02 s in V2–V3, or QS complexes; ≥ 0.03 s and ≥ 1 mm deep in others) suggest prior MI. Mimics: LVH, cardiomyopathy, WPW, LBBB, pulmonary disease (poor R progression), left-to-right placement, normal Q in III and aVR, septal q.

## 7. Other important patterns

### 7.1 Pericarditis and tamponade

Pericarditis stages: (I) diffuse concave ST elevation with PR depression (first days); (II) normalisation; (III) T-wave inversion; (IV) normalisation. Tamponade: sinus tachycardia, low voltage, electrical alternans.

### 7.2 Pulmonary embolism

Most commonly **sinus tachycardia** and non-specific ST–T changes; classical **S1Q3T3** (S in I, Q and T inversion in III) is neither sensitive nor specific; other: new RBBB, right axis deviation, T-wave inversion in V1–V4 (RV strain), clockwise rotation, P pulmonale. A normal ECG does not exclude PE. Differential for anterior T inversions: PE, anterior ischaemia (Wellens), RV strain, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, arrhythmogenic RV cardiomyopathy, persistent juvenile pattern.

### 7.3 Electrolyte and metabolic effects

| Disorder | ECG |
|---|---|
| **Hyperkalaemia** | Peaked narrow-based T (K ~5.5–6.5) → PR prolongation, P flattening/loss → QRS widening (6.5–8) → sine-wave pattern → asystole/VF; ECG can be normal at high K (poor sensitivity); also causes bradycardia, AV block, ST elevation mimicking Brugada or STEMI |
| **Hypokalaemia** | Flattened T, ST depression, prominent U wave, apparent QT prolongation (QU), arrhythmias (torsades, AF) |
| **Hypercalcaemia** | Short QT (ST segment shortened) |
| **Hypocalcaemia** | Prolonged QT (long ST) |
| **Hypomagnesaemia** | Prolonged QT/torsades, resembles hypokalaemia |
| **Hypothermia** | Sinus bradycardia, AF, shivering artefact, **J (Osborn) waves**, prolonged intervals, VF risk below 28 °C |
| **Hypothyroidism** | Sinus bradycardia, low voltage, T flattening |
| **Hyperthyroidism** | Sinus tachycardia, AF |

### 7.4 Drug effects and toxicity

- **Digoxin**: "scooped" ST depression (reverse tick), T flattening, short QT; toxicity: atrial tachycardia with block, ventricular bigeminy, bidirectional VT, AV blocks, hyperkalaemia in acute toxicity.
- **Tricyclic antidepressant overdose**: sinus tachycardia, QRS > 100 ms, terminal R in aVR > 3 mm, wide-complex tachycardia; sodium bicarbonate.
- **Class IC drugs (flecainide)**, **class IA** (quinidine, procainamide): QRS widening, QT prolongation.
- **QT-prolonging drugs**: macrolides, fluoroquinolones, antipsychotics (haloperidol, quetiapine), ondansetron, methadone, citalopram, sotalol, amiodarone, azole antifungals, hydroxychloroquine, many others (CredibleMeds list).
- **β-blockers, CCBs**: bradycardia, AV block.

### 7.5 Channelopathies and inherited conditions

- **Long QT syndrome** (congenital types 1–3, acquired): QTc > 480–500 ms; syncope with exertion/swimming (LQT1), sudden noise/arousal (LQT2), sleep (LQT3); β-blockers, avoid triggers/QT drugs; ICD for high-risk. Family screening.
- **Short QT syndrome**: QTc < 340 ms.
- **Brugada syndrome**: type 1 coved ST elevation ≥ 2 mm with negative T in V1–V2 (spontaneous or sodium-channel-blocker challenge); syncope, nocturnal VF; avoid fever (treat promptly) and provoking drugs; ICD for symptomatic.
- **Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM)**: LVH, deep narrow "dagger" Q waves (lateral/inferior), giant T inversions (apical HCM), left atrial enlargement.
- **Arrhythmogenic right ventricular cardiomyopathy**: epsilon wave, T inversion V1–V3, VT with LBBB morphology.
- **Dilated cardiomyopathy, amyloid** (low voltage with pseudo-infarct pattern and thick LV), **sarcoid** (AV block, VT).
- **Athlete's heart**: bradycardia, LVH voltage, early repolarisation, first-degree block; differentiate from pathology (ESC criteria).

### 7.6 Pacemaker ECGs

Atrial pacing spike before P; ventricular pacing spike before wide LBBB-pattern QRS (RV apical); dual-chamber sequences; failure to capture (spike without QRS), failure to sense (inappropriate spike), failure to pace (absent spikes). Interpretation of ischaemia in paced rhythm requires specific criteria (Sgarbossa applies).

## 8. Using the ECG clinically

### 8.1 Indications

Chest pain, dyspnoea, palpitations, syncope/presyncope, suspected arrhythmia, electrolyte disturbance, drug toxicity (TCA, digoxin, QT drugs), pre-operative assessment in selected patients, screening in hypertension/diabetes (baseline), monitoring in critical illness, stroke (AF detection). **Obtain and interpret within 10 minutes** for chest pain suspected as ACS.

### 8.2 Urgency classification

| Immediate action (minutes) | Urgent (hours) | Outpatient/Non-urgent |
|---|---|---|
| VF, pulseless VT, asystole, torsades; VT with instability; STEMI/occlusion MI; complete heart block with symptoms or ventricular escape; Mobitz II with symptoms; hyperkalaemia with ECG changes; WCT; AF with pre-excitation and instability; severe bradycardia with shock; tamponade signs | New AF with fast rate or heart failure; NSTEMI; Wellens; new LBBB with symptoms; QTc > 500 ms; pericarditis; Brugada type 1 with syncope; high-grade AV block; unexplained syncope with bifascicular block; PE signs | Isolated PACs/PVCs; first-degree AV block; incomplete RBBB; LAFB; early repolarisation; stable chronic AF (rate control and anticoagulation planning); non-specific ST–T changes in asymptomatic |

### 8.3 Automated interpretation

Computer statements (e.g., "acute MI", "AF", "normal ECG") are right most of the time on clear tracings but fail in rhythm disorders (over-calling AF, missing flutter), LBBB, pacing, hypertrophy and subtle ischaemia; STEMI algorithms may miss occlusion MI. Always over-read.

### 8.4 Rhythm monitoring beyond the 12-lead ECG

Holter (24–72 h), event recorders, patch monitors (7–14 days), implantable loop recorders (up to 3 years) for infrequent symptoms; smartwatch tracings for AF detection require confirmation with a 12-lead ECG; exercise ECG (limited sensitivity ~68%, specificity ~77%); echocardiography and imaging alternatives (chapter 67).

## Worked cases

### Case 1: "Gastric pain" with a normal troponin at first

**Presentation.** Mr A., 58, diabetic, 90 minutes of epigastric pain and sweating, nausea. ECG: sinus rhythm 88/min; ST elevation 2 mm in II, III, aVF (III > II), reciprocal ST depression in I and aVL; ST elevation in V1 and V4R. Initial hs-troponin is not yet elevated.

**Reasoning.** Inferior STEMI with RV involvement (ST elevation V4R, III > II suggests RCA). Troponin is not required before reperfusion. Hypotension risk with nitrates and diuretics (preload-dependent RV).

**Action.** Activate the cath-lab pathway (primary PCI within 90–120 minutes of first contact; fibrinolysis if not available within 120 minutes), aspirin 300 mg, P2Y12 inhibitor and heparin per protocol, oxygen only if SpO₂ < 90%, **avoid nitrates**, give IV fluids if hypotensive, atropine/pacing for bradycardia or AV block. Right coronary occlusion found and stented.

**Lessons.** Obtain right-sided leads in inferior MI; diagnose from the ECG, not from troponin.

### Case 2: Palpitations in a young woman

**Presentation.** Ms B., 27, sudden onset palpitations, HR 190 regular, BP 110/70, narrow QRS, no visible P waves. Vagal manoeuvre (modified Valsalva with leg raise) ineffective.

**Reasoning.** Regular narrow-complex tachycardia at 190: AVNRT most likely (young woman, abrupt onset). Flutter at 150 and sinus tachycardia are excluded by rate and absence of P. Stable, so a vagal manoeuvre then adenosine.

**Action.** Adenosine 6 mg rapid IV (large vein, flush) → 12 mg if no response; warn of transient chest tightness; record a 12-lead during conversion (pseudo r′ in V1 after conversion in AVNRT). Sinus rhythm restored. Baseline ECG afterwards: look for pre-excitation (delta wave). Refer for electrophysiology study/ablation if recurrent; teach the modified Valsalva.

**Lessons.** Treat regular narrow tachycardia stepwise; capture an ECG at conversion; exclude WPW before long-term nodal-blocking therapy.

### Case 3: Wide-complex tachycardia in a man with an old MI

**Presentation.** Mr C., 68, previous anterior MI, EF 30%, sudden palpitations and light-headedness. HR 170 regular, BP 98/60, QRS 160 ms with northwest axis; visible dissociated P waves in the rhythm strip (capture beat).

**Reasoning.** AV dissociation, wide QRS, structural heart disease: ventricular tachycardia. The relative stability does not make it SVT. Verapamil would be harmful.

**Action.** Given borderline BP, symptoms and risk, prepare for sedation and synchronised cardioversion (100–200 J biphasic); alternatively IV amiodarone 300 mg if stable and monitored. Correct K (aim 4.5–5.0) and Mg; ICD evaluation for secondary prevention (VT with EF ≤ 35%); coronary assessment.

**Lessons.** In WCT assume VT, especially with prior MI; avoid AV nodal blockers; the diagnosis of VT here rests on dissociation.

### Case 4: The sinus tachycardia that is not just anxiety

**Presentation.** Ms D., 31, postpartum 2 weeks, sudden pleuritic chest pain and dyspnoea. HR 118, SpO₂ 93%. ECG: sinus tachycardia, T-wave inversion in V1–V4 and III, new rightward axis shift; no ST elevation.

**Reasoning.** T-wave inversion in V1–V4 and III signifies RV strain: pulmonary embolism is likely (postpartum is a risk factor). The combination of tachycardia, hypoxaemia and RV strain suggests intermediate-high-risk PE. The ECG alone cannot diagnose; imaging is required.

**Action.** Wells/clinical probability high → CT pulmonary angiography; start anticoagulation (LMWH) while awaiting imaging; troponin and BNP/echo for RV strain; escalation if hypotension (thrombolysis).

**Lessons.** A normal-appearing ECG with tachycardia plus anterior T inversion should prompt thought of PE; ECG helps risk-stratify.

### Case 5: Syncope with a 2:1 block

**Presentation.** Mr E., 74, two episodes of syncope. ECG: P waves 80/min, ventricular rate 40/min, regular, QRS 140 ms (RBBB + LAFB pattern), every second P wave conducted.

**Reasoning.** 2:1 AV block with a wide QRS and bifascicular disease: infranodal block, high risk of progression to complete heart block and asystole. Syncope is likely arrhythmic.

**Action.** Admit on telemetry, avoid AV nodal and rate-slowing drugs, atropine unlikely to help (infranodal), transcutaneous pacing pads on, isoprenaline/temporary pacing if unstable, early permanent pacemaker; look for reversible causes (ischaemia, hyperkalaemia, drugs, Lyme in endemic areas, thyroid).

**Lessons.** Syncope plus bifascicular block or advanced AV block requires admission and pacing assessment.

### Case 6: QT prolongation after antibiotics

**Presentation.** Mrs F., 72, on citalopram and furosemide, started on clarithromycin for pneumonia; now palpitations and a near-faint. ECG: QTc 560 ms, K 3.0, Mg 0.6; ventricular ectopics.

**Reasoning.** Acquired long QT from multiple QT-prolonging agents plus hypokalaemia and hypomagnesaemia (diuretic): high risk for torsades.

**Action.** Stop clarithromycin and citalopram (review); IV magnesium sulfate 2 g over 10–15 minutes; replace potassium to 4.5–5.0; continuous monitoring; if torsades recurs: repeat magnesium, overdrive pacing or isoprenaline; defibrillate if VF/pulseless VT. Choose a non-QT antibiotic (e.g., amoxicillin or doxycycline for pneumonia per guidelines).

**Lessons.** Review the drug list when QTc is long; correct electrolytes first; recognise combinations.

### Case 7: ECG of the chest pain that is not an infarct

**Presentation.** Mr G., 24, sharp central chest pain worse lying flat and on inspiration after a viral illness. ECG: widespread concave ST elevation (I, II, III, aVF, V2–V6), PR depression, PR elevation in aVR, ST depression in aVR and V1, no reciprocal depression elsewhere. hs-troponin mildly raised.

**Reasoning.** Acute pericarditis (myopericarditis with troponin leak). The diffuse distribution crossing coronary territories and PR depression separate it from STEMI.

**Action.** Echo (exclude effusion and wall motion abnormality), NSAID (ibuprofen 600 mg tds) plus colchicine for 3 months, gastro-protection, restrict strenuous exercise for 3 months (myopericarditis: 3–6 months); review in a week; return if worsening, syncope, dyspnoea (tamponade).

**Lessons.** Distribution, morphology and the PR segment point towards pericarditis; if still in doubt and the patient has risk factors, treat as ACS until excluded.

## Common pitfalls

1. **Reading the machine** instead of the tracing; over- and under-diagnosing AF, STEMI.
2. **Ignoring the clinical context**, e.g., treating a pericarditis ECG as STEMI or vice versa.
3. **Waiting for troponin** in a diagnostic STEMI.
4. **Single ECG** in ongoing pain: repeat every 15–30 minutes and obtain posterior and right-sided leads.
5. **Missing STEMI equivalents** (posterior, de Winter, Wellens, LBBB with Sgarbossa, hyperacute T).
6. **Treating wide-complex tachycardia as SVT** and giving verapamil/diltiazem.
7. **Giving AV nodal blockers in pre-excited AF.**
8. **Failing to read the old ECG** for comparison (old LBBB, old Q waves).
9. **Calling "normal ECG" in the presence of ongoing symptoms** and discharging; a normal ECG does not exclude ACS, PE or arrhythmia.
10. **QTc errors**: using Bazett at extreme heart rates; ignoring drug interactions.
11. **Not recognising lead reversal** (and unnecessary alarm).
12. **Misinterpreting artefact** (Parkinsonian tremor as flutter/VT).
13. **Not checking potassium** in unexplained wide QRS or bradycardia.
14. **Assuming sinus tachycardia is the disease** instead of looking for its cause.
15. **Over-reliance on S1Q3T3** for PE.

---

## Key points

- Use the same systematic method every time: rate, rhythm, axis, intervals, P, QRS, ST, T, U; compare with old.
- Wide-complex tachycardia is VT until proven otherwise; unstable patients get synchronised cardioversion.
- STEMI is diagnosed by ECG; do not wait for troponin. Learn equivalents: posterior, de Winter, Wellens, Sgarbossa, left main pattern.
- Diffuse concave ST elevation with PR depression: pericarditis; LBBB and paced rhythms require special criteria.
- AV block: Mobitz II, high-grade and complete block need pacing; Wenckebach is usually benign.
- Check electrolytes and drug lists with unexplained ECG changes; hyperkalaemia and long QT are emergencies.
- AF with pre-excitation: no nodal blockers. Treat the unstable with DC cardioversion.
- The ECG is a clinical tool: pre-test probability, symptoms and serial tracings determine its meaning.

## Self-assessment

**Questions**

1. Describe the steps for reading an ECG in order.
2. How do you estimate heart rate on a 25 mm/s tracing?
3. List five features favouring VT over SVT with aberrancy.
4. What leads show ST elevation in an RCA inferior MI with RV involvement, and why avoid nitrates?
5. Name three STEMI equivalents.
6. What ECG features distinguish pericarditis from STEMI?
7. Describe the progression of ECG changes in hyperkalaemia.
8. A patient with WPW develops irregular, fast, wide-complex tachycardia. Which drugs should be avoided and what is the treatment?
9. What defines Mobitz II and why does it require pacing?
10. Which ECG findings may be present in pulmonary embolism, and what is the best test to confirm it?

**Answers**

1. Rate, rhythm, axis, intervals (PR, QRS, QT), P waves, QRS morphology (voltage, Q waves, progression), ST segment, T waves, U waves, then compare with old ECGs and correlate clinically.
2. 300 ÷ number of large squares between R waves (regular rhythm), or 1500 ÷ small squares; for irregular rhythms count QRS complexes in 6 seconds × 10.
3. AV dissociation, capture/fusion beats, QRS > 140–160 ms, northwest/extreme axis, concordance across chest leads, history of MI/structural disease, RS interval > 100 ms, initial R wave in aVR.
4. II, III, aVF (III > II) and V1 / V4R; nitrates reduce preload in a preload-dependent RV, causing severe hypotension.
5. Posterior MI, de Winter pattern, Wellens syndrome (pre-occlusion), left main pattern (diffuse ST depression with aVR elevation), Sgarbossa-positive LBBB, hyperacute T waves.
6. Diffuse concave ST elevation not confined to a coronary territory, PR depression (elevation in aVR), absence of reciprocal ST depression (except aVR and V1), no Q waves.
7. Peaked T waves → PR prolongation and P-wave flattening → QRS widening → sine wave → VF/asystole; note that the ECG can be normal despite high K.
8. Avoid adenosine, verapamil, diltiazem, β-blockers and digoxin (they block the AV node, favour accessory pathway conduction and may precipitate VF); treat with DC cardioversion (unstable) or procainamide/ibutilide; definitive ablation.
9. Intermittent non-conducted P waves with constant PR intervals before the dropped beat (usually wide QRS), indicating infranodal disease; it can progress abruptly to complete heart block and asystole, so a pacemaker is indicated.
10. Sinus tachycardia (commonest), T inversion V1–V4 and III, S1Q3T3, new RBBB, right axis deviation, P pulmonale; normal ECG possible; confirm with CT pulmonary angiography (or V/Q scan), guided by clinical probability and D-dimer.
