## Chapter 40. Respiratory Failure and Airway Compromise

### Learning objectives

After studying this chapter you should be able to:

1. Define type 1 (hypoxaemic) and type 2 (hypercapnic) respiratory failure, explain their mechanisms, and interpret arterial blood gases, including acute versus chronic hypercapnia.
2. Recognise the clinical signs of hypoxia, hypercapnia, respiratory muscle fatigue and impending respiratory arrest.
3. Recognise and manage acute upper airway obstruction (epiglottitis, croup, foreign body, angioedema, burns, neck haematoma) and know the strategy for the "can't intubate, can't oxygenate" scenario.
4. Choose the correct oxygen delivery device and target, and avoid both hypoxia and hyperoxia.
5. Apply non-invasive ventilation (NIV), CPAP and high-flow nasal oxygen appropriately, and know their contraindications and failure criteria.
6. Recognise the indications for intubation, plan a safe emergency intubation, and use lung-protective ventilation, including for ARDS and obstructive lung disease.
7. Manage the commonest causes: COPD exacerbation, life-threatening asthma, pulmonary oedema, pneumonia, neuromuscular respiratory failure, opioid toxicity and obesity hypoventilation.

---

### 40.1 Definitions

**Respiratory failure** is the inability of the respiratory system to maintain adequate oxygenation and/or carbon dioxide elimination to meet the body's metabolic needs. It is diagnosed by **arterial blood gas** values together with the clinical picture.

| | **Type 1 (hypoxaemic)** | **Type 2 (hypercapnic, "ventilatory")** |
|---|---|---|
| **PaO₂ (room air)** | **<8 kPa (60 mmHg)** | Low (usually) |
| **PaCO₂** | Normal or low | **>6.0 kPa (45 mmHg)** |
| **Mechanism** | Failure of oxygen transfer: V/Q mismatch, shunt, diffusion limitation | Failure of ventilation: inadequate alveolar ventilation |
| **Examples** | Pneumonia, pulmonary oedema, ARDS, pulmonary embolism, early asthma, pulmonary fibrosis, pneumothorax | COPD, severe asthma with exhaustion, neuromuscular disease, chest wall disease, obesity hypoventilation, drugs depressing the respiratory centre |

Additional categories include **type 3 (perioperative: atelectasis)** and **type 4 (shock: hypoperfusion of respiratory muscles).** Many patients have **mixed** failure (e.g. severe COPD exacerbation with pneumonia).

**Normal arterial blood gas values (on air):** pH 7.35–7.45; PaO₂ 10–13.3 kPa (75–100 mmHg); PaCO₂ 4.7–6.0 kPa (35–45 mmHg); HCO₃⁻ 22–26 mmol/L; base excess ±2 mmol/L; SaO₂ 95–100%. (Pregnancy: PaCO₂ is normally about 4.0 kPa, so 5 kPa already reflects relative hypoventilation.)

**Acute vs chronic respiratory failure:**

- **Acute:** minutes to days; acidosis (low pH) in hypercapnia because there has been no time for renal compensation; clinical urgency.
- **Chronic:** weeks to years; the kidneys retain bicarbonate so pH is near normal (compensated); **acute-on-chronic** failure (e.g. COPD exacerbation) shows a raised PaCO₂ with a **low pH** despite a high bicarbonate.

### 40.2 Pathophysiology

#### Mechanisms of hypoxaemia

1. **Ventilation–perfusion (V/Q) mismatch** (the commonest): airways obstruction, consolidation, atelectasis, pulmonary embolism; corrects with oxygen.
2. **Shunt:** perfusion of unventilated alveoli (severe pneumonia, ARDS, pulmonary oedema, atelectasis) or anatomical right-to-left shunts (congenital heart disease, pulmonary arteriovenous malformation); **poorly responsive to oxygen.**
3. **Diffusion limitation:** interstitial lung disease, pulmonary oedema; usually exacerbated by exercise.
4. **Hypoventilation:** raises PaCO₂ and lowers PaO₂; the alveolar–arterial (A–a) gradient is normal.
5. **Reduced inspired oxygen:** altitude, hypoxic gas mixtures, oxygen supply failure.

The **alveolar gas equation** (PAO₂ = FiO₂ × [barometric pressure − water vapour pressure] − PaCO₂/R) allows calculation of the **A–a gradient** (normal about 2.0–3.0 kPa in young adults, increasing with age). A raised gradient indicates V/Q mismatch, shunt or diffusion defects; a normal gradient indicates hypoventilation or low FiO₂.

The **PaO₂/FiO₂ (P/F) ratio** is used to grade severity: normal >400 mmHg (53 kPa); <300 mmHg (40 kPa) acute lung injury; <200 moderate; <100 severe.

#### Mechanisms of hypercapnia

PaCO₂ is inversely proportional to **alveolar ventilation** (minute ventilation minus dead-space ventilation) and proportional to CO₂ production.

- **Reduced respiratory drive:** opioids, sedatives, anaesthetics, brainstem lesions, hypothyroidism, obesity hypoventilation syndrome, central sleep apnoea.
- **Neuromuscular failure:** spinal cord injury, motor neurone disease, Guillain–Barré syndrome, myasthenia gravis, muscular dystrophies, phrenic nerve injury, critical illness neuromyopathy, botulism, organophosphate poisoning.
- **Chest wall and pleural disease:** kyphoscoliosis, flail chest, obesity, large pleural effusion, pneumothorax, ankylosing spondylitis.
- **Airflow obstruction and increased work of breathing:** COPD, severe asthma, upper airway obstruction, cystic fibrosis, bronchiectasis, with **respiratory muscle fatigue** as the final common pathway.
- **Increased dead space:** PE, emphysema, ARDS.
- **Increased CO₂ production:** fever, sepsis, burns, overfeeding with carbohydrates.

### 40.3 Recognising respiratory failure at the bedside

#### Signs of hypoxaemia

- **Tachypnoea,** tachycardia, anxiety, restlessness, agitation, confusion, drowsiness, **central cyanosis** (late; unreliable in anaemia, polycythaemia, poor light), sweating, bradycardia and cardiac arrhythmia (late), seizures, coma.
- **SpO₂ <90%;** reliability reduced in shock, hypothermia, peripheral vasoconstriction, nail polish, carbon monoxide (falsely normal), methaemoglobinaemia (reads about 85%), movement.

#### Signs of hypercapnia

- **Headache (morning),** drowsiness, confusion, flapping tremor (asterixis), **warm peripheries, bounding pulse,** vasodilatation, conjunctival injection, **papilloedema** (rare), myoclonus, sweating, coma ("CO₂ narcosis"), respiratory acidosis.
- Often a **slow or shallow** breathing pattern in the late stages as the patient tires.

#### Signs of increased work of breathing and impending failure

- Use of **accessory muscles** (sternocleidomastoid, scalene), **intercostal and subcostal recession,** nasal flaring, tracheal tug, pursed-lip breathing, **tripod posture,** inability to complete sentences, diaphoresis, **paradoxical (see-saw) breathing,** respiratory alternans.
- **Exhaustion:** reducing respiratory rate, feeble effort, drowsiness, **silent chest** in asthma, confusion, cyanosis, bradycardia: **pre-arrest signs.** Do not be reassured by a falling respiratory rate in a drowsy patient.

#### Investigation

- **Continuous SpO₂ and ECG.**
- **Arterial blood gas** (gold standard for PaO₂, PaCO₂, pH, bicarbonate, lactate; venous gas can estimate pH and PaCO₂ but not oxygenation).
- **Chest X-ray** (consolidation, effusion, pneumothorax, hyperinflation, oedema), **ECG, FBC, U&E, CRP, glucose, BNP, troponin, D-dimer/CTPA** as indicated, **sputum and blood cultures,** **peak flow/spirometry** (if feasible), **bedside ultrasound** (lung: B-lines, pleural effusion, pneumothorax; cardiac function).
- In neuromuscular disease: **vital capacity (FVC) and maximal inspiratory pressure (MIP);** bulbar function.

#### Interpreting the blood gas in respiratory failure

Step-by-step: (1) pH: acidaemia or alkalaemia; (2) PaCO₂: respiratory component; (3) HCO₃⁻/base excess: metabolic component; (4) compensation; (5) PaO₂ and A–a gradient; (6) lactate and anion gap.

| Pattern | Interpretation |
|---|---|
| pH ↓, PaCO₂ ↑, HCO₃⁻ normal | **Acute respiratory acidosis** (acute hypercapnic failure) |
| pH ↓ or low-normal, PaCO₂ ↑, HCO₃⁻ ↑ | **Acute-on-chronic respiratory acidosis** (e.g. COPD exacerbation) |
| pH normal, PaCO₂ ↑, HCO₃⁻ ↑ | **Chronic compensated respiratory acidosis** |
| pH ↓, PaCO₂ ↓, HCO₃⁻ ↓ | **Metabolic acidosis with respiratory compensation** (e.g. DKA, sepsis) |
| PaO₂ ↓, PaCO₂ low-normal/↓, normal pH | **Type 1 failure** |

**Rules of thumb:** in **acute** respiratory acidosis, HCO₃⁻ rises about 1 mmol/L for each 10 mmHg (1.3 kPa) rise in PaCO₂; in **chronic** hypercapnia, about 3.5–4 mmol/L per 10 mmHg. **A normal or "reassuring" PaCO₂ in an asthmatic or tiring patient with severe distress is ominous** (impending exhaustion).

### 40.4 Oxygen therapy

#### Principles

- **Oxygen is a drug:** prescribe a **target saturation range,** monitor, and adjust.
- **Critically ill patients:** start **high-flow oxygen (10–15 L/min) via a reservoir mask,** then titrate to target as soon as the patient is stable.
- **Target SpO₂:** **94–98%** for most acutely ill patients; **88–92%** for patients at risk of **hypercapnic respiratory failure** (COPD, severe obesity, chest wall deformities, neuromuscular disease, cystic fibrosis/bronchiectasis with chronic infection, previous hypercapnic failure, or **known oxygen alert card**).
- **Avoid hyperoxia:** harmful in many conditions (post-cardiac arrest, stroke, myocardial infarction without hypoxia, COPD) and may cause absorption atelectasis, CO₂ retention (mechanisms: loss of hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction increasing dead space, Haldane effect, reduced hypoxic drive), and oxygen toxicity (prolonged high FiO₂).
- **Special situations where high-concentration oxygen is given regardless of saturation:** carbon monoxide poisoning (100%), cluster headache, pneumothorax before drainage, major trauma, shock, anaphylaxis, sickle crisis with hypoxia, cardiac arrest.

#### Delivery devices

| Device | Flow | Approximate FiO₂ | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Nasal cannulae** | 1–6 L/min | 24–44% | Comfortable; low flows for stable patients |
| **Simple face mask** | 5–10 L/min | 40–60% | Minimum flow 5 L/min to flush CO₂ |
| **Venturi mask (24%, 28%, 35%, 40%, 60%)** | Colour-coded valve with specified flow | **Fixed, accurate FiO₂** | Preferred for **controlled oxygen** in COPD (start with 24–28%) |
| **Reservoir (non-rebreather) mask** | 10–15 L/min | 60–90% | Critical illness; keep the reservoir inflated |
| **High-flow nasal oxygen (HFNO)** | Up to 60–70 L/min (adults), heated and humidified | 21–100%, adjustable | Reduces work of breathing; flushes dead space; low-level PEEP; tolerated better than masks; useful in type 1 failure (pneumonia, COVID-19), post-extubation; can reduce intubation compared with standard oxygen in acute hypoxaemic respiratory failure |
| **CPAP** (continuous positive airway pressure) | Pressure 5–15 cmH₂O | Up to 100% | Recruits alveoli; reduces preload and afterload (**cardiogenic pulmonary oedema**), splints airways (**OSA**), type 1 failure |
| **NIV (BiPAP)** | Inspiratory pressure (IPAP) and expiratory pressure (EPAP) | Titratable | Hypercapnic failure |
| **Bag-valve-mask** | 15 L/min with reservoir | Up to ~95% | Emergency ventilation |
| **Invasive mechanical ventilation** | | | See 40.7 |

### 40.5 Acute upper airway obstruction

#### Recognition

- **Stridor** (inspiratory = at or above the glottis; biphasic = subglottic; expiratory = lower trachea/bronchi), **voice change** (muffled "hot-potato" voice in epiglottitis or peritonsillar abscess; hoarseness in laryngeal disease), **drooling and inability to swallow,** **dyspnoea and use of accessory muscles,** **tripod or "sniffing" position,** **cyanosis,** **see-saw breathing,** anxiety, silent chest in complete obstruction.
- **History:** sudden onset (foreign body, anaphylaxis, angioedema), fever with sore throat (epiglottitis, retropharyngeal abscess), post-operative (neck haematoma), burns or smoke inhalation (singed nasal hair, soot in the mouth, hoarseness), trauma, tumour, known allergy, ACE inhibitors, recent intubation (subglottic stenosis), thyroid disease.

#### Causes and key features

| Cause | Features | Specific management |
|---|---|---|
| **Foreign body aspiration** | Sudden choking, cough, stridor; children, older adults with swallowing problems, intoxicated | Back blows, abdominal thrusts (Chapter 37); laryngoscopy and Magill forceps; bronchoscopy for lower airway foreign body |
| **Anaphylaxis / angioedema** | Swollen lips, tongue, face, stridor, wheeze, urticaria | **IM adrenaline,** oxygen; ACE-inhibitor angioedema: airway prediction (nasendoscopy), early intubation, icatibant (Chapter 31) |
| **Epiglottitis (acute supraglottitis)** | Rare now in children after Hib vaccination; **adults** too; sudden sore throat, fever, dysphagia, drooling, muffled voice, stridor, tripod | **Keep the patient calm and upright;** do not examine the throat or lie flat; **senior anaesthetist and ENT** for securing the airway in theatre (gas induction, tracheostomy backup), then IV ceftriaxone ± steroids |
| **Croup (laryngotracheobronchitis)** | Child 6 months–3 years, barking cough, hoarseness, inspiratory stridor, low-grade fever | **Dexamethasone** (0.15–0.6 mg/kg single oral dose), **nebulised adrenaline** for moderate–severe; humidified air has no proven benefit; admit if stridor at rest, hypoxia |
| **Bacterial tracheitis** | Toxic child, high fever, stridor, purulent secretions | Airway management, antibiotics |
| **Retropharyngeal or peritonsillar abscess, Ludwig angina** | Fever, sore throat, trismus, neck swelling, drooling, floor of mouth elevation | Airway first (avoid supine position), IV antibiotics, surgical drainage |
| **Neck haematoma (post-thyroid/parathyroid/carotid surgery)** | Neck swelling, tight wound, stridor, hours after surgery | **Emergency: remove the sutures/clips at the bedside to decompress,** oxygen, call surgeon and anaesthetist; intubate if needed |
| **Burns and inhalation injury** | Facial burns, singed nasal hair, soot, hoarseness, stridor, carbonaceous sputum | **Early intubation** before oedema progresses; oxygen 100%; carbon monoxide and cyanide treatment |
| **Trauma (laryngeal, facial, neck)** | Subcutaneous emphysema, voice change, haemoptysis | Maintain spinal precautions; early definitive airway |
| **Tumours, vocal cord palsy, laryngeal oedema, tracheal stenosis, goitre** | Progressive stridor, hoarseness | Heliox, steroids, urgent ENT, tracheostomy |
| **Obstructive sleep apnoea, tongue obstruction in unconscious patients** | Snoring, apnoea | Positioning, airway adjuncts, CPAP |
| **Bilateral vocal cord paralysis, laryngospasm, vocal cord dysfunction** | Stridor after surgery or extubation | Oxygen, positive pressure, suxamethonium (anaesthesia), reassure |

#### General management of upper airway obstruction

1. **Call for senior airway help early** (anaesthesia, ENT, ICU).
2. **High-flow oxygen;** allow the patient to **adopt the position of comfort** (usually sitting up); **avoid agitating** the patient (especially children): do not insert tongue depressors, cannulate, or separate the child from parents in suspected epiglottitis.
3. **Nebulised adrenaline** (1 mg in 5 mL = 1:1,000 adrenaline, or 0.5 mL/kg of 1:1,000 up to 5 mL in children) provides temporary relief of subglottic oedema; **heliox** (helium–oxygen mixture) reduces turbulent flow; **corticosteroids** (dexamethasone, hydrocortisone) for inflammation; **IV antibiotics** for infection.
4. **Prepare for a definitive airway** (see below), with a **"double set-up"** (equipment and team ready for both intubation and surgical airway) in theatre or the resus bay.
5. **Complete obstruction (cannot ventilate, cannot speak, cyanosis, collapse):** **emergency front-of-neck access (cricothyroidotomy).**

#### Front-of-neck access (the "can't intubate, can't oxygenate," CICO, situation)

The **Difficult Airway Society (DAS)** algorithm: **Plan A** facemask ventilation and tracheal intubation (optimise position, pre-oxygenation, video laryngoscopy, limit attempts to three, declare failure early); **Plan B** supraglottic airway device; **Plan C** facemask ventilation with neuromuscular blockade reversal/waking the patient; **Plan D:** **emergency front-of-neck access (scalpel–bougie–tube technique):** extend the neck, identify the cricothyroid membrane (palpate or vertical skin incision if impalpable), **horizontal stab incision through the membrane,** rotate the blade, slide a **bougie** along the blade into the trachea, railroad a **size 6.0 cuffed tracheal tube,** inflate the cuff, ventilate, and confirm with capnography. Declare the **CICO** situation aloud and call for help; do not delay.

#### Predicting a difficult airway

- **LEMON:** **L**ook externally (facial trauma, large incisors, beard, obesity), **E**valuate the 3-3-2 rule (mouth opening three fingers, mandibular length three fingers, thyromental distance two fingers), **M**allampati score (classes III–IV), **O**bstruction (epiglottitis, abscess, tumour, trauma), **N**eck mobility (cervical spine immobilisation, rheumatoid arthritis, ankylosing spondylitis).
- **MACOCHA** (ICU): Mallampati III–IV, Apnoea syndrome, Cervical spine limitation, Opening of the mouth <3 cm, Coma, Hypoxia, non-Anaesthetist operator.
- **Physiologically difficult airway:** severe hypoxia, shock, severe acidosis, raised right-sided pressures, high intracranial pressure: **resuscitate and optimise before intubation** (pre-oxygenate, give fluids or vasopressors, use reduced-dose induction agents, use NIV or HFNO to pre-oxygenate).

### 40.6 Non-invasive respiratory support

#### Non-invasive ventilation (NIV, bilevel positive airway pressure)

**Indications:**

- **Acute hypercapnic respiratory failure in COPD exacerbation** with **pH <7.35 and PaCO₂ >6.5 kPa** (>48 mmHg) after an hour of optimal medical therapy (bronchodilators, controlled oxygen, steroids, antibiotics). **Reduces intubation and mortality.**
- **Acute cardiogenic pulmonary oedema** (CPAP or NIV; CPAP is the first-line).
- **Hypercapnic failure from neuromuscular disease, chest wall deformity,** obesity hypoventilation (acute-on-chronic).
- **Immunocompromised patients** with acute respiratory failure (to avoid intubation).
- **Weaning from invasive ventilation and post-extubation** in high-risk patients.
- **Palliative use** to relieve dyspnoea.
- **Acute asthma:** limited evidence; used in selected cases under ICU supervision.
- **Pneumonia, ARDS:** limited benefit, high failure rate; HFNO may be preferred; use in an ICU or HDU.

**Contraindications (relative or absolute):** **respiratory arrest, inability to protect the airway (GCS <8 unless due to hypercapnia and expected to improve rapidly),** **vomiting, facial trauma or burns,** fixed upper airway obstruction, **undrained pneumothorax,** recent upper GI or airway surgery, **haemodynamic instability** (severe shock, arrhythmia), severe agitation or uncooperativeness, copious secretions.

**Practical use:**

1. **Explain to the patient,** fit a well-fitting mask (full face or nasal), start with **IPAP 10 cmH₂O and EPAP 4–5 cmH₂O** (titrate IPAP up by 2–5 cmH₂O every 10–20 minutes to 15–20 cmH₂O as tolerated, according to the chest rise, respiratory rate, and CO₂), with oxygen titrated to **SpO₂ 88–92%** (COPD).
2. **Monitor closely:** respiratory rate, mental state, work of breathing, SpO₂, **ABG at 1 hour (and 4 hours),** mask leak, comfort, gastric distension, skin damage.
3. **Success:** reduction in respiratory rate, improvement in pH and PaCO₂ within 1–2 hours.
4. **Failure:** worsening acidosis (pH falling), persistent hypoxia, exhaustion, deteriorating consciousness, intolerance: **escalate to intubation** (unless a **treatment ceiling** has been set: decide and document **before starting NIV** whether the patient is for intubation or NIV as the ceiling of care).
5. **Wean** gradually; continue home NIV if chronic hypercapnic failure persists.

#### CPAP

Single pressure (5–10 cmH₂O): treatment of **cardiogenic pulmonary oedema** (reduces preload and afterload, recruits alveoli), type 1 failure (pneumonia), obstructive sleep apnoea; avoid in hypercapnic failure as a single treatment.

#### High-flow nasal oxygen

Improved oxygenation and comfort, lowers respiratory rate, and may reduce intubation rates in acute hypoxaemic respiratory failure; **monitor** (ROX index = [SpO₂/FiO₂]/respiratory rate; values <3.85 at 12 hours predict failure); do not delay intubation if the patient deteriorates.

### 40.7 Invasive ventilation

#### Indications for intubation and mechanical ventilation

- **Airway protection or obstruction** (GCS ≤8, loss of airway reflexes, upper airway oedema, trauma, burns).
- **Failure of oxygenation** (SpO₂ <90% or PaO₂ <8 kPa despite maximal non-invasive support).
- **Failure of ventilation** (rising PaCO₂ with acidosis, exhaustion, failed NIV).
- **Respiratory muscle fatigue or impending arrest.**
- **Shock** (reduce oxygen consumption), **cardiac arrest,** **severe trauma,** **neuromuscular failure** (FVC <15–20 mL/kg or falling rapidly, MIP less negative than −30 cmH₂O, bulbar failure).
- **Planned procedures** and **transfer** with unstable gas exchange.

#### Safe emergency intubation (the "7 Ps" approach)

1. **Preparation:** team, roles, equipment (laryngoscopes: video laryngoscope; bougie; tube sizes; suction; supraglottic and surgical airway equipment; monitoring including **capnography**); drugs drawn up; plan A, B, C, D stated aloud.
2. **Pre-oxygenation:** 3–5 minutes of 100% oxygen via a tight-fitting mask (or **HFNO/NIV**) in the sitting or head-up position, with apnoeic oxygenation (nasal cannulae at 15 L/min during laryngoscopy).
3. **Pre-treatment/optimisation:** treat hypotension (fluids, vasopressors ready), manage raised ICP, give atropine in children if needed; consider fentanyl for haemodynamic blunting.
4. **Paralysis with induction (rapid-sequence induction):** induction agent (ketamine, etomidate, propofol, at reduced dose in shock) and a rapid-onset neuromuscular blocker (rocuronium 1.2 mg/kg or suxamethonium 1.5 mg/kg); cricoid pressure is optional and evidence is mixed.
5. **Protection and positioning:** head-up, ear-to-sternal-notch alignment ("sniffing" position); in-line stabilisation if trauma.
6. **Placement with proof:** direct or video laryngoscopy; **confirm with continuous waveform capnography** (the standard), chest rise, bilateral breath sounds; secure the tube; chest X-ray.
7. **Post-intubation management:** sedation and analgesia, ventilator settings, haemodynamic support, gastric tube, cuff pressure, positioning, plan for ICU.

#### Ventilator settings: lung-protective ventilation

- **Tidal volume 6 mL/kg predicted body weight (PBW)** (range 4–8), **plateau pressure <30 cmH₂O,** **driving pressure (plateau − PEEP) <15 cmH₂O,** **PEEP** titrated to oxygenation and compliance (5–15+ cmH₂O), **respiratory rate** to maintain adequate pH (permissive hypercapnia acceptable, pH >7.2), **FiO₂** lowest to keep SpO₂ 92–96% (88–92% in ARDS, per local protocol).
- **PBW (kg)** = 50 + 0.91 × (height in cm − 152.4) for men; 45.5 + 0.91 × (height in cm − 152.4) for women.

#### Acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS)

- **Berlin definition:** acute onset within **1 week** of a known clinical insult or new/worsening respiratory symptoms; **bilateral opacities** on chest imaging not fully explained by effusions, collapse or nodules; **respiratory failure not fully explained by cardiac failure or fluid overload;** **PaO₂/FiO₂ ≤300 mmHg** with PEEP ≥5 cmH₂O: **mild** (200–300), **moderate** (100–200), **severe** (≤100).
- **Causes:** pneumonia (including viral, COVID-19), sepsis, aspiration of gastric contents, trauma, pancreatitis, transfusion (TRALI), inhalation injury, drowning, drugs, burns.
- **Management:** treat the cause; **lung-protective ventilation;** **prone positioning for ≥12–16 hours/day** in moderate–severe ARDS (P/F <150) (reduces mortality); **conservative fluid strategy** once shock resolved; **early short-course neuromuscular blockade** in severe ARDS with high driving pressure; corticosteroids (dexamethasone) may benefit selected patients; **ECMO** referral for refractory hypoxaemia; avoid unnecessary sedation; early mobilisation; VTE prophylaxis; nutrition.

#### Ventilating patients with obstructive disease (asthma, COPD)

- **Risk of dynamic hyperinflation and auto-PEEP, barotrauma and hypotension:** use **low respiratory rate (8–10/min), low tidal volume, short inspiratory time/long expiratory time (I:E 1:4 or more), permissive hypercapnia,** minimal or no external PEEP in asthma; watch for **tension pneumothorax and hypotension;** if the patient suddenly deteriorates, **disconnect from the ventilator** to allow exhalation.
- Continue bronchodilators, steroids, magnesium, ketamine; consider ECMO.

#### Ventilator complications

Barotrauma (pneumothorax), ventilator-associated pneumonia, ventilator-induced lung injury, haemodynamic effects (reduced venous return), oxygen toxicity, tube-related injury, sedation-related delirium, diaphragmatic dysfunction, ICU-acquired weakness. Prevention: lung-protective settings, head-up 30°, oral hygiene, sedation holidays, early mobilisation, spontaneous breathing trials.

### 40.8 Specific causes and their management

#### Acute exacerbation of COPD with hypercapnia

- **Controlled oxygen** (Venturi 24–28%, target 88–92%); recheck ABG after 30–60 minutes; **nebulised salbutamol and ipratropium** (air-driven nebulisers if hypercapnic; oxygen by nasal cannulae in between), **oral prednisolone 30–40 mg for 5 days,** **antibiotics** (increased sputum purulence, volume, dyspnoea: amoxicillin, doxycycline, or per local guidance), treat precipitants (infection, heart failure, PE, pneumothorax), **NIV** if pH <7.35 and PaCO₂ >6.5 kPa persisting after one hour; **intubation** if NIV fails and the patient is suitable. **Consider the patient's wishes and ceiling of treatment** before escalation. Magnesium and aminophylline are rarely helpful.

#### Acute severe and life-threatening asthma

- **Severity (adult):** moderate (PEF 50–75%); **acute severe** (PEF 33–50%, RR ≥25, HR ≥110, cannot complete sentences); **life-threatening** (PEF <33%, SpO₂ <92%, PaO₂ <8 kPa, **"normal" PaCO₂ (4.6–6 kPa),** silent chest, cyanosis, poor respiratory effort, bradycardia, arrhythmia, hypotension, exhaustion, confusion, coma); **near-fatal** (raised PaCO₂ and/or need for ventilation).
- **Treatment:** **high-flow oxygen** (target 94–98%), **salbutamol 5 mg nebulised (continuous or repeated)** plus **ipratropium 0.5 mg,** **prednisolone 40–50 mg orally** (or IV hydrocortisone 100 mg), **IV magnesium sulphate 1.2–2 g over 20 minutes** (acute severe, life-threatening), **IV salbutamol or aminophylline** (selected), **ketamine** or sevoflurane in refractory cases; chest X-ray (exclude pneumothorax), ABG; **early senior and ICU involvement;** **intubation** if exhaustion, coma, or refractory hypoxia (ventilate with permissive hypercapnia, as above).
- **Discharge only when stable** (PEF >75% best/predicted, minimal symptoms), with steroid course, inhaler technique, written action plan, follow-up within 48 hours.

#### Acute pulmonary oedema

- Sit upright, oxygen, **CPAP/NIV,** IV loop diuretic (furosemide 40–80 mg), IV nitrate (if BP adequate), treat precipitant (ischaemia, arrhythmia, hypertension); morphine is no longer recommended routinely (Chapter 23).

#### Severe pneumonia and respiratory failure

- Oxygen, **antibiotics within 1 hour** if septic, **CPAP or HFNO** for hypoxia, early ICU if needing >60% oxygen or non-invasive support (CURB-65, SMART-COP, ATS/IDSA criteria for severe CAP), intubation for failure; treat complications (effusion, empyema); consider influenza and COVID-19.

#### Pulmonary embolism with hypoxia

- Oxygen, anticoagulation, thrombolysis if shock, avoid intubation unless necessary (cardiovascular collapse risk) (Chapter 45).

#### Pneumothorax and chest trauma

- Chest drain; tension pneumothorax needle decompression; flail chest: analgesia (epidural, paravertebral, serratus plane blocks), early NIV/ventilation if failing; haemothorax: drain, surgery.

#### Neuromuscular respiratory failure

- **Guillain–Barré syndrome and myasthenic crisis** can progress to respiratory failure over hours: monitor **FVC** (4–6 hourly and sitting/lying), **MIP,** bulbar function, and cough strength; **transfer to ICU/HDU** when **FVC <20 mL/kg** (or <1–1.5 L), **fall by >30% from baseline, MIP weaker than −30 cmH₂O, MEP <40 cmH₂O,** bulbar weakness, or inability to count to 20 on a single breath; **elective intubation** before hypercapnia and arrest; treat the cause (IVIG or plasma exchange for GBS and myasthenic crisis; anticholinesterase withheld in crisis; avoid drugs that worsen myasthenia, e.g. aminoglycosides, macrolides, fluoroquinolones, magnesium, beta-blockers). **Note:** blood gas abnormalities are late signs; do not wait for hypercapnia.
- **Motor neurone disease, muscular dystrophies, spinal cord injury (C3–C5):** NIV, cough assist, tracheostomy as per patient wishes.

#### Obesity hypoventilation syndrome (OHS)

- BMI >30, daytime hypercapnia (PaCO₂ >6 kPa), sleep-disordered breathing, daytime somnolence, hypoxia, polycythaemia, cor pulmonale. **Acute decompensation:** **controlled oxygen, NIV** (higher pressures), diuretics for cor pulmonale; long-term nocturnal NIV/CPAP and weight loss.

#### Opioid and sedative toxicity

- Support ventilation (bag-valve-mask), **naloxone** titrated to respiratory effort (0.4–2 mg IV, repeat; infusion for long-acting opioids), observe for re-narcotisation; **flumazenil** only in selected pure benzodiazepine overdoses.

#### Other

- **Aspiration pneumonitis,** **acute chest syndrome (sickle cell),** **inhalation injury and carbon monoxide poisoning** (100% oxygen, hyperbaric oxygen in selected cases), **cyanide poisoning** (hydroxocobalamin), **drowning,** **high-altitude pulmonary oedema** (descent, oxygen, nifedipine), **severe anaemia** (transfusion), **methaemoglobinaemia** (methylene blue).

---

### 40.9 Special populations

- **Infants and children:** signs of respiratory distress: **tachypnoea, grunting (expiratory, an attempt to maintain PEEP), nasal flaring, head bobbing, recession (intercostal, subcostal, sternal), tracheal tug, cyanosis, exhaustion with a slowing rate, apnoea (infants).** Common causes: **bronchiolitis** (infants <12 months; RSV; supportive care, HFNO, CPAP), **croup, asthma, pneumonia, epiglottitis (rare), foreign body, anaphylaxis, sepsis, cardiac (congenital heart disease),** and **neonatal respiratory distress syndrome** (surfactant, CPAP). Children **deteriorate quickly,** and **bradycardia and low conscious level are pre-arrest signs.** Use age-specific equipment, weight-based dosing, and PALS algorithms (Chapter 59).
- **Pregnancy:** physiological hyperventilation (normal PaCO₂ about 4.0 kPa), reduced FRC, faster desaturation; difficult airway (oedema, obesity); **target SpO₂ ≥95%** to maintain fetal oxygenation; left lateral tilt; consider PE, amniotic fluid embolism, peripartum cardiomyopathy, pneumonia (influenza), asthma, aspiration.
- **Older adults:** reduced reserve, atypical presentations (confusion), comorbidity; **discuss ceilings of treatment** (NIV vs intubation) early.
- **Patients with advanced chronic disease or at the end of life:** consider palliative approaches; opioids and benzodiazepines relieve breathlessness; NIV may help in selected patients as a trial; document treatment escalation plans and respect advance decisions.

### 40.10 Worked cases

#### Case 1: Hypercapnic COPD exacerbation

*A 72-year-old man with severe COPD, found drowsy, on 15 L/min oxygen by reservoir mask given by the ambulance crew. RR 14, HR 106, flapping tremor, bounding pulse. ABG on oxygen: pH 7.21, PaCO₂ 10.8 kPa (81 mmHg), PaO₂ 17 kPa (128 mmHg), HCO₃⁻ 32.*

**Reasoning.** **Acute-on-chronic respiratory acidosis** worsened by uncontrolled high-flow oxygen (loss of hypoxic drive, increased dead space).

**Action.** Switch to a **Venturi 28% mask** (target SpO₂ 88–92%), nebulised salbutamol/ipratropium (air-driven), prednisolone, antibiotics if infective; start **NIV** (pH <7.35, PaCO₂ >6.5 kPa); repeat ABG after 1 hour; intubate if worsening and appropriate; discuss ceiling of care; issue an oxygen alert card.

#### Case 2: Life-threatening asthma

*A 22-year-old woman with asthma has had 2 days of worsening symptoms. She cannot speak, is exhausted, RR 34, HR 140, SpO₂ 88% on oxygen, silent chest. PEF unrecordable. ABG: pH 7.28, PaCO₂ 6.5 kPa, PaO₂ 7.5 kPa.*

**Reasoning.** **Near-fatal asthma:** hypoxia, hypercapnia, silent chest, exhaustion.

**Action.** Senior and ICU help immediately; high-flow oxygen, continuous nebulised salbutamol/ipratropium, IV hydrocortisone, **IV magnesium,** IV salbutamol, chest X-ray, preparation for **intubation** by the most experienced operator (ketamine induction, large tube), then ventilate with low rate and long expiratory time; watch for hypotension and pneumothorax.

#### Case 3: Epiglottitis in an adult

*A 38-year-old man has a 12-hour history of severe sore throat, muffled voice, inability to swallow his saliva, and fever. He sits forward drooling, with soft inspiratory stridor.*

**Reasoning.** **Acute epiglottitis** with airway at risk.

**Action.** **Do not examine the throat or lay him flat;** high-flow oxygen; call anaesthetist and ENT; prepare for **intubation in theatre with a surgical airway back-up;** after the airway is secured: IV ceftriaxone, steroids, blood cultures.

#### Case 4: Post-thyroidectomy neck haematoma

*A woman, 4 hours after total thyroidectomy, develops neck swelling, stridor and increasing distress; SpO₂ 90%.*

**Reasoning.** **Expanding haematoma compressing the airway** (venous obstruction and laryngeal oedema): surgical emergency.

**Action.** **Open the wound at the bedside immediately** (remove skin clips and sutures, evacuate clot, release strap muscles), high-flow oxygen, call surgeon and anaesthetist, intubate if needed (laryngeal oedema may persist after decompression), return to theatre.

#### Case 5: Guillain–Barré syndrome

*A 40-year-old man 2 weeks after a diarrhoeal illness has ascending weakness, areflexia, and difficulty swallowing. FVC 1.6 L (about 20 mL/kg, down from 2.9 L yesterday). SpO₂ 95% on air; ABG normal.*

**Reasoning.** **GBS with impending neuromuscular respiratory failure** (falling FVC, bulbar involvement) despite normal gases.

**Action.** Transfer to ICU, intubate electively if FVC <15 mL/kg or deteriorating, **IVIG or plasma exchange,** monitor autonomic instability, VTE prophylaxis, nutrition.

#### Case 6: ARDS from pneumonia

*A 55-year-old man with severe pneumonia becomes more hypoxic over 36 hours: bilateral infiltrates on chest X-ray, PaO₂ 8.5 kPa on FiO₂ 0.8 (P/F 80 mmHg), PEEP 12, normal echo.*

**Reasoning.** **Severe ARDS** (P/F ≤100).

**Action.** Lung-protective ventilation (6 mL/kg PBW, plateau <30), **prone positioning** for 16 hours/day, neuromuscular blockade, restrictive fluids, treat infection, consider ECMO referral if refractory, dexamethasone, careful sedation and early mobilisation when stable.

#### Case 7: Obesity hypoventilation

*A 54-year-old man, BMI 48, is admitted with ankle oedema, morning headaches, daytime sleepiness and breathlessness. ABG on air: pH 7.32, PaCO₂ 8.2 kPa, PaO₂ 6.5 kPa, HCO₃⁻ 36. Hb 190.*

**Reasoning.** **Acute-on-chronic hypercapnic respiratory failure due to OHS,** with cor pulmonale.

**Action.** **Controlled oxygen (88–92%),** **NIV** with adequate pressures, diuretics, treat precipitant (infection), then long-term nocturnal NIV/CPAP, weight loss support, sleep study, venesection not required unless severe.

#### Case 8: Opioid overdose

*A 31-year-old man is found unresponsive; RR 5, SpO₂ 80%, pupils 1 mm. ABG: pH 7.18, PaCO₂ 9.5 kPa.*

**Reasoning.** **Opioid-induced hypoventilation** (type 2 failure).

**Action.** Airway positioning, **bag-valve-mask ventilation,** **naloxone** (0.4 mg IV, repeat/infusion), prolonged observation, assess for aspiration and co-ingestants.

### 40.11 Common pitfalls

- **Relying on SpO₂ alone,** missing hypercapnia (and the reverse: giving oxygen to correct a "low SpO₂" in a patient with hypoventilation).
- **Giving uncontrolled high-flow oxygen** to COPD patients, or **withholding oxygen** from hypoxic patients because of fear of CO₂ retention.
- **Interpreting a "normal" PaCO₂ in a tiring asthmatic as reassuring.**
- **Waiting for blood gas deterioration** in neuromuscular disease before intubation.
- **Using NIV when contraindicated,** failing to set a **treatment ceiling,** or **persisting with failing NIV.**
- **Examining the throat of a child with suspected epiglottitis,** or agitating a child with stridor.
- **Delay in surgical airway** in a CICO situation (fixation on repeated intubation attempts).
- **Intubating the physiologically unprepared patient** (hypoxic, hypotensive).
- **Failing to use waveform capnography** to confirm tube position.
- **Ventilating asthmatics and COPD patients with high rates** (auto-PEEP, haemodynamic collapse).
- **Ventilating ARDS with large tidal volumes.**
- **Failing to consider pneumothorax, PE, pulmonary oedema, and aspiration** as treatable causes.
- **Neglecting the patient's wishes** and prognosis in chronic lung disease.
- **Forgetting to remove sutures in neck haematoma.**

---

### 40.12 Key points

- Type 1 failure: PaO₂ <8 kPa with normal/low PaCO₂; type 2: PaCO₂ >6 kPa with respiratory acidosis in acute failure. Interpret gases systematically, distinguishing acute from chronic.
- Hypoxia: agitation, confusion, tachycardia, cyanosis; hypercapnia: headache, flap, bounding pulse, drowsiness; exhaustion and silent chest are pre-arrest signs.
- Target SpO₂ 94–98% (88–92% if at risk of hypercapnia); avoid both hypoxia and hyperoxia; use Venturi masks for controlled oxygen.
- NIV is first-line for COPD hypercapnic acidosis, and CPAP/NIV for cardiogenic pulmonary oedema; set ceilings and reassess at 1 hour.
- Upper airway obstruction: stridor, drooling, voice change; call senior airway help; avoid agitation; prepare for surgical airway; know the CICO plan.
- Safe intubation requires preparation, pre-oxygenation, physiological optimisation, capnography confirmation, and a plan B, C and D.
- Ventilate lungs protectively (6 mL/kg PBW, plateau <30), prone positioning in severe ARDS, and low rate with long expiration in obstructive disease.
- In neuromuscular failure, follow FVC and MIP; gases are late.

### 40.13 Self-assessment

1. Define type 1 and type 2 respiratory failure with ABG criteria, and give two causes of each.
   *Answer: Type 1: PaO₂ <8 kPa (60 mmHg) with normal or low PaCO₂ (pneumonia, pulmonary oedema, ARDS, PE); type 2: PaCO₂ >6 kPa (45 mmHg) with hypoxia and acidosis in acute failure (COPD exacerbation, neuromuscular disease, opioid overdose, obesity hypoventilation).*

2. What target saturation range and mask would you use for a patient with COPD in acute exacerbation?
   *Answer: 88–92%, initially with a Venturi mask (24–28%), titrated by blood gases.*

3. List the criteria to start NIV in a COPD exacerbation and the criteria for failure.
   *Answer: pH <7.35 and PaCO₂ >6.5 kPa after an hour of optimal medical treatment; failure: worsening pH or PaCO₂, persisting hypoxia, exhaustion, falling conscious level, inability to tolerate, with intubation if within the ceiling of care.*

4. Describe the immediate management of suspected adult epiglottitis.
   *Answer: Keep the patient upright and calm, avoid examining the throat, give oxygen, call senior anaesthetic and ENT help, secure the airway in theatre with a surgical airway back-up, then IV antibiotics and steroids.*

5. State the lung-protective ventilator settings for ARDS.
   *Answer: Tidal volume about 6 mL/kg predicted body weight, plateau pressure <30 cmH₂O, driving pressure <15, PEEP titrated, permissive hypercapnia, prone positioning for severe disease.*

6. Why is blood gas analysis unreliable for deciding when to intubate a patient with Guillain–Barré syndrome?
   *Answer: Hypercapnia and hypoxia appear late; monitor FVC, MIP and bulbar function and intubate before respiratory failure develops.*
