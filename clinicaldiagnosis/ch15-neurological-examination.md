## Chapter 15. Neurological Examination

### Learning objectives

After studying this chapter you should be able to:

1. Perform a screening neurological examination and a detailed examination guided by the history.
2. Score the Glasgow Coma Scale and describe the level of consciousness precisely.
3. Test each cranial nerve, recognise the common patterns of abnormality, and interpret fundoscopy.
4. Examine tone, power, reflexes and coordination, and distinguish upper from lower motor neurone lesions.
5. Test sensory modalities and map sensory loss to dermatomes, peripheral nerves and spinal cord patterns.
6. Recognise characteristic gaits and cerebellar signs.
7. Localise a lesion anatomically (cortex, subcortical, brainstem, cerebellum, spinal cord, root, plexus, nerve, neuromuscular junction, muscle).
8. Identify neurological emergencies at the bedside: stroke, meningitis, raised intracranial pressure, cord compression and cauda equina syndrome.

---

### 15.1 Principles: the examination serves localisation

Neurological diagnosis proceeds in two steps:

1. **Where is the lesion?** (anatomical localisation, from history and examination)
2. **What is the lesion?** (pathological process, from the time course and associated features)

The examination answers the first question. The **time course** from the history answers much of the second: sudden onset suggests a vascular event; evolution over hours to days suggests inflammation or infection; over weeks to months suggests tumour or demyelination in relapses; over years suggests degeneration; episodic with recovery suggests migraine, epilepsy or TIA.

#### Practical advice

- **Take the history first and form hypotheses**, then choose the targeted examination. A full "textbook" neurological examination takes 30+ minutes and is rarely needed; a **screening examination** (about 5 minutes) is used for patients without neurological complaints or when the problem is vague.
- **Compare left and right**, and arm and leg. Asymmetry is the most reliable sign.
- **Describe what you see** (e.g., "power 4/5 in right hip flexion") rather than "weakness."
- **Ask what the patient can do**: dressing, buttons, stairs, rising from a chair, speech, reading.
- **Observe from the moment the patient enters**: gait, posture, speech, facial expression, spontaneous movements, arm swing.

### 15.2 Higher functions and consciousness

#### Level of consciousness: AVPU and the Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS)

**AVPU** (Alert, Voice, Pain, Unresponsive) is a rapid screening tool. For a more detailed assessment, use the **Glasgow Coma Scale**:

| Component | Response | Score |
|---|---|---|
| **Eye opening (E)** | Spontaneous | 4 |
| | To voice | 3 |
| | To pain | 2 |
| | None | 1 |
| **Verbal (V)** | Oriented | 5 |
| | Confused | 4 |
| | Inappropriate words | 3 |
| | Incomprehensible sounds | 2 |
| | None | 1 |
| **Motor (M)** | Obeys commands | 6 |
| | Localises to pain | 5 |
| | Withdraws from pain | 4 |
| | Abnormal flexion (decorticate) | 3 |
| | Extension (decerebrate) | 2 |
| | None | 1 |

Total 3–15. **GCS ≤8** generally indicates a need to secure the airway. Record components separately ("E3 V4 M5 = 12") and use the **best motor response** and, in hemiparesis, the best limb. Apply a **central stimulus** (trapezius squeeze, supraorbital pressure) to test pain responses; avoid sternal rub (bruising).

#### Quick bedside cognitive screen

Orientation (time, place, person), attention (months of the year backwards, serial 7s), registration and recall of three words, naming, repetition, and following commands. Chapter 20 describes formal assessment (MoCA, MMSE, 4AT).

#### Speech and language

- **Dysarthria:** abnormal articulation (slurred), with normal language content. Causes: cerebellar disease, bulbar palsy, pseudobulbar palsy, extrapyramidal disease, facial weakness.
- **Dysphonia:** abnormal voice quality (hoarse, whispering): vocal cord or recurrent laryngeal nerve problems.
- **Dysphasia (aphasia):** abnormal language, localising to the dominant hemisphere (left in most people).
  - **Broca (expressive):** non-fluent, effortful speech, good comprehension; lesion in the inferior frontal gyrus.
  - **Wernicke (receptive):** fluent, meaningless speech with poor comprehension; lesion in the superior temporal gyrus.
  - **Conduction:** poor repetition with fluent speech and intact comprehension.
  - **Global:** all aspects impaired.
- **Other cortical signs:** apraxia (inability to perform a learned movement despite intact power), agnosia, neglect (parietal lobe, usually right: ignores the left side of space), visual field defects, acalculia, agraphia, finger agnosia and left-right disorientation (Gerstmann syndrome, dominant parietal lobe).

### 15.3 Cranial nerves

Work through each in order. Lesions are classified as **upper motor neurone (UMN)** or **lower motor neurone (LMN)** where relevant.

#### I: Olfactory

Test each nostril with a familiar smell (coffee, soap), not irritants like ammonia. **Anosmia:** head injury (shearing of olfactory fibres), viral infection (including COVID-19), frontal tumour/meningioma (Foster Kennedy syndrome with optic atrophy), Parkinson disease, smoking, nasal disease.

#### II: Optic

- **Visual acuity:** Snellen chart at 6 m (each eye separately, with glasses); if poor, count fingers, hand movements, light perception.
- **Colour vision:** Ishihara plates (red desaturation is an early sign of optic neuritis).
- **Visual fields by confrontation:** Sit at eye level, 1 m away; cover one eye each; test the four quadrants with a moving finger or hat pin, comparing with your own field. Test for **visual neglect** with both hands simultaneously.
- **Pupils:** size, symmetry, shape, reaction to light (direct and consensual) and accommodation. **Relative afferent pupillary defect (RAPD, Marcus Gunn pupil):** swing a light between the eyes; the affected pupil dilates when light shines on it (the afferent pathway is impaired); optic neuritis, ischaemic or compressive optic neuropathy, severe retinal disease.
- **Fundoscopy:** optic disc (colour, margins, cup), vessels, macula, retinal background.

| Visual field defect | Lesion |
|---|---|
| **Monocular blindness** | Retina, optic nerve (amaurosis fugax, optic neuritis, central retinal artery occlusion, giant cell arteritis) |
| **Bitemporal hemianopia** | Optic chiasm (pituitary tumour, craniopharyngioma) |
| **Homonymous hemianopia** | Optic tract, radiation or occipital cortex contralateral to the field loss (stroke, tumour) |
| **Homonymous quadrantanopia** ("pie in the sky") | Temporal lobe (Meyer's loop) lesion: upper quadrants |
| **Homonymous lower quadrantanopia** ("pie on the floor") | Parietal lobe lesion |
| **Homonymous hemianopia with macular sparing** | Posterior cerebral artery occlusion |
| **Central scotoma** | Optic neuritis, macular disease |
| **Tunnel vision** | Glaucoma, retinitis pigmentosa |

**Papilloedema** (swollen optic disc with blurred margins, loss of venous pulsation, haemorrhages, enlarged blind spot) signals raised intracranial pressure. Causes: mass lesions, idiopathic intracranial hypertension, cerebral venous thrombosis, malignant hypertension, meningitis. Visual acuity is usually normal until late. Differentiate from **optic neuritis** (reduced acuity, painful eye movement, RAPD, disc swollen in a minority).

#### III, IV, VI: Oculomotor, trochlear, abducens

- **Inspect** for ptosis, eye position (squint/strabismus), and pupils.
- **Eye movements:** ask the patient to follow your finger in an "H" pattern, keeping the head still; ask about double vision (diplopia) and watch for nystagmus.
- **Third nerve palsy:** eye deviates **down and out**, ptosis, dilated, poorly reactive pupil (parasympathetic fibres run on the surface of the nerve). A **painful third nerve palsy with a dilated pupil** is a **posterior communicating artery aneurysm** until excluded (emergency). A **pupil-sparing** third nerve palsy suggests microvascular ischaemia (diabetes, hypertension).
- **Fourth nerve palsy:** vertical diplopia, worse looking down (reading, stairs), head tilt away from the affected side.
- **Sixth nerve palsy:** horizontal diplopia, inability to abduct the eye; a "false localising sign" in raised intracranial pressure; also diabetes, MS, Wernicke's encephalopathy.
- **Internuclear ophthalmoplegia (INO):** impaired adduction of one eye with nystagmus in the abducting eye on lateral gaze; lesion in the medial longitudinal fasciculus; classically multiple sclerosis in young adults, or brainstem stroke in older patients.
- **Horner syndrome:** ptosis (partial), miosis, anhidrosis, enophthalmos. Lesion anywhere on the sympathetic pathway: central (brainstem stroke, lateral medullary syndrome), preganglionic (Pancoast tumour, neck trauma, carotid dissection), postganglionic (cavernous sinus, carotid dissection).
- **Nystagmus:** involuntary rhythmic eye movements; named by the fast phase. Horizontal gaze-evoked nystagmus is common with drugs (alcohol, anticonvulsants) and cerebellar disease. Vertical nystagmus suggests brainstem disease.
- **Conjugate gaze palsy:** eyes deviate toward the side of a destructive hemispheric lesion (stroke) and away from the side of an irritative focus (seizure).

#### V: Trigeminal

- **Sensory:** test light touch (cotton wool) and pin prick in the three divisions (V1 ophthalmic, V2 maxillary, V3 mandibular).
- **Motor:** clench the jaw and palpate masseters and temporalis; open the jaw against resistance (jaw deviates toward the weak side).
- **Reflexes:** corneal reflex (V afferent, VII efferent); jaw jerk (increased in bilateral UMN lesions, pseudobulbar palsy).
- **Trigeminal neuralgia:** paroxysms of stabbing pain in a division (usually V2 or V3), triggered by touch or chewing; normal neurological examination.

#### VII: Facial

- **Inspect** for asymmetry, nasolabial fold flattening, drooping of the mouth.
- **Motor:** ask the patient to raise the eyebrows, close the eyes tightly (try to open them), show the teeth, puff the cheeks, whistle.
- **UMN vs LMN:** the forehead receives bilateral cortical innervation; therefore a **UMN lesion (stroke)** spares the forehead; an **LMN lesion (Bell palsy, parotid tumour, Ramsay Hunt syndrome)** affects the entire half of the face including the forehead.
- **Other features of LMN palsy:** hyperacusis (stapedius), loss of taste in the anterior two thirds of the tongue (chorda tympani), reduced tearing; vesicles in the ear canal suggest Ramsay Hunt syndrome (herpes zoster oticus).

#### VIII: Vestibulocochlear

- **Hearing:** whisper test, finger rub; **Rinne** (air versus bone conduction) and **Weber** tests with a 512 Hz tuning fork (Chapter 17).
- **Vestibular:** nystagmus, head impulse test, Dix–Hallpike manoeuvre, gait and Romberg (Chapter 24).

#### IX, X: Glossopharyngeal and vagus

- **Palate:** ask the patient to say "ah"; the uvula deviates **away** from a vagal lesion.
- **Gag reflex** (rarely tested; unreliable).
- **Voice:** hoarse or nasal.
- **Swallowing:** observe the patient swallow water (bedside swallow screen) in suspected stroke or bulbar disease.
- **Bulbar palsy (LMN):** flaccid, wasted fasciculating tongue, nasal speech, nasal regurgitation, weak cough (motor neurone disease, Guillain–Barré, myasthenia gravis, brainstem stroke).
- **Pseudobulbar palsy (UMN):** spastic tongue, slow "Donald Duck" speech, brisk jaw jerk, emotional lability (bilateral hemispheric strokes, motor neurone disease, multiple sclerosis).

#### XI: Accessory

- Shrug the shoulders against resistance (trapezius); turn the head against resistance (sternocleidomastoid; the muscle opposite the side of turn). Weakness: neck surgery, trauma, motor neurone disease.

#### XII: Hypoglossal

- Inspect the tongue at rest (wasting, fasciculation) and on protrusion. The tongue deviates **toward the side of a lower motor neurone lesion.** A small, spastic tongue indicates UMN disease.

### 15.4 The motor system

#### Inspection

- **Bulk:** wasting (LMN lesions, disuse, cachexia), hypertrophy (pseudohypertrophy in Duchenne muscular dystrophy).
- **Fasciculations:** brief visible twitches of muscle fascicles, at rest (LMN: motor neurone disease, radiculopathy); benign fasciculations are common and not accompanied by weakness.
- **Abnormal movements:** tremor, chorea, dystonia, myoclonus, tics, hemiballismus.

#### Tone

Assess with the patient relaxed, by passively moving the limb (flexing and extending the elbow, wrist, knee, ankle) at varying speeds.

| Abnormality | Description | Cause |
|---|---|---|
| **Spasticity** (clasp-knife) | Velocity-dependent resistance, with sudden release | UMN lesion (stroke, spinal cord disease, MS) |
| **Rigidity (lead-pipe)** | Constant resistance throughout the range | Parkinson disease, neuroleptic malignant syndrome |
| **Cogwheel rigidity** | Ratchet-like, with tremor superimposed | Parkinson disease |
| **Hypotonia** | Floppy | LMN lesion, cerebellar disease, acute spinal cord shock, muscle disease |
| **Gegenhalten (paratonia)** | Variable resistance that matches the force applied | Frontal lobe disease, dementia |
| **Clonus** | Rhythmic contractions on sudden stretch (dorsiflex the foot; >3 beats is sustained) | UMN lesion |

#### Power

Test the major muscle groups against resistance, comparing the sides. Grade with the **Medical Research Council (MRC) scale**:

| Grade | Description |
|---|---|
| 0 | No contraction |
| 1 | Flicker of contraction |
| 2 | Movement with gravity eliminated |
| 3 | Movement against gravity |
| 4 | Movement against gravity and some resistance (4-, 4, 4+ subdivisions) |
| 5 | Normal power |

**Pyramidal pattern of weakness (UMN):** in the arm, extensors are weaker than flexors; in the leg, flexors weaker than extensors (the extremity "posture" is flexed arm and extended leg in hemiplegic stroke).

**Pronator drift:** ask the patient to hold both arms outstretched, palms up, eyes closed, for 20–30 seconds. Pronation and downward drift of one arm indicates a subtle UMN weakness (also seen with parietal lesions, which cause updrift). Cerebellar disease causes drift upward.

**Common muscle tests and myotomes**

| Movement | Muscle / myotome (root) |
|---|---|
| Shoulder abduction | Deltoid, supraspinatus (C5) |
| Elbow flexion | Biceps (C5–6) |
| Elbow extension | Triceps (C7) |
| Wrist extension | Radial nerve (C6–7) |
| Finger extension | C7 |
| Finger flexion | C8 |
| Finger abduction | T1 (ulnar nerve) |
| Thumb abduction | Median nerve, C8–T1 |
| Hip flexion | Iliopsoas (L1–2) |
| Hip adduction | L2–3 |
| Hip extension | Gluteus maximus (L5–S1) |
| Knee extension | Quadriceps (L3–4) |
| Knee flexion | Hamstrings (L5–S1) |
| Ankle dorsiflexion | Tibialis anterior (L4–5) |
| Great toe extension | L5 |
| Ankle plantar flexion | Gastrocnemius (S1–2) |
| Ankle eversion | L5–S1 |
| Ankle inversion | L4–5 |

**Functional tests:** rising from a chair without using the arms (proximal leg), walking on heels (L4–5) and toes (S1), hopping.

**Patterns:**

- **Hemiparesis:** one side of the body (stroke).
- **Paraparesis:** both legs (spinal cord, cauda equina, parasagittal lesions, bilateral neuropathy).
- **Quadriparesis:** all four limbs (cervical cord, Guillain–Barré, brainstem, myopathy).
- **Proximal weakness (difficulty with stairs, rising from chairs, combing hair):** myopathy, polymyositis, thyroid disease, steroid-induced myopathy.
- **Distal weakness:** neuropathy.
- **Fatigable weakness:** myasthenia gravis (ptosis worsens with sustained upgaze; weakness increases with repetition).

### 15.5 Reflexes

Use a reflex hammer and ensure the patient is relaxed. Compare the two sides. Use reinforcement (Jendrassik manoeuvre: patient clenches teeth or hooks fingers together) for reflexes that are hard to elicit.

| Reflex | Root | Technique |
|---|---|---|
| **Biceps** | C5–6 | Place your thumb on the biceps tendon, strike your thumb |
| **Supinator (brachioradialis)** | C5–6 | Strike the lower radius ~5 cm above the wrist |
| **Triceps** | C7 | Strike the tendon above the olecranon with the arm flexed |
| **Knee** | L3–4 | Strike the patellar tendon |
| **Ankle** | S1 | Strike the Achilles tendon with the foot dorsiflexed |
| **Plantar response** | S1 | Run a stick up the lateral border of the sole, then across the ball of the foot |

**Grading:** 0 absent; 1+ diminished; 2+ normal; 3+ brisk; 4+ very brisk with clonus.

**Plantar response (Babinski sign):** the normal response is flexion (downgoing) of the great toe; an **extensor response (upgoing toe, with fanning of the other toes)** indicates an **upper motor neurone lesion.** In infants under about one year, an upgoing plantar is normal.

#### UMN versus LMN

| Feature | Upper motor neurone | Lower motor neurone |
|---|---|---|
| Tone | Increased (spastic) | Decreased (flaccid) |
| Power | Weakness in a pyramidal pattern | Weakness in the muscle(s) supplied by the nerve/root |
| Wasting | Minimal or late (disuse) | Marked, early |
| Fasciculations | Absent | May be present |
| Reflexes | Brisk, clonus | Reduced or absent |
| Plantar | Extensor | Flexor |
| Examples | Stroke, MS, cord compression | Peripheral neuropathy, radiculopathy, anterior horn cell disease, Guillain–Barré |

In **acute** UMN lesions (acute stroke, acute cord transection), there may be initial flaccidity and areflexia (**spinal shock** or cerebral diaschisis) before spasticity and hyper-reflexia develop over days to weeks.

**Mixed UMN and LMN signs** (e.g., wasted fasciculating arms with brisk reflexes in the legs and extensor plantars) suggest **motor neurone disease** or a combination of cervical myelopathy and radiculopathy.

### 15.6 Sensory examination

Test with the patient's eyes closed, comparing sides, distal to proximal, in a pattern guided by the history. Demonstrate each test first with eyes open.

| Modality | Test | Pathway |
|---|---|---|
| **Light touch** | Cotton wool | Dorsal columns and spinothalamic |
| **Pain (pinprick)** | Neurotip or disposable pin | **Spinothalamic tract** (crosses in the cord, a few segments above entry) |
| **Temperature** | Cold object (tuning fork) | Spinothalamic |
| **Vibration** | 128 Hz tuning fork on bony prominences (great toe IP joint, medial malleolus, tibial tuberosity, finger tips) | **Dorsal columns** (ipsilateral; cross in the medulla) |
| **Joint position sense** | Move the distal phalanx up/down by holding the sides | Dorsal columns |
| **Two-point discrimination, stereognosis, graphaesthesia** | Cortical sensory functions | Parietal lobe |
| **Romberg test** | Stand with feet together, eyes open then closed; unsteadiness only with eyes closed = sensory ataxia (dorsal column loss or proprioceptive neuropathy); instability with eyes open = cerebellar | Joint position sense |

**Sensory inattention (extinction):** the patient detects a stimulus on either side when tested separately but misses the contralateral one when both sides are touched simultaneously (parietal lesion).

#### Dermatomes (key landmarks)

| Level | Landmark |
|---|---|
| C5 | Lateral upper arm (deltoid patch) |
| C6 | Thumb |
| C7 | Middle finger |
| C8 | Little finger |
| T4 | Nipple |
| T10 | Umbilicus |
| L1 | Inguinal region |
| L3 | Anterior thigh/knee |
| L4 | Medial malleolus, medial leg |
| L5 | Dorsum of foot, great toe |
| S1 | Lateral foot, little toe, sole |
| S2–S4 | Perineum, saddle area |

#### Patterns of sensory loss

| Pattern | Likely site |
|---|---|
| **Glove-and-stocking** (distal, symmetrical, starting in the feet) | Peripheral polyneuropathy (diabetes, alcohol, B12 deficiency, drugs, uraemia, hypothyroidism) |
| **Dermatomal** | Nerve root (radiculopathy: disc, herpes zoster) |
| **Peripheral nerve distribution** | Mononeuropathy (median in carpal tunnel: thumb, index, middle, radial half of ring finger; ulnar: little finger and ulnar half of ring; lateral femoral cutaneous nerve: meralgia paraesthetica) |
| **Sensory level on the trunk** | Spinal cord lesion (compression, transverse myelitis) |
| **Hemisensory loss (face, arm, leg)** | Thalamus or contralateral cortex |
| **Crossed (face one side, body the other)** | Brainstem lesion (lateral medullary syndrome) |
| **Dissociated sensory loss** (pain and temperature lost, light touch and vibration preserved), "cape" distribution over the shoulders | Syringomyelia (central cord lesion crossing fibres) |
| **Saddle anaesthesia** | Cauda equina or conus medullaris |

#### Spinal cord syndromes

- **Brown-Séquard (hemisection):** **ipsilateral** weakness (UMN) and loss of dorsal column function (vibration, proprioception) below the lesion; **contralateral** loss of pain and temperature beginning a few segments below the lesion.
- **Anterior cord syndrome:** motor loss, loss of pain and temperature below the level; dorsal column function preserved (anterior spinal artery infarct).
- **Central cord syndrome:** greater weakness in the arms than the legs, variable sensory loss (hyperextension injury in cervical spondylosis).
- **Posterior column syndrome:** loss of vibration and proprioception, sensory ataxia (B12 deficiency, tabes dorsalis).
- **Cauda equina syndrome:** low back pain, bilateral sciatica or leg weakness, saddle anaesthesia, urinary retention/overflow incontinence, loss of anal tone, faecal incontinence; LMN signs in the legs. **A surgical emergency** (Chapter 29).

### 15.7 Coordination and cerebellar signs

Cerebellar disease causes **ipsilateral** incoordination. Remember **DANISH**:

- **D**ysdiadochokinesia (impaired rapid alternating movements, e.g., pronation/supination of the hand).
- **A**taxia (gait or trunk; truncal ataxia in midline lesions).
- **N**ystagmus (gaze-evoked, toward the side of the lesion).
- **I**ntention tremor (finger-to-nose test: tremor increases on approach to target) and **past-pointing** (dysmetria).
- **S**lurred (scanning, staccato) speech.
- **H**ypotonia (with pendular reflexes).

Tests: finger-to-nose, heel-to-shin, rapid alternating movements, rebound, tandem gait, check for truncal sway when sitting.

**Causes:** alcohol, drugs (phenytoin, carbamazepine), multiple sclerosis, stroke, tumour (including metastases and paraneoplastic degeneration), hypothyroidism, inherited ataxias, vitamin deficiency (B1, B12, E). **Acute cerebellar ataxia with headache, vomiting and decreased consciousness** may indicate cerebellar haemorrhage, infarction or posterior fossa mass; an emergency.

### 15.8 Gait

Watch the patient walk, turn and walk back. Ask them to walk on heels and toes, and tandem (heel-to-toe). Common abnormal gaits:

| Gait | Description | Cause |
|---|---|---|
| **Hemiplegic** | Arm flexed, leg extended and circumducted | Stroke |
| **Spastic (scissoring)** | Stiff legs, crossing | Spinal cord disease, cerebral palsy |
| **Parkinsonian** | Shuffling, short steps, stooped posture, reduced arm swing, difficulty turning ("en bloc"), festination, freezing | Parkinson disease, drug-induced parkinsonism |
| **Cerebellar (ataxic)** | Wide-based, staggering, "drunken" | Cerebellar lesion, alcohol |
| **Sensory ataxic** | Stamping, watching the feet; worse in the dark | Dorsal column / large-fibre neuropathy |
| **Foot drop (steppage)** | High stepping to clear a dropped foot | Common peroneal nerve palsy, L5 radiculopathy, peripheral neuropathy |
| **Waddling (Trendelenburg)** | Pelvis dips on the swing side | Proximal myopathy, hip pathology |
| **Antalgic** | Shortened stance on the painful side | Pain from hip, knee, foot |
| **Apraxic (magnetic)** | Feet appear stuck to the floor, shuffling, wide-based | Normal pressure hydrocephalus, frontal lobe disease, vascular dementia |
| **Functional** | Inconsistent, bizarre, dramatic, variable with distraction | Functional neurological disorder |

### 15.9 Meningeal irritation and raised intracranial pressure

#### Meningism

- **Neck stiffness** (resistance to passive flexion, normal passive rotation).
- **Kernig sign:** with the hip flexed to 90°, the knee cannot be extended fully because of pain/hamstring spasm.
- **Brudzinski sign:** passive neck flexion causes involuntary hip/knee flexion.
- **Photophobia, headache, vomiting.**

These signs are **insensitive**; their absence does not exclude meningitis, especially in infants, older adults and the immunosuppressed. Fever, headache, altered consciousness and a non-blanching rash should prompt immediate antibiotics (Chapter 41).

#### Raised intracranial pressure

Headache (worse lying down, on waking, coughing), vomiting, papilloedema, sixth-nerve palsy, drowsiness, **Cushing response** (hypertension, bradycardia, irregular breathing; late), pupil dilatation (uncal herniation compressing the third nerve), decorticate/decerebrate posturing. **Never perform a lumbar puncture** in a patient with signs of raised ICP or focal deficits without prior imaging.

### 15.10 Localising the lesion

Reasoning from signs to site:

| If you find… | Think… |
|---|---|
| Cortical signs (aphasia, neglect, apraxia, visual field defect, seizures) | Cerebral cortex |
| Contralateral face, arm, leg weakness + homonymous hemianopia + aphasia/neglect | Middle cerebral artery territory (cortical) |
| Pure contralateral motor hemiparesis (face, arm, leg equally), no cortical signs | Internal capsule / pons (lacunar stroke) |
| Ipsilateral cranial nerve palsy + contralateral hemiparesis | Brainstem |
| Vertigo, nystagmus, ataxia, dysphagia, Horner, ipsilateral facial pain/numbness + contralateral body pain/temperature loss | Lateral medullary syndrome (PICA / vertebral artery) |
| Ipsilateral limb ataxia | Cerebellar hemisphere |
| Bilateral leg UMN signs + sensory level | Spinal cord |
| LMN signs + dermatomal sensory loss + pain | Nerve root |
| Distal symmetrical sensory loss + LMN signs + absent ankle reflexes | Peripheral neuropathy |
| Fatigable weakness, ptosis, diplopia, normal reflexes and sensation | Neuromuscular junction (myasthenia gravis) |
| Proximal weakness, normal sensation, normal or reduced reflexes | Muscle (myopathy) |
| Tremor at rest, bradykinesia, rigidity | Basal ganglia (Parkinson) |

### 15.11 Stroke at the bedside

- **FAST** (Face drooping, Arm weakness, Speech difficulty, Time to call emergency services): community tool. Extended tools include BE-FAST (Balance, Eyes).
- **Recognise stroke mimics** (hypoglycaemia, seizure with Todd paresis, migraine aura, Bell palsy, functional, subdural). **Check glucose in every patient with suspected stroke.**
- **NIHSS (National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale):** a standardised 0–42 score of deficits; used for severity and eligibility for thrombolysis/thrombectomy.
- **Time is brain:** onset time or "last known well" determines eligibility for reperfusion therapy. Urgent CT head (to exclude haemorrhage), CT angiography for large vessel occlusion.
- **Bedside swallow screen** before oral intake.
- **TIA** is a stroke warning: transient (typically <1 hour) deficit with full recovery; urgent assessment is needed because of the high early stroke risk.

### 15.12 Worked cases

#### Case 1: Left MCA stroke

*A 74-year-old man with atrial fibrillation develops sudden speech difficulty and right-sided weakness 90 minutes before arrival. He is alert but non-fluent, obeys simple commands, with right facial droop sparing the forehead, right arm power 1/5 and right leg 3/5, right homonymous hemianopia, brisk right reflexes and an upgoing right plantar. Blood glucose 6.8 mmol/L.*

**Localisation.** Left hemisphere (right-sided deficits) with aphasia (dominant hemisphere), visual field loss and cortical signs: **left middle cerebral artery territory infarct,** probably cardioembolic from AF.

**Action.** Activate the stroke pathway immediately, urgent non-contrast CT (exclude haemorrhage) and CT angiography; if eligible, **thrombolysis** and/or **mechanical thrombectomy** (time-dependent). Monitor airway, swallowing, blood pressure, glucose.

#### Case 2: Bell palsy versus stroke

*A 38-year-old woman wakes with her right face drooping. She cannot close the right eye, raise the right eyebrow, or smile on the right. There is pain behind the ear the day before. Arm and leg power, speech, sensation and reflexes are normal. No ear vesicles.*

**Localisation.** Entire half of the face including the forehead = **LMN facial palsy** (Bell palsy). A stroke would spare the forehead.

**Management.** Oral corticosteroids started early (within 72 hours); eye protection (lubricant, taping at night); review if no recovery by 3 weeks. Look for vesicles (Ramsay Hunt), parotid mass, bilateral palsy (Lyme disease, sarcoidosis, Guillain–Barré), and other cranial nerve involvement.

#### Case 3: Painful third nerve palsy

*A 52-year-old woman has a severe headache and double vision. The right eye is deviated down and out with complete ptosis; the right pupil is dilated and non-reactive.*

**Reasoning.** Complete third nerve palsy **with pupil involvement** and headache is a **compressive lesion until proven otherwise,** most often a posterior communicating artery aneurysm. This is an emergency.

**Action.** Urgent CT angiography/MR angiography; neurosurgical/neuro-interventional referral; avoid delay.

#### Case 4: Spinal cord compression

*A 66-year-old man with prostate cancer has had 3 days of thoracic back pain followed by leg weakness and difficulty walking. He has urinary hesitancy. Examination: legs spastic with power 3/5, brisk knee and ankle reflexes with sustained clonus, bilateral extensor plantars, a sensory level to pinprick at T8, and tenderness over the T7 spinous process.*

**Localisation.** Spinal cord at or just above T8, **metastatic spinal cord compression.**

**Action.** An oncological emergency: high-dose dexamethasone (per local protocol), **urgent MRI of the whole spine,** immobilisation precautions, early oncology and spinal surgery/radiotherapy involvement. Outcome depends on pre-treatment neurological function, so speed matters.

#### Case 5: Guillain–Barré syndrome

*A 32-year-old man, 10 days after a diarrhoeal illness, has tingling in the feet followed by leg weakness progressing over 3 days and now difficulty climbing stairs. Examination: symmetrical leg weakness 3/5, arm weakness 4/5, absent knee and ankle reflexes, mild distal sensory loss, bilateral facial weakness. Forced vital capacity is falling.*

**Localisation.** Symmetrical ascending LMN weakness with areflexia after an infection: **acute inflammatory demyelinating polyneuropathy.**

**Action.** Admit to monitored care. Serial **forced vital capacity** and negative inspiratory pressure (respiratory failure risk), autonomic monitoring (arrhythmia, BP instability), lumbar puncture (raised protein with normal cell count: albuminocytological dissociation), nerve conduction studies, IV immunoglobulin or plasma exchange.

#### Case 6: Parkinson disease

*A 70-year-old man has a 2-year history of slowness, a tremor in the right hand at rest, and reduced facial expression. He walks with short shuffling steps and reduced right arm swing, turning "en bloc." Tone is increased with cogwheel rigidity; finger tapping decrement on the right; pull test normal.*

**Localisation/diagnosis.** Bradykinesia with rest tremor and rigidity: **idiopathic Parkinson disease** (asymmetrical onset, good levodopa response). Red flags for atypical parkinsonism: early falls, early dementia, symmetrical signs, autonomic failure, poor levodopa response, vertical gaze palsy.

#### Case 7: Carpal tunnel syndrome

*A 48-year-old woman has nocturnal tingling and pain in her right hand, waking her at night, relieved by shaking the hand. Sensory loss over the thumb, index, middle finger, and radial half of the ring finger; weakness of thumb abduction with thenar wasting. Tinel sign (percussion over the carpal tunnel) and Phalen sign (wrist flexion for 60 seconds) reproduce symptoms.*

**Localisation.** Median nerve at the wrist: **carpal tunnel syndrome.** Look for associated conditions: pregnancy, hypothyroidism, diabetes, rheumatoid arthritis, acromegaly, amyloid. Nerve conduction studies confirm; treatment: night splints, steroid injection, surgical release if severe.

### 15.13 Common pitfalls

- **Testing everything without a hypothesis,** or testing only what is easy.
- **Failing to compare sides.**
- **Ignoring the history.** Time course drives the likely pathology.
- **Missing a neglect or visual field defect** because the patient "answers questions well."
- **Mislabelling LMN facial palsy as a stroke (or the reverse).**
- **Forgetting spinal cord and cauda equina emergencies** when assessing leg weakness and back pain.
- **Doing a lumbar puncture before imaging** when there are signs of raised ICP.
- **Over-calling subtle findings** (a single brisk reflex or minor asymmetry in a well person).
- **Attributing altered consciousness to intoxication** without excluding head injury, hypoglycaemia and infection.
- **Failing to check glucose** in suspected stroke or altered mental state.
- **Relying on meningism** to rule out meningitis.
- **Not documenting the examination precisely** (e.g., "neuro exam normal" instead of listing key findings).

---

### 15.14 Key points

- Neurological diagnosis = **where** (from signs) and **what** (from time course).
- Use the GCS (components recorded separately) for consciousness; GCS ≤8 usually needs airway protection.
- Test all cranial nerves; the forehead is spared in UMN facial palsy but involved in LMN palsy.
- Distinguish UMN (spastic, brisk reflexes, extensor plantar) from LMN (flaccid, wasted, reduced reflexes) signs.
- Map sensory loss to pattern: glove-and-stocking, dermatomal, peripheral nerve, spinal level, or hemisensory.
- Cerebellar lesions cause ipsilateral signs (DANISH).
- Recognise neurological emergencies: stroke, meningitis, raised ICP, painful third nerve palsy, cord compression, cauda equina, Guillain–Barré.
- Check glucose in every acute neurological presentation.

### 15.15 Self-assessment

1. Calculate the GCS: opens eyes to pain, says inappropriate words, withdraws from pain.
   *Answer: E2 V3 M4 = 9.*

2. Why does a stroke spare the forehead while Bell palsy does not?
   *Answer: The upper face receives bilateral cortical innervation, so a unilateral UMN lesion is compensated; an LMN (facial nerve) lesion affects all muscles of that side.*

3. List the findings of an upper motor neurone lesion.
   *Answer: Increased tone (spasticity), pyramidal-pattern weakness, brisk reflexes, clonus, extensor plantar response, minimal wasting.*

4. A man has right-sided loss of vibration and proprioception below T10 and left-sided loss of pain and temperature below T12. Name the syndrome and the side of the lesion.
   *Answer: Brown-Séquard syndrome; right hemicord lesion at about T10.*

5. What are the features of cauda equina syndrome?
   *Answer: Low back pain, bilateral sciatica or leg weakness, saddle anaesthesia, urinary retention/overflow, faecal incontinence, loss of anal tone; LMN signs; surgical emergency.*

6. Why is a third nerve palsy with a dilated pupil and headache an emergency?
   *Answer: It suggests compression by a posterior communicating artery aneurysm, which may rupture.*
