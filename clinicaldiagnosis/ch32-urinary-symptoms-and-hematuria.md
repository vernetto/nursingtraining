## Chapter 32. Urinary Symptoms and Hematuria

### Learning objectives

After studying this chapter you should be able to:

1. Classify lower urinary tract symptoms (storage, voiding, post-micturition) and relate them to the common causes: benign prostatic enlargement, overactive bladder, infection, polyuria and neurological disease.
2. Distinguish polyuria from frequency, and evaluate polyuria and nocturia with a bladder diary and targeted tests.
3. Diagnose and manage urinary tract infection across settings (uncomplicated cystitis, pyelonephritis, men, pregnancy, catheters, older adults) and avoid treating asymptomatic bacteriuria.
4. Recognise acute urinary retention and the infected obstructed kidney as urgent problems.
5. Confirm haematuria, distinguish glomerular from non-glomerular causes, and decide who needs urological, nephrological or both evaluations.
6. Identify the risk factors that make haematuria likely to represent cancer, and never attribute haematuria to anticoagulants without investigation.
7. Interpret urinalysis (dipstick, microscopy, sterile pyuria, proteinuria) and use imaging and cystoscopy appropriately.

---

### 32.1 Overview

Urinary symptoms are very common and range from trivial to life-threatening. They include **lower urinary tract symptoms (LUTS)** (frequency, urgency, nocturia, hesitancy, weak stream, incomplete emptying, incontinence), **dysuria** (painful urination), **changes in urine volume** (polyuria, oliguria, anuria), **changes in urine appearance** (haematuria, cloudy, frothy, dark), and **loin pain.**

The approach has four steps:

1. **Characterise the symptom** and its time course.
2. **Identify urgent problems:** sepsis with urinary source, obstruction, retention, acute kidney injury, visible haematuria with clots or heavy bleeding, loin pain with fever, testicular torsion (Chapter 31), pregnancy-related complications.
3. **Test the urine** (dipstick, microscopy, culture) and the kidneys (creatinine, imaging) as indicated.
4. **Assess the risk of cancer or serious renal disease** for haematuria, and refer appropriately.

### 32.2 Lower urinary tract symptoms (LUTS)

LUTS are grouped into three categories:

| Group | Symptoms | Typical causes |
|---|---|---|
| **Storage** (irritative) | **Frequency, urgency, nocturia,** urgency incontinence, bladder pain | Overactive bladder, infection, bladder cancer/carcinoma in situ, stones, interstitial cystitis/bladder pain syndrome, outlet obstruction (secondary detrusor overactivity), neurogenic bladder, polyuria |
| **Voiding** (obstructive) | **Hesitancy, weak or intermittent stream, straining, prolonged voiding, terminal dribbling,** feeling of incomplete emptying | **Benign prostatic enlargement (BPE),** urethral stricture, bladder neck obstruction, detrusor underactivity, prostate cancer, neurogenic bladder, constipation, medications, pelvic organ prolapse (women) |
| **Post-micturition** | Post-micturition dribble, sensation of incomplete emptying | Urethral diverticulum, BPE, pelvic floor weakness |

#### History

- **Pattern:** onset, duration, progression; daytime and night-time frequency (number of voids); nocturia (number of times waking to void; ≥2 per night is clinically significant); urgency (sudden compelling desire to void); incontinence (stress, urge, overflow, continuous); stream; straining; sensation of incomplete emptying; intermittency; terminal dribble.
- **Associated symptoms:** dysuria, haematuria, loin or suprapubic pain, fever, discharge, sexual dysfunction, constipation, back pain, leg weakness, saddle numbness.
- **Fluid intake:** amount and type (caffeine, alcohol, fizzy drinks), time of drinking in the evening.
- **Medications:** **diuretics, anticholinergics** (tricyclics, antipsychotics, antihistamines, oxybutynin: retention and constipation), **alpha-agonists** (decongestants: bladder neck contraction), **opioids, calcium channel blockers (nocturia, oedema), SGLT2 inhibitors (glycosuria, genital infections), lithium (nephrogenic DI), ACE inhibitors (cough), 5-alpha-reductase inhibitors.**
- **Medical conditions:** diabetes, neurological disease (MS, Parkinson, stroke, spinal cord injury, dementia), heart failure and sleep apnoea (nocturnal polyuria), pelvic surgery, radiotherapy, prostate disease, obstetric history (women), menopause.
- **Impact:** bother score; sleep disruption, falls at night, social restrictions.
- **Validated tools:** **International Prostate Symptom Score (IPSS)** for men; **ICIQ** for incontinence; **bladder diary.**

#### Bladder diary (frequency–volume chart)

Record for at least 3 days: times and volumes of each void, fluid intake, urgency episodes, leakage. It distinguishes:

- **Polyuria** (>3 L/24 hours, or >40 mL/kg/day).
- **Nocturnal polyuria** (nocturnal urine >33% of the 24-hour volume in adults over 65; >20% in young adults).
- **Reduced bladder capacity** (small, frequent voids: overactive bladder, interstitial cystitis, outlet obstruction).
- **High fluid intake** habits (so-called "behavioural frequency").

#### Examination

- **Abdomen:** palpable bladder (dullness to percussion above the pubis), masses, kidneys, scars, hernias.
- **External genitalia:** phimosis, meatal stenosis, discharge, masses, epididymal tenderness, hydrocele.
- **Digital rectal examination (men):** prostate size, consistency (smooth firm enlargement in BPH; hard irregular nodule in cancer; tender boggy in acute prostatitis), anal tone, faecal loading.
- **Pelvic examination (women):** atrophic vaginitis, prolapse (cystocele, rectocele), urethral diverticulum, masses, pelvic floor strength, stress test (cough with full bladder in lithotomy or standing).
- **Neurological examination:** lower limb tone, power, reflexes, perineal sensation, anal tone and voluntary contraction, bulbocavernosus reflex; gait.
- **Cognition and mobility** (functional incontinence).

#### Investigations

- **Urinalysis** (dipstick; microscopy and culture if indicated).
- **U&E, creatinine/eGFR** (obstructive uropathy), **glucose/HbA1c,** calcium (polyuria), **PSA** after counselling (men with LUTS or abnormal DRE; interpretation depends on age, symptoms, infection, instrumentation).
- **Post-void residual (PVR)** by bladder scan (normally <50 mL; >200 mL significant; chronic retention often >300 mL).
- **Uroflowmetry:** peak flow rate (Qmax; <10 mL/s suggests obstruction in men).
- **Renal and bladder ultrasound** (hydronephrosis, bladder wall thickness, residual volume, prostate size, stones).
- **Cystoscopy** for haematuria, suspected bladder tumour, refractory storage symptoms, stricture.
- **Urodynamics** for complex or surgical cases.

### 32.3 Common causes of LUTS and their management

#### Benign prostatic enlargement and obstruction

- Men over 50; progressive voiding symptoms (and secondary storage symptoms), nocturia; enlarged smooth prostate on DRE (size does not correlate with symptoms).
- **Complications:** acute urinary retention, chronic retention with overflow incontinence, recurrent UTI, bladder stones, haematuria, hydronephrosis and renal impairment.
- **Management:** lifestyle (fluid timing, caffeine and alcohol reduction, treat constipation); **alpha-blockers** (tamsulosin, alfuzosin; rapid relief; orthostatic hypotension, retrograde ejaculation, intra-operative floppy iris syndrome); **5-alpha-reductase inhibitors** (finasteride, dutasteride; shrink large prostates over 6 months; lower PSA by about 50%); anticholinergics or beta-3 agonists for persistent storage symptoms (after confirming PVR is low); PDE5 inhibitors (tadalafil); surgery (TURP, laser enucleation, other minimally invasive procedures) for refractory symptoms or complications.

#### Overactive bladder (OAB)

- **Urgency,** with or without urgency incontinence, usually with frequency and nocturia, in the absence of infection or other pathology; women and men.
- **Management:** behavioural therapy (bladder training, fluid management, pelvic floor exercises, weight loss), **antimuscarinics** (oxybutynin, solifenacin, tolterodine; **anticholinergic burden in older adults: cognitive impairment, constipation, dry mouth**) or **beta-3 agonist** (mirabegron, vibegron); botulinum toxin injection, sacral neuromodulation, tibial nerve stimulation in refractory cases.

#### Nocturia

- Defined as waking at night to void. Causes: **nocturnal polyuria** (heart failure, sleep apnoea, evening fluids, diuretics late in the day, venous insufficiency with fluid mobilisation on lying, diabetes, diabetes insipidus, hypercalcaemia, hypokalaemia, CKD), reduced bladder capacity (OAB, obstruction), sleep disorders (insomnia, sleep apnoea).
- Treat the cause; desmopressin (with strict sodium monitoring; contraindicated in older adults with hyponatraemia risk).

#### Bladder pain syndrome / interstitial cystitis

- Chronic suprapubic pain related to bladder filling, with frequency and nocturia, in the absence of infection or other pathology; women. Pain relieved by voiding. Diagnosis by exclusion (urine culture, cystoscopy). Treatment: education, diet, pelvic floor physiotherapy, amitriptyline, pentosan polysulfate, intravesical therapy.

#### Neurogenic bladder

- Spinal cord injury, MS, Parkinson, stroke, diabetic autonomic neuropathy, cauda equina syndrome, spina bifida: detrusor overactivity, detrusor-sphincter dyssynergia, or underactive bladder; risk of high pressures and upper tract damage, infection. Assess with urodynamics, ultrasound; manage with intermittent self-catheterisation, anticholinergics, botulinum toxin; renal surveillance.

#### Others

- **Urethral stricture** (men: previous instrumentation, trauma, infection; slow stream, spraying).
- **Pelvic organ prolapse, atrophic vaginitis** (women: topical vaginal oestrogen helps frequency and recurrent UTI).
- **Constipation** (a very common contributor).
- **Delirium and immobility** (functional incontinence).

### 32.4 Polyuria

**Polyuria** is the production of >3 L of urine per day in adults (>40 mL/kg/day). Patients often confuse it with **frequency** (many small voids). The diary clarifies.

| Mechanism | Causes |
|---|---|
| **Osmotic diuresis** (solute-driven) | **Hyperglycaemia (diabetes mellitus),** mannitol, high-protein feeding, urea (post-obstructive, recovering AKI), radiocontrast, SGLT2 inhibitors |
| **Water diuresis** (urine dilute, low osmolality) | **Primary polydipsia** (psychogenic, hypothalamic, drug-induced), **central diabetes insipidus** (ADH deficiency: pituitary or hypothalamic disease, trauma, tumour, infiltrative disease, idiopathic), **nephrogenic diabetes insipidus** (lithium, hypercalcaemia, hypokalaemia, chronic kidney disease, tubulo-interstitial disease, obstruction, sickle cell disease, demeclocycline, inherited) |
| **Other** | Diuretics, caffeine, alcohol, resolution of oedema, recovery from acute tubular necrosis, post-obstructive diuresis, hypercalcaemia, hypokalaemia |

#### Approach

- Confirm polyuria with a 24-hour urine collection (volume) and bladder diary.
- **Glucose, calcium, potassium, sodium, creatinine, osmolality** (serum and urine).
- **Urine osmolality:** >600–700 mOsm/kg suggests solute diuresis or normal concentrating ability; <250–300 mOsm/kg suggests water diuresis (DI or polydipsia).
- **Water deprivation test (or copeptin-based tests)** with desmopressin response to separate primary polydipsia, central DI and nephrogenic DI. Must be supervised in a specialist setting because of the risk of dehydration and hypernatraemia.
- Check pituitary function and MRI in central DI.

### 32.5 Dysuria and urinary tract infection

**Dysuria** (burning, stinging or pain on voiding) is the cardinal symptom of lower urinary tract infection, but also occurs in urethritis, vaginitis, genital herpes, prostatitis, stones, interstitial cystitis, and chemical irritation.

#### Classification of UTI

| Type | Description |
|---|---|
| **Uncomplicated cystitis** | Acute lower UTI in non-pregnant, premenopausal women without structural or functional abnormalities |
| **Complicated UTI** | UTI in men, pregnancy, children, older adults, diabetics, immunosuppressed, indwelling catheters, urinary obstruction (stones, BPH, stricture), neurogenic bladder, recent instrumentation, resistant organism, or systemic symptoms |
| **Acute pyelonephritis** | Upper tract infection with fever, loin pain, malaise, nausea and vomiting, with or without lower tract symptoms |
| **Recurrent UTI** | ≥2 episodes in 6 months or ≥3 in 12 months |
| **Asymptomatic bacteriuria** | Positive urine culture (≥10⁵ CFU/mL in two samples in women) without symptoms |
| **Catheter-associated UTI** | UTI in a patient with a catheter (or within 48 hours of removal) with symptoms |

#### Uncomplicated cystitis in women

- **Symptoms:** dysuria, frequency, urgency, suprapubic discomfort, occasionally haematuria; **absence of vaginal discharge or irritation** raises the probability of UTI to over 90%. Fever and loin pain suggest upper tract involvement.
- **Diagnosis:** clinical; **dipstick:** **nitrite positive** (specific for Enterobacteriaceae) and/or **leucocyte esterase positive**; a negative dipstick for both makes UTI unlikely in a woman with typical symptoms (about 20% or less probability), though culture should be considered if symptoms persist. Urine culture is not necessary for a first uncomplicated episode, but is indicated in treatment failure, recurrent infection, pregnancy, men, and suspected complicated disease.
- **Organisms:** *E. coli* (75–90%), *Staphylococcus saprophyticus* (young sexually active women), *Klebsiella*, *Proteus*, *Enterococcus.*
- **Treatment (follow local resistance patterns):** short courses of **nitrofurantoin (3–5 days; avoid if eGFR <30–45 mL/min or near term in pregnancy), fosfomycin (single dose), pivmecillinam (3–5 days), trimethoprim (3 days, only where local resistance is low).** Fluoroquinolones should be avoided for uncomplicated cystitis because of adverse effects and resistance. Beta-lactam alternatives (cefalexin, amoxicillin-clavulanate) have lower efficacy. Analgesia (paracetamol, NSAID); delayed antibiotic prescribing or NSAIDs alone is an option for mild symptoms (but with a higher risk of progression to pyelonephritis).
- **Safety-net:** return if no improvement in 48 hours, fever, loin pain, vomiting.

#### Acute pyelonephritis

- **Features:** fever (≥38 °C), rigors, loin or flank pain, costovertebral angle tenderness, nausea and vomiting, malaise; may have lower urinary symptoms; sepsis in severe cases (hypotension, tachycardia, confusion).
- **Investigations:** urinalysis, **urine culture before antibiotics,** blood cultures (if febrile or septic), FBC, CRP, U&E/creatinine, lactate if unwell, pregnancy test; **imaging (ultrasound, CT)** if there is **no response within 48–72 hours, sepsis, suspected obstruction (colic, haematuria, previous stones), diabetes, immunosuppression, male sex, or recurrent infection.**
- **Treatment:**
  - **Outpatient oral therapy** for mild to moderate disease without vomiting or sepsis: ciprofloxacin or levofloxacin (if local resistance is <10% and no contraindications), oral cephalosporin (cefpodoxime), trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (if susceptible); 7 days for most regimens (5–7 days with fluoroquinolones; 10–14 days for beta-lactams), according to local guidance.
  - **Admission and IV antibiotics** (ceftriaxone, piperacillin-tazobactam, gentamicin/amikacin, carbapenem if ESBL risk) for sepsis, vomiting, pregnancy, older or frail patients, immunosuppression, obstruction, or failure of oral therapy.
- **Emergency: infected obstructed kidney** (pyonephrosis): fever, loin pain, sepsis in a patient with a stone or other obstruction: **urgent decompression** (percutaneous nephrostomy or ureteric stent) **plus antibiotics;** delay can be fatal. Maintain a very low threshold for imaging in septic patients with loin pain.
- **Complications:** emphysematous pyelonephritis (diabetics, gas on imaging, high mortality), renal abscess, perinephric abscess, papillary necrosis, bacteraemia, sepsis, renal scarring in children.

#### Men

- UTI in men is by definition complicated and warrants culture and consideration of an underlying cause (prostatic obstruction, stones, instrumentation, STI in younger men).
- **Acute bacterial prostatitis:** fever, rigors, dysuria, frequency, perineal and suprapubic pain, retention, a **tender, swollen, boggy prostate** on gentle examination (avoid vigorous massage: risk of bacteraemia). Treat with 2–4 weeks (up to 6) of fluoroquinolone or trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (good prostate penetration), IV antibiotics if septic; urinary retention may need suprapubic catheter.
- **Chronic prostatitis / chronic pelvic pain syndrome:** pain for >3 months in the pelvic region with urinary and sexual symptoms, often without infection; multimodal therapy.
- **Epididymo-orchitis:** scrotal pain and swelling with fever; **in men <35 years, think chlamydia and gonorrhoea; in older men and those with urinary tract abnormalities, enteric organisms.** Exclude torsion (Chapter 31).
- **Urethritis:** dysuria with urethral discharge (gonorrhoea, chlamydia, *Mycoplasma genitalium*, trichomonas, herpes); NAAT testing and partner treatment.

#### Pregnancy

- **Asymptomatic bacteriuria must be screened for and treated** (risk of pyelonephritis, preterm birth). UTI in pregnancy: culture-guided treatment (nitrofurantoin avoided in the first trimester in some guidance and at term; cefalexin, amoxicillin, fosfomycin options). **Pyelonephritis requires admission,** IV antibiotics, and follow-up cultures.

#### Catheter-associated UTI

- Colonisation is universal in long-term catheters: **do not test or treat bacteriuria without symptoms** (fever, new suprapubic pain, loin pain, rigors, delirium with systemic signs); change the catheter before collecting a specimen, and treat symptomatic infection with culture-guided therapy for 5–7 days (7–14 days if severe). Remove the catheter as soon as possible. Avoid prophylactic antibiotics except in selected contexts.

#### Older adults

- **Asymptomatic bacteriuria is very common** (up to 15–50% in institutionalised older adults); a positive dipstick or culture is **not** a reason to attribute confusion, falls or reduced appetite to UTI (Chapter 2). Treat only if there are **new urinary symptoms (dysuria, frequency, urgency, suprapubic pain, new incontinence), fever, loin pain or systemic signs of sepsis** without another source. Consider other causes of delirium and use clinical decision tools.

#### Recurrent UTI in women

- Confirm each episode with culture; look for contributors: sexual activity, spermicide use, new partner, postmenopausal oestrogen deficiency, incomplete emptying, prolapse, stones, diabetes (glycosuria), catheter, immunosuppression.
- **Prevention:** behavioural measures (hydration; prompt voiding; post-coital voiding); **topical vaginal oestrogen in postmenopausal women;** non-antibiotic options (methenamine hippurate, D-mannose has limited evidence, cranberry products have modest evidence, immunostimulant OM-89, vaccines); **continuous or post-coital low-dose antibiotic prophylaxis** (nitrofurantoin, trimethoprim, cefalexin) for 3–6 months in selected patients; self-start therapy.
- Imaging or cystoscopy when atypical features: haematuria between episodes, persistent infection with the same organism, stones, pyelonephritis, male sex, or poor response.

#### Sterile pyuria

Leucocytes in urine with negative standard cultures. Causes: **partially treated UTI, urethritis (chlamydia, gonorrhoea, mycoplasma), renal tuberculosis** (early-morning urine samples ×3 for acid-fast bacilli), **stones,** interstitial nephritis (drugs: NSAIDs, antibiotics), analgesic nephropathy, bladder tumour, catheterisation, interstitial cystitis, Kawasaki disease (children), contamination with vaginal discharge, glomerulonephritis, vaginitis.

### 32.6 Acute and chronic urinary retention

#### Acute urinary retention (AUR)

- **Painful inability to void,** with a palpable, tender, distended bladder (suprapubic dullness).
- **Causes:**
  - **Obstructive (most common):** **BPH** (often precipitated by medications, constipation, infection, immobility, alcohol, cold weather, surgery), urethral stricture, prostate cancer, clot retention (haematuria), phimosis/paraphimosis, urethral stone, pelvic mass, severe constipation, pregnancy (retroverted uterus), fibroids.
  - **Drugs:** anticholinergics, tricyclic antidepressants, antipsychotics, antihistamines, opioids, **alpha-agonists (decongestants), NSAIDs, calcium channel blockers,** benzodiazepines, muscle relaxants, general anaesthesia and spinal anaesthesia.
  - **Neurogenic:** **cauda equina syndrome** (Chapter 29; **painless retention with saddle anaesthesia is an emergency**), spinal cord compression, multiple sclerosis, diabetic neuropathy, Parkinson disease, herpes zoster (sacral), Guillain–Barré.
  - **Infective and inflammatory:** UTI, prostatitis, genital herpes, vulvovaginitis in women (painful voiding).
  - **Post-operative and post-partum.**
- **Management:** urethral catheterisation (record the volume drained), analgesia, review medications, treat constipation, check U&E, creatinine, urinalysis, culture; start alpha-blocker; **trial without catheter** after 2–3 days (with alpha-blocker) for BPH-related retention; refer to urology if recurrent or failure; look for hydronephrosis (ultrasound) if the retained volume was large (>1 L) or creatinine is raised.
- **Post-obstructive diuresis** can follow relief of chronic obstruction; monitor urine output, electrolytes and fluid balance; replace fluids with care.
- **Haematuria ex vacuo** (bleeding after decompression) is usually self-limiting.

#### Chronic retention

- **Painless;** large residual volume (often >300 mL); symptoms: frequency, nocturia, poor stream, dribbling, **overflow incontinence,** recurrent infections, bladder stones; renal impairment from back-pressure; hypertension.
- Management: catheterise (monitor for post-obstructive diuresis), treat cause, intermittent self-catheterisation where appropriate.

### 32.7 Urinary incontinence (brief)

| Type | Mechanism | Features | Treatment |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Stress** | Weak urethral sphincter or pelvic floor | Leakage with coughing, sneezing, exercise; women (childbirth, menopause, obesity), men (post-prostatectomy) | Pelvic floor muscle training, weight loss, duloxetine (some regions), vaginal pessary, mid-urethral sling, bulking agents |
| **Urge** | Detrusor overactivity | Sudden urgency with leakage | Bladder training, antimuscarinics/β3-agonists, botulinum toxin, neuromodulation |
| **Mixed** | Both | | Treat the dominant component |
| **Overflow** | Chronic retention | Dribbling, weak stream, distended bladder | Relieve obstruction, catheterise |
| **Functional** | Cognitive or physical limits | Cannot reach toilet in time; dementia, immobility | Environmental measures, timed voiding, toilet access |
| **Continuous** | Fistula (vesicovaginal, ureterovaginal), ectopic ureter | Continuous leakage; after surgery, obstetric injury, radiotherapy | Surgical repair |
| **Transient/reversible** (DIAPPERS) | Delirium, Infection, Atrophic vaginitis, Pharmaceuticals, Psychological, Excess urine output, Restricted mobility, Stool impaction | | Treat the cause |

Assess: bladder diary, cough stress test, PVR, urinalysis, pelvic examination (women), DRE (men), neurological examination, cognitive function. Urodynamics only before invasive therapy or when diagnosis is unclear.

### 32.8 Oliguria and anuria

- **Oliguria:** urine output <400 mL/day (or <0.5 mL/kg/hour for ≥6 hours); **anuria:** <100 mL/day. Both are red flags for **acute kidney injury (AKI)** (Chapter 49).
- **Initial questions:** Is the bladder empty? (**Bladder scan and catheter** to exclude retention, particularly in older men.) Is the catheter blocked? What is the volume status (hypovolaemia, sepsis, heart failure)? Which drugs (NSAIDs, ACE inhibitors, diuretics, contrast, antibiotics)? Are there signs of obstruction (hydronephrosis on ultrasound)?
- **Categories of AKI:** pre-renal (hypovolaemia, low cardiac output, hepatorenal), intrinsic renal (acute tubular necrosis, glomerulonephritis, acute interstitial nephritis, vasculitis, rhabdomyolysis, haemolytic uraemic syndrome), post-renal (obstruction).
- **Urgent tasks:** assess ABCDE and fluid status, urinalysis (blood, protein, casts), U&E, potassium, ECG, blood gas, bladder scan, renal ultrasound, medication review, treat sepsis, relieve obstruction.

# Hematuria

### 32.9 Definitions and confirmation

**Haematuria** is the presence of red blood cells in urine.

- **Visible (macroscopic, gross) haematuria:** urine red, pink, brown, or tea-coloured, visible to the naked eye.
- **Non-visible (microscopic) haematuria:** detected on dipstick or microscopy only. Definitions vary: dipstick ≥1+ (some guidelines require ≥2+ or two out of three positive samples), or ≥3 red blood cells per high-power field on microscopy of a properly collected, centrifuged midstream specimen.

#### Confirming real haematuria

- **Dipstick** detects haemoglobin by peroxidase-like activity: **false positives** from myoglobin (rhabdomyolysis), haemoglobin (haemolysis), semen, oxidising agents, povidone-iodine, bacterial peroxidases (UTI); **false negatives** from high-dose vitamin C. **Always confirm with microscopy** (RBCs present) before starting a work-up for non-visible haematuria.
- **Exclude contamination** (menstruation, vaginal bleeding, anal bleeding), recent vigorous exercise, sexual intercourse, trauma, UTI (repeat the test after treatment).
- **Other causes of red or dark urine without haematuria:** **beetroot, rhubarb, blackberries; drugs** (rifampicin, phenazopyridine, nitrofurantoin (brown), metronidazole, methyldopa, levodopa, senna, phenytoin, chloroquine); **myoglobinuria and haemoglobinuria** (dipstick positive, no RBC on microscopy); **bilirubinuria** (dark brown, yellow foam); **porphyria** (urine darkens on standing).

### 32.10 Causes of haematuria

#### Non-glomerular (urological) causes

| Cause | Features |
|---|---|
| **Urinary tract infection** | Dysuria, frequency, urgency, positive culture, resolves after treatment |
| **Stones (calculi)** | Colicky loin-to-groin pain, nausea, restlessness, visible or microscopic haematuria, history of stones |
| **Bladder cancer** | **Painless visible haematuria,** often intermittent, in smokers, older age (peak 60–80), occupational exposure to aromatic amines (dyes, rubber, textiles, leather, paints), cyclophosphamide, pelvic radiotherapy, chronic catheter; storage symptoms and non-resolving UTI-like symptoms (carcinoma in situ) |
| **Renal cell carcinoma** | Haematuria, flank pain, palpable mass (classic triad in only a minority), weight loss, fever, polycythaemia, varicocele (left, non-reducing) |
| **Upper tract urothelial carcinoma** | Painless haematuria, flank pain; smoking, analgesic abuse, Balkan nephropathy |
| **Prostate: BPH, prostate cancer, prostatitis** | Terminal haematuria, LUTS, abnormal DRE or PSA |
| **Trauma, instrumentation, catheterisation** | History |
| **Polycystic kidney disease** | Family history, hypertension, bilateral renal masses |
| **Renal papillary necrosis** | Diabetes, sickle cell disease, analgesic nephropathy, obstruction, TB; passage of necrotic papillae |
| **Sickle cell disease or trait** | Painless haematuria (right > left) |
| **Medullary sponge kidney, renal infarction, renal vein thrombosis, arteriovenous malformation, loin pain-haematuria syndrome** | |
| **Radiation or chemical cystitis** (cyclophosphamide, ifosfamide) | History of treatment |
| **Schistosomiasis** (*S. haematobium*) | Endemic areas (Africa, Middle East), terminal haematuria, freshwater exposure, squamous cell bladder cancer |
| **Tuberculosis** | Sterile pyuria, haematuria, constitutional features |
| **Exercise-induced ("march haematuria")** | Resolves within 72 hours after rest |
| **Anticoagulation or bleeding disorders** | **Haematuria in anticoagulated patients is often due to a urological abnormality** (more than a third in some series) and requires full evaluation |
| **Idiopathic / benign** | After complete evaluation, no cause is found in many |

#### Glomerular causes

Glomerular haematuria originates in the glomerulus and implies renal parenchymal disease. **Features favouring glomerular origin:**

- **Brown, cola- or tea-coloured urine** (not bright red), no clots.
- **Proteinuria** (dipstick ≥1+, uPCR or uACR raised); oedema, hypertension.
- **Dysmorphic red cells (acanthocytes) and red cell casts** on urine microscopy.
- Associated renal impairment, family history, systemic features (rash, arthralgia, sinusitis, hearing loss, haemoptysis).

| Cause | Clues |
|---|---|
| **IgA nephropathy** (most common primary glomerulonephritis worldwide) | Recurrent episodes of visible haematuria **1–2 days after an upper respiratory infection** ("synpharyngitic"), young adults; or persistent microscopic haematuria; may have proteinuria and hypertension |
| **Post-infectious glomerulonephritis** (post-streptococcal) | **1–3 weeks** after pharyngitis or skin infection; oedema, hypertension, cola urine, low C3 |
| **Alport syndrome** | X-linked or autosomal; haematuria, sensorineural deafness, ocular abnormalities, family history, progressive CKD |
| **Thin basement membrane disease** | Benign familial haematuria, normal renal function, no proteinuria |
| **Lupus nephritis, vasculitis (ANCA), anti-GBM disease (Goodpasture), IgA vasculitis (Henoch–Schönlein), membranoproliferative GN, cryoglobulinaemia, endocarditis-associated GN** | Systemic features, rapidly progressive GN: urgent referral |
| **Hereditary nephritis, fibronectin glomerulopathy,** and others | |

### 32.11 Evaluation of haematuria

#### History

- **Visible haematuria:** timing in the stream (initial: urethral or prostatic source; terminal: bladder neck or prostate; total: bladder or upper tract), clots (non-glomerular), pain (colic: stone or clot; painless: tumour), recurrent pattern, relation to infection or exercise.
- **Associated symptoms:** LUTS, dysuria, fever, loin pain, weight loss, rash, joint pain, sore throat, oedema, hearing loss, haemoptysis, bleeding elsewhere.
- **Risk factors for malignancy:** age >40 (higher risk >60), male, **smoking (current or former; dose-dependent),** occupational chemical exposure, pelvic radiation, cyclophosphamide, analgesic abuse, chronic cystitis, indwelling catheter, schistosomiasis, previous urological cancer, family history, previous gross haematuria.
- **Medications:** anticoagulants, antiplatelets, NSAIDs, cyclophosphamide, penicillamine; herbal remedies (aristolochic acid).
- **Family history:** polycystic kidney disease, Alport, kidney failure, sickle cell, stones.
- **Recent trauma, exercise, instrumentation, UTI.**

#### Examination

- Blood pressure, oedema, rash, abdominal masses, palpable bladder, loin tenderness, DRE (prostate), genitalia, signs of systemic disease, fundoscopy (hypertensive changes).

#### Urine and blood tests

- **Urine dipstick and microscopy** (RBC morphology, casts), **culture** (treat and repeat the test; haematuria should not be dismissed as "UTI" unless it resolves after treatment).
- **Quantify proteinuria** (uACR or uPCR).
- **eGFR/creatinine, electrolytes, FBC, coagulation, glucose, lipid profile** as indicated.
- **If glomerular features:** C3/C4, ANA, anti-dsDNA, ANCA, anti-GBM, ASO/anti-DNase B, hepatitis B/C, HIV, cryoglobulins, serum protein electrophoresis.
- **Urine cytology:** limited sensitivity (high-grade tumours); **not** a screening tool for low-grade disease; may be used in high-risk situations.
- **Urine biomarkers** (NMP22, UroVysion, others) have limited adoption.
- Tests for TB (early-morning urine ×3) and schistosomiasis (terminal urine, eggs) in appropriate settings.

#### Imaging and cystoscopy

| Test | Role |
|---|---|
| **Ultrasound (renal tract)** | First-line, widely available: stones, hydronephrosis, renal masses, bladder lesions, polycystic kidneys, prostate size |
| **CT urography (multiphase)** | Preferred upper-tract imaging for high-risk haematuria (detects stones, renal masses, upper-tract urothelial tumours); radiation and contrast; consider MR urography or ultrasound with retrograde pyelography if CT is not appropriate (pregnancy, young, renal impairment, contrast allergy) |
| **Flexible cystoscopy** | **Essential for visible haematuria** in adults and for microscopic haematuria in those at risk; detects bladder tumours (white-light, blue-light/narrow-band imaging improve detection) |
| **Ureteroscopy, retrograde pyelography** | Upper-tract filling defects |
| **Renal biopsy** | Glomerular haematuria with proteinuria, falling GFR, or suspected GN, interpreted by nephrology |

#### Risk-based approach

**Visible haematuria (adult):**

- **Urgent referral** to urology (suspected cancer pathway) for patients ≥45 years (many guidelines use ≥45 for unexplained visible haematuria without UTI, ≥60 with dysuria and negative or persistent microscopic haematuria after UTI treatment) and for all patients with risk factors, recurrent or persistent visible haematuria.
- **Evaluation:** cystoscopy plus upper-tract imaging (CT urography). Young patients with isolated, self-limited visible haematuria after infection or exercise and normal tests may be observed.
- **Do not attribute to anticoagulants or antiplatelets;** these may unmask underlying disease.

**Non-visible haematuria:**

1. **Confirm** (two of three samples positive; microscopy with ≥3 RBC/HPF).
2. **Check for proteinuria, hypertension, eGFR:** if present or if glomerular features, refer to nephrology (and, when risk factors exist, perform urological evaluation too).
3. **Stratify the risk of urinary tract malignancy** (e.g. using the AUA/SUFU risk categories: low risk: age <60 (men) or <60 (women), never smoked or <10 pack-years, 3–10 RBC/HPF, no previous visible haematuria or other risk factors; intermediate; high: men ≥60, >30 pack-years, >25 RBC/HPF, previous gross haematuria):
   - **Low risk:** repeat urinalysis within 6–12 months, or ultrasound ± cystoscopy by shared decision-making.
   - **Intermediate risk:** cystoscopy and renal ultrasound (or CT urography).
   - **High risk:** cystoscopy and CT urography.
4. **If no cause is found:** follow-up urinalysis, blood pressure and renal function, annually for 2 years or so; repeat evaluation if visible haematuria, new symptoms, or persistent abnormalities.

**Children:** haematuria is more commonly glomerular (post-infectious, IgA, Alport, thin membrane) or due to hypercalciuria, UTI, trauma, stones; evaluate with urinalysis, protein, BP, renal function, renal ultrasound, and specialist referral for glomerular features. A child with cola-coloured urine, hypertension and oedema following an infection has acute GN until proven otherwise.

### 32.12 Special populations

- **Children and infants:** UTI may present with non-specific symptoms (fever without source, irritability, poor feeding, vomiting, jaundice in neonates); obtain a **clean-catch or catheter or suprapubic sample** (bag samples are unreliable and prone to contamination); investigate for vesicoureteric reflux and structural anomalies according to guidelines; nocturnal enuresis, daytime wetting, constipation (Chapter 59).
- **Pregnancy:** screen for bacteriuria; treat all positive cultures; pyelonephritis is an emergency; haematuria in pregnancy: infection, stones, glomerular disease; proteinuria and hypertension: pre-eclampsia.
- **Older adults:** high rates of asymptomatic bacteriuria; atypical UTI presentations; urinary retention (BPH, drugs, constipation); incontinence and falls; AKI from obstruction; avoid anticholinergic burden.
- **Diabetes:** increased risk of UTI, emphysematous pyelonephritis, papillary necrosis, neurogenic bladder, glycosuria (SGLT2 inhibitors increase genital mycotic infections; euglycaemic DKA).
- **Immunosuppressed and transplant patients:** atypical organisms (fungal, BK virus, CMV), higher risk of urosepsis.
- **Spinal cord injury:** neurogenic bladder, autonomic dysreflexia (headache, hypertension triggered by bladder distension).

### 32.13 Worked cases

#### Case 1: Uncomplicated cystitis

*A 26-year-old woman has 2 days of dysuria, frequency and urgency, with no vaginal discharge, fever or loin pain. Dipstick: leucocytes ++, nitrite positive. Pregnancy test negative.*

**Reasoning.** Typical symptoms and positive dipstick: **uncomplicated cystitis** (high probability >90%). Culture is not needed for a first episode.

**Action.** Nitrofurantoin 3 days, or a single dose of fosfomycin, or pivmecillinam per local guidance; analgesia; fluids; safety-net (fever, loin pain, vomiting, no improvement in 48 hours).

#### Case 2: Obstructed infected kidney

*A 34-year-old woman with right loin pain for 2 days, then fever 39.4 °C, rigors, and vomiting. HR 122, BP 92/54, right costovertebral angle tenderness. Urine: blood and leucocytes. Creatinine 130 µmol/L. Ultrasound: moderate hydronephrosis; CT: 8 mm proximal ureteric stone.*

**Reasoning.** Pyelonephritis with sepsis and obstruction (**infected hydronephrosis**).

**Action.** Sepsis bundle (cultures, IV broad-spectrum antibiotics, fluids), **urgent decompression** (ureteric stent or percutaneous nephrostomy) — definitive stone treatment later when infection resolved. Do not delay for definitive imaging if the patient is unstable.

#### Case 3: Painless visible haematuria

*A 68-year-old man (45 pack-years) has had two episodes of painless, bright red urine over a month, with no dysuria. Examination normal. Urine microscopy: non-dysmorphic RBCs. Creatinine normal. He is on aspirin.*

**Reasoning.** Painless visible haematuria in a smoker over 60: **bladder cancer** until proved otherwise (aspirin does not explain it).

**Action.** Urgent referral; flexible cystoscopy and CT urography; transurethral resection of bladder tumour and staging if a lesion is found; smoking cessation.

#### Case 4: IgA nephropathy

*A 22-year-old man has had cola-coloured urine 1 day after a sore throat, for the second time in a year. BP 142/88; no oedema. Urine: protein 1+, RBC casts and dysmorphic RBCs. eGFR 88. uPCR 90 mg/mmol.*

**Reasoning.** Synpharyngitic haematuria with proteinuria and dysmorphic RBCs: **IgA nephropathy** (glomerular).

**Action.** Nephrology referral, renal biopsy to confirm and grade, blood pressure control with ACE inhibitor/ARB, proteinuria reduction, supportive immunosuppression per risk; monitoring of eGFR and proteinuria.

#### Case 5: Acute urinary retention

*A 76-year-old man with BPH attends with painful inability to void for 12 hours after a cold remedy containing pseudoephedrine and an episode of constipation. The bladder is palpable to the umbilicus. Catheterisation drains 900 mL.*

**Reasoning.** **Acute retention** precipitated by decongestant and constipation on a background of BPH.

**Action.** Catheterise, alpha-blocker, treat constipation, stop pseudoephedrine, check U&E and urine, monitor for post-obstructive diuresis, trial without catheter in 2–3 days; consider 5-alpha-reductase inhibitor and urology referral if recurrent.

#### Case 6: Nocturia and polyuria

*A 59-year-old man gets up four times each night to void, with daytime polyuria and thirst, and a 4-kg weight loss. Glucose 24 mmol/L, HbA1c 105 mmol/mol, urine glucose 4+, ketones negative.*

**Reasoning.** **Osmotic polyuria from diabetes mellitus** (new-onset type 2). Nocturia is secondary to polyuria.

**Action.** Confirm the diagnosis, start treatment, check for complications and cardiovascular risk. Reassess nocturia after glycaemic control; consider sleep apnoea if persistent.

#### Case 7: Confusion in a care-home resident

*An 84-year-old woman with dementia has had 2 days of reduced appetite and drowsiness. The care home dipstick: leucocytes +++, nitrite negative. She has no dysuria, fever or suprapubic tenderness; she has a cough and tachypnoea; chest X-ray shows consolidation.*

**Reasoning.** A positive dipstick in an older woman is common asymptomatic bacteriuria; she has no urinary symptoms; **pneumonia** explains the picture.

**Action.** Treat pneumonia; do not attribute the delirium to UTI; avoid unnecessary antibiotics for bacteriuria; review after treatment.

#### Case 8: Acute prostatitis

*A 45-year-old man has fever, rigors, perineal pain and dysuria for 2 days. DRE: very tender, warm, swollen prostate. Urine: leucocytes and nitrites. CRP 160.*

**Reasoning.** **Acute bacterial prostatitis.**

**Action.** Blood and urine cultures, antibiotics with good prostate penetration (fluoroquinolone or trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole), IV if septic; avoid vigorous prostatic massage; catheter (suprapubic if retention); treat 2–4 weeks; assess for underlying obstruction or STI.

#### Case 9: Schistosomiasis

*A 19-year-old man from rural Zambia has terminal haematuria and suprapubic discomfort for 3 months, with a history of swimming in a lake. Urine microscopy (terminal sample, midday): terminal-spined eggs. Ultrasound: bladder wall thickening, mild hydronephrosis.*

**Reasoning.** **Urogenital schistosomiasis** (*S. haematobium*).

**Action.** Praziquantel (single or repeat dose), follow-up ultrasound and cystoscopy as needed (risk of squamous bladder cancer long term), public health measures; screen for co-infections.

#### Case 10: Recurrent UTI in a postmenopausal woman

*A 67-year-old woman has had four culture-proven *E. coli* UTIs in the last year. She has vaginal dryness and urgency. PVR 40 mL; renal tract ultrasound normal; no haematuria between episodes.*

**Reasoning.** Recurrent UTI associated with **genitourinary syndrome of menopause** (atrophic changes).

**Action.** **Topical vaginal oestrogen,** hydration, post-coital voiding, consider methenamine hippurate, and low-dose antibiotic prophylaxis if needed; reserve cystoscopy and imaging for atypical features.

#### Case 11: Sterile pyuria

*A 38-year-old man from a high-TB-incidence country has 4 months of dysuria, frequency, mild haematuria and weight loss. Dipstick: leucocytes +++, blood +. Standard cultures negative on three occasions. Three early-morning urine samples: acid-fast bacilli on PCR.*

**Reasoning.** **Genitourinary tuberculosis** (sterile pyuria is a hallmark).

**Action.** Full anti-tuberculous therapy; imaging of the renal tract (CT urography for strictures, calcification), notification, contact tracing, HIV test.

### 32.14 Common pitfalls

- **Treating dipstick findings** rather than symptoms (particularly in older adults and catheterised patients).
- **Prescribing antibiotics for asymptomatic bacteriuria** (except pregnancy and before urological procedures).
- **Missing the obstructed infected kidney** in a septic patient with loin pain.
- **Failing to image men and patients with failure to respond to treatment** after UTI.
- **Failing to take a urine culture** before antibiotics in complicated UTI.
- **Using fluoroquinolones as first-line** for uncomplicated cystitis.
- **Ignoring pregnancy** in women with urinary symptoms or flank pain.
- **Attributing haematuria to anticoagulants or UTI** without investigation or follow-up.
- **Not confirming dipstick haematuria with microscopy.**
- **Failing to perform cystoscopy** for visible haematuria; relying on cytology or ultrasound alone.
- **Missing glomerular causes** (not checking proteinuria, BP, renal function).
- **Overlooking medications** (anticholinergics, decongestants) in retention.
- **Missing cauda equina** in painless urinary retention with back pain.
- **Forgetting digital rectal examination and post-void residual** in men with LUTS.
- **Ordering PSA** without discussion of benefits and harms.

---

### 32.15 Key points

- Classify LUTS into storage, voiding and post-micturition; use bladder diary, urinalysis, PVR and DRE in men; review medications.
- Distinguish polyuria (volume >3 L/day) from frequency; test glucose, calcium, potassium, osmolality.
- Diagnose uncomplicated cystitis clinically with dipstick support; use short courses of narrow-spectrum antibiotics according to local resistance.
- Pyelonephritis: cultures, appropriate antibiotics, and imaging if no rapid response; infected obstructed kidney needs urgent decompression.
- Do not test or treat asymptomatic bacteriuria (except in pregnancy and pre-urological surgery); dipstick-positive confusion in older adults is rarely UTI.
- Acute retention: painful, catheterise, identify causes (BPH, drugs, constipation, cauda equina); painless retention with back pain and saddle numbness is an emergency.
- Haematuria: confirm with microscopy; visible haematuria in adults needs cystoscopy and upper-tract imaging; non-visible haematuria is risk-stratified; glomerular features (proteinuria, dysmorphic RBC, casts, raised creatinine, hypertension) require nephrology.
- Never attribute haematuria to anticoagulation without investigating.

### 32.16 Self-assessment

1. List the three groups of LUTS and give one cause of each.
   *Answer: Storage (overactive bladder), voiding (benign prostatic enlargement, stricture), post-micturition (post-void dribble).*

2. In which situations should asymptomatic bacteriuria be treated?
   *Answer: Pregnancy and before invasive urological procedures with mucosal bleeding.*

3. What urinalysis findings favour glomerular rather than urological haematuria?
   *Answer: Brown/cola urine without clots, proteinuria, dysmorphic red cells or acanthocytes, red cell casts, raised creatinine, hypertension, oedema.*

4. A 66-year-old ex-smoker has a single episode of painless visible haematuria. What are the next steps?
   *Answer: Urgent urology referral for flexible cystoscopy and upper-tract imaging (CT urography or ultrasound), with blood tests, urinalysis, and renal function.*

5. What is sterile pyuria and name four causes.
   *Answer: Leucocytes in urine with negative routine culture; causes include partially treated UTI, urethritis (chlamydia), tuberculosis, stones, interstitial nephritis, bladder tumour, catheter.*

6. When is post-obstructive diuresis a concern and how is it managed?
   *Answer: After relief of chronic or high-volume retention; monitor urine output, electrolytes and fluid balance and replace losses carefully.*
