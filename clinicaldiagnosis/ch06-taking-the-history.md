# Part II: The Clinical Encounter

## Chapter 6. Taking the History

### Learning objectives

After studying this chapter you should be able to:

1. Structure a consultation so that it is efficient, patient-centred and diagnostically productive.
2. Use open and closed questions in the right order, and recognise the effect of leading questions.
3. Elicit the chief complaint and the patient's agenda, ideas, concerns and expectations.
4. Characterise a symptom systematically with the SOCRATES, OLDCARTS and OPQRST frameworks.
5. Take a focused review of systems and ask targeted red-flag questions.
6. Clarify vague terms ("dizzy", "tired", "indigestion") and convert patient language into clinical meaning.
7. Summarise and check understanding, and use the history to generate and test hypotheses.

---

### 6.1 Why the history matters most

In patient after patient, in study after study of how diagnoses are reached, the **history provides the leading diagnosis most of the time**. The examination refines it, and investigations confirm or exclude it. A common teaching figure holds that history alone points to the final diagnosis in the majority of patients seen in general medicine; although the exact proportion varies by setting, the principle is robust: **if you take an excellent history, the rest of the work becomes much easier. If you take a poor one, you will compensate with tests, and the tests will often mislead you.**

The history also does things nothing else does:

- It reveals the **patient's story, their fears and their priorities**, which determine whether any plan you make will be followed.
- It establishes **trust**, which affects how much the patient will tell you later.
- It identifies **risk factors, exposures and timing** that no test can reconstruct.
- It is the only way to know what the symptom **means to this person.**

A history is therefore both a diagnostic procedure and a therapeutic relationship, started in the same few minutes.

---

### 6.2 The structure of a consultation

Several models describe the sequence of a good consultation (Calgary–Cambridge, the Four Habits model, and others). They overlap greatly. A practical version:

1. **Prepare.** Read what is available (referral letter, previous notes, observations). Check the patient's name. Make sure the room is appropriate and that you will not be interrupted if possible.
2. **Initiate the encounter.** Greet the patient by name, introduce yourself and your role, confirm what they would like to be called, explain what you will do, and obtain consent (especially for students or others present). Put the patient at ease: sit at the same level, minimise barriers.
3. **Open the discussion.** Ask an open question: "What brings you in today?" or "How can I help?" Let the patient speak.
4. **Gather the information.** Explore the main complaint(s) in detail, then past, family, social and medication history, then a review of systems (Chapter 7).
5. **Examine** (Part III), explaining each step.
6. **Explain and plan.** Share your thinking, check understanding, negotiate the plan, and give safety-netting advice.
7. **Close.** Summarise, ask if there is anything else, and make arrangements for follow-up.

The history proper has a typical **classic order**:

| Component | Content |
|---|---|
| Chief (presenting) complaint | The problem in the patient's own words |
| History of the presenting complaint | Detailed chronological account and characterisation |
| Past medical and surgical history | Prior illnesses, operations, hospitalisations |
| Medication and allergy history | Prescribed, over-the-counter, herbal, adherence, reactions |
| Family history | Illnesses in relatives, causes of death |
| Social history | Home, work, relationships, habits, travel, exposures |
| Systems review | Targeted questions on other body systems |
| Patient's perspective | Ideas, concerns, expectations (ICE) |

Do not treat this as a rigid script. In practice you will jump between sections as the patient's story demands. The skill lies in **maintaining the thread of the story while ensuring the checklist is covered.**

### 6.3 Opening: let the patient speak

#### The first minute

The opening has a large effect on the rest of the consultation. Research on consultations repeatedly finds that clinicians interrupt patients early, often within the first half minute, and that patients who are allowed to finish their opening statement usually do so within one to two minutes. Interruption leads to *less* information, not more: the patient's most important concern is frequently mentioned last.

Good practice:

- Begin with a **truly open question**: "What brought you in today?" "Tell me what's been happening."
- Use **facilitation** (nodding, "mm-hmm", "go on") and **silence**. A few seconds of silence is often followed by the most important information.
- Don't write continuously or look at the screen throughout. Take brief notes and keep eye contact.
- Use **echoing** ("Chest pain?") or **reflecting** ("That sounds frightening") to invite elaboration.

#### Agenda setting

Patients frequently arrive with more than one concern. If you find out only at the end ("Oh, doctor, while I'm here…"), the consultation can derail. After the opening, ask:

> "So we'll look at the chest pain first. Is there anything else you wanted to discuss today, so that we can plan our time?"

Repeat "Anything else?" until the patient says no. This simple loop dramatically reduces "doorknob" disclosures, in which the real worry surfaces as the patient is leaving.

Then **negotiate the order** if there are many items: urgent safety issues come first; some can be deferred to a second visit by agreement.

#### The patient's ideas, concerns and expectations (ICE)

For every significant symptom, try to learn:

- **Ideas:** "What do you think might be causing this?"
- **Concerns:** "Is there anything in particular that worries you about it?"
- **Expectations:** "What were you hoping we could do today?"

Examples of why this matters: the man with indigestion who fears he will die of a heart attack as his father did; the young woman with headaches who is afraid of a brain tumour; the patient who wants a sick note, not a diagnosis. A technically correct plan that ignores the concern leaves the patient dissatisfied and may not be followed.

### 6.4 Question types and techniques

#### Open questions

"Tell me about the pain." Open questions invite narrative, are less likely to introduce bias, and reveal what the patient considers important. Use them at the start of each new topic.

#### Focused (directed) questions

"Where exactly is the pain? Can you point to it with one finger?" These narrow the topic but still allow the patient to supply the answer.

#### Closed questions

"Does the pain go to your arm?" "Yes/no" questions are efficient for specifics and for checking red flags. Used excessively, they take control from the patient and can cause them to give less.

#### Leading questions

"You don't get any pain in your jaw, do you?" This form suggests the answer, and patients, especially anxious, elderly or deferential ones, tend to agree with the clinician. Avoid it. Instead: "Does the pain go anywhere else?" and then, if needed, give a neutral list: "Does it go to your arm, jaw, back, or somewhere else?"

#### Multiple questions

"Have you had any fever, chills, night sweats or weight loss?" The patient answers the last item or the easiest. Ask one question at a time.

#### Funnelling

A powerful sequence is to go from **open → focused → closed** within each topic:

1. "Tell me about the cough." (open)
2. "What does the cough bring up?" (focused)
3. "Is the sputum ever blood-stained?" (closed)

Then summarise before moving to the next topic.

#### Other techniques

- **Clarification:** "When you say 'dizzy', what do you mean?" (see 6.8).
- **Summarising:** "Let me check I've understood. You've had..." Summaries correct errors, show listening and give the patient a chance to add.
- **Signposting:** "Now I'd like to ask about your past health."
- **Empathic statements:** "That must have been very worrying." These are not wasted time; they make later disclosure more likely.
- **Normalising sensitive topics:** "Many people with this problem also have..." before asking about sexual function, mood or alcohol.
- **Using the patient's language:** reflect the words they use; avoid jargon, and explain it when unavoidable.

### 6.5 Characterising a symptom

The symptom is the central unit of a medical history. Every symptom has dimensions. A systematic checklist guarantees that nothing is forgotten and makes the account comparable between clinicians.

#### SOCRATES (especially for pain)

| Letter | Question | Examples of what you learn |
|---|---|---|
| **S**ite | Where exactly? | Central chest vs. left-sided; right iliac fossa vs. periumbilical |
| **O**nset | When and how did it start? Sudden or gradual? What was the patient doing? | Sudden onset: rupture, embolism, torsion. Gradual: inflammation, neoplasm |
| **C**haracter | What is it like? | Pressure/tight (ischaemia), sharp/pleuritic (pleura, pericardium), burning (reflux, nerve), colicky (hollow viscus), tearing (dissection) |
| **R**adiation | Does it spread? | Left arm/jaw (cardiac), back (pancreas, aorta), shoulder tip (diaphragm irritation), groin (ureter) |
| **A**ssociations | What else occurs with it? | Sweating, nausea, breathlessness, vomiting, fever, urinary or bowel change |
| **T**ime course | Constant or intermittent? Duration of episodes? Trend? | 20-minute episodes (angina) vs. seconds (musculoskeletal) vs. hours (unstable) |
| **E**xacerbating / relieving factors | What makes it better or worse? | Exertion, food, posture, breathing, movement, rest, medications |
| **S**everity | How bad? | Scale 0–10; effect on activity, sleep, work |

#### OLDCARTS and OPQRST

OLDCARTS (Onset, Location, Duration, Character, Aggravating/Alleviating factors, Radiation, Timing, Severity) and OPQRST (Onset, Provocation/Palliation, Quality, Radiation, Severity, Time) cover the same ground and are used widely in emergency and primary care. Choose one and know it well; the framework matters less than the habit.

#### Non-pain symptoms

For a symptom such as cough, fatigue or vomiting, adapt the same logic:

- **When did it begin, and how?** (Onset)
- **Pattern** (constant, episodic, progressive, diurnal variation)
- **Quality** (what exactly is the experience?)
- **Severity and impact** (what can't they do now?)
- **Associated symptoms** (especially those that would distinguish between diagnoses)
- **What makes it better or worse?**
- **What has been tried, and what happened?**
- **Previous episodes?**

#### Chronology

Build a **timeline.** Ask: "When were you last completely well?" Then walk forward: "What happened next?" Chronologies expose progression, triggers and patterns that are lost in a list of symptoms. In an emergency, anchor on times of onset (stroke, MI, sepsis treatments are time-dependent).

#### Quantifying

Convert vague words into measurable things where possible.

- "A lot of blood" → "How much? A teaspoon, a cup? How many pads or tissues?"
- "Walks very little" → "How far can you walk on the flat before you must stop? How many stairs?"
- "Drinks a lot" → units per week.
- "Weight loss" → how much, over what time, intentional or not?
- "Fever" → measured temperature or just felt hot?

#### Functional impact

Ask what the symptom stops the patient from doing. The answer reveals severity and what matters to the patient, and sets the goal of treatment.

### 6.6 Focused questions for hypothesis testing

Once you have a few hypotheses (Chapter 3), tailor questions to discriminate between them. Instead of asking everything, ask what **changes the probabilities.**

**Example: acute breathlessness.**

| To raise or lower | Ask |
|---|---|
| Asthma | Wheeze? Night or early morning symptoms? Triggers (cold air, allergens)? Personal/family atopy? |
| Heart failure | Orthopnoea (how many pillows)? Paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnoea? Ankle swelling? Weight gain? |
| Pulmonary embolism | Sudden onset? Pleuritic pain? Haemoptysis? Leg swelling? Recent surgery, travel, immobility, cancer, hormones? Previous VTE? |
| Pneumonia | Fever, productive cough, rigors, pleuritic pain? |
| Anaemia | Heavy periods, black stool, diet? |
| Anxiety / hyperventilation | Tingling of fingers and lips, sense of panic, situational trigger |

Notice that every question is tied to a hypothesis. An unfocused list of questions asked in a fixed sequence regardless of the presentation is inefficient and risks missing the nuance of the particular patient.

#### Pertinent negatives

Documenting the **absence** of certain features is as important as the presence of others. "No fever, no cough, no haemoptysis, no leg swelling" in a patient with chest pain tells the next reader which diagnoses you have already considered.

### 6.7 The review of systems and red flags

A **review of systems (ROS)** is a set of brief questions about each body system, to detect symptoms the patient did not mention. It is not necessary to ask all questions of all patients; adapt to the presentation, but do cover key systems when the presentation is vague or the patient is a new one.

A concise ROS:

- **General:** fever, night sweats, weight change, fatigue, appetite.
- **Cardiovascular:** chest pain, palpitations, orthopnoea, oedema, claudication, syncope.
- **Respiratory:** cough, sputum, haemoptysis, wheeze, breathlessness.
- **Gastrointestinal:** dysphagia, nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, change in bowel habit, blood in stool, jaundice.
- **Genitourinary:** frequency, dysuria, haematuria, incontinence, sexual function; menstrual and obstetric history (Chapter 19).
- **Neurological:** headache, weakness, numbness, vision changes, seizures, speech or memory problems.
- **Musculoskeletal:** joint pain, swelling, stiffness, back pain.
- **Skin:** rash, itch, lumps, mole changes.
- **Endocrine:** heat or cold intolerance, polyuria, polydipsia.
- **Haematological:** bruising, bleeding.
- **Psychiatric:** mood, sleep, anxiety, suicidal thoughts.

#### Red flags

A red flag is a feature in the history that raises the probability of serious disease. Learn those for the major presentations (Chapter 72). Examples:

| Presentation | Red-flag questions |
|---|---|
| Headache | Sudden "worst ever"? New after age 50? Fever or neck stiffness? Neurological symptoms? Worse lying down or on coughing? Cancer or immunosuppression? Recent head injury? |
| Back pain | Age <20 or >55? Trauma? Fever? Cancer history? Unexplained weight loss? Night pain? Saddle numbness? Bladder or bowel dysfunction? Bilateral leg weakness? |
| Abdominal pain | Persistent vomiting? Blood in vomit or stool? Weight loss? Dysphagia? Pain waking from sleep? Pregnancy possible? |
| Chest pain | Exertional? Associated with breathlessness, sweating, syncope? Tearing pain to back? Pleuritic with leg swelling? |
| Cough | >3 weeks? Haemoptysis? Weight loss? Fever, night sweats? Smoking history? Exposure to TB? |

Ask red-flag questions routinely, not only when you are already suspicious. Where the answer is "yes", explore it fully.

### 6.8 Clarifying patient language

Patients use words differently from clinicians. A few words are notoriously ambiguous, and misinterpreting them leads to wrong pathways.

#### "Dizzy"

May mean:

- **Vertigo:** a sense of spinning, of self or surroundings (inner ear, brainstem).
- **Presyncope:** near-faint, "going to black out" (cardiovascular, orthostatic, anaemia).
- **Disequilibrium:** unsteadiness on walking (neuropathy, cerebellar, multi-sensory deficit).
- **Non-specific lightheadedness:** often anxiety, hyperventilation, metabolic, medication-related.

Ask: "Is it a spinning feeling, a feeling you're going to pass out, or unsteadiness on your feet?"

#### "Tired"

May mean sleepiness, muscle weakness, low mood, exhaustion after exertion, or breathlessness. Ask: "Do you fall asleep during the day? Do your muscles feel weak? Do you feel low or lack interest in things?"

#### "Indigestion" or "heartburn"

Can be reflux, peptic ulcer, biliary pain or angina. Ask about relation to food, exertion, radiation and associated features.

#### "Diarrhoea"

Might mean loose stools, frequent stools, urgency, incontinence, or "overflow" from constipation. Ask about consistency, frequency, blood, nocturnal symptoms, and weight loss.

#### "Weakness"

Could be true loss of power (neurological, muscular), fatigue, or pain-limited movement. Ask to demonstrate what they cannot do.

#### "Blackout"

Ask for a collateral account: loss of consciousness with sudden collapse and rapid recovery suggests syncope; prolonged confusion after or tongue-biting suggests seizure.

#### "Palpitations"

Ask the patient to **tap out the rhythm** on the table: regular fast, irregular, or missing beats.

General rule: **whenever a patient uses a diagnostic label ("I have a migraine", "it's my gallbladder", "I had a heart attack"), ask what they mean and what happened, and why they think so.** Labels may be right, wrong or borrowed from a relative.

### 6.9 Special situations in history taking

#### The patient who gives a long, rambling account

Allow a short period of free speech, then gently structure: "I'd like to make sure I understand the main problems. Could we go back to when the pain first started?" Use summaries and signposting to regain control without disrespect. Time is limited; politely reframe: "We have about 15 minutes; let's focus on what's bothering you most."

#### The patient who gives minimal information

Use open prompts, silence and reflective statements. Consider whether the patient is anxious, in pain, mistrustful, embarrassed, intoxicated, cognitively impaired or from a culture where direct complaint is discouraged. Ask: "Is there anything that is making it hard to talk about this today?" Collateral history may be essential (Chapter 8).

#### The anxious patient

Explore the content of the anxiety ("What do you fear it might be?"), validate it, and avoid premature reassurance ("It's nothing") before you have assessed; reassurance given too early often fails and loses credibility.

#### The patient in pain or distress

Begin with relief: analgesia, comfort, a quiet place. Obtain a *brief* history focused on the immediate problem; return for details once stable.

#### The unconscious or confused patient

Rely on collateral history from relatives, ambulance crew, medical alert bracelets, medicine lists, GP records, witness statements. Ask: "When was the patient last seen well? What happened? Any history of epilepsy, diabetes, drugs, alcohol, depression, head injury?"

#### The child

The history comes from the parent or carer, but include the child as much as age allows. Observe the child's behaviour while you talk: it is a diagnostic sign. Ask about feeding, wet nappies, activity, immunisations, developmental milestones and birth history (Chapter 59).

#### Time-critical presentations

In an emergency, switch to a **focused "AMPLE" history:** Allergies, Medications, Past history, Last meal, Events leading to the presentation. Complete the full history once the patient is stable.

---

### 6.10 Documenting and using the history

Write the history in a structured, legible way, with the patient's own words in quotation marks where useful. Distinguish what the patient said from what you inferred. Record pertinent positives and negatives, times, and sources of information (patient, relative, notes). A well-written history is a communication tool for the entire team.

Finally, **use** it. Before you start the examination, stop and ask: "What are my top three hypotheses? What do I expect to find on examination if each is true?" This transforms the examination from a ritual into a test of hypotheses.

### 6.11 Worked cases (with sample dialogue)

#### Case 1: A "heart burn" that is not

*A 58-year-old man attends with "indigestion" for 2 weeks.*

**Doctor:** What brings you in today?
**Patient:** I've got this indigestion. It keeps coming and it's getting worse.
**Doctor:** Tell me about it from the beginning.
**Patient:** About two weeks ago I had a burning feeling in my chest after climbing the stairs at work. I thought it was the spicy lunch.
**Doctor:** Where exactly did you feel it?
**Patient:** Here, in the middle of my chest [places fist on sternum].
**Doctor:** What happened to it?
**Patient:** It went away after I sat down for a few minutes. It came back this week when I was carrying shopping.
**Doctor:** What did it feel like?
**Patient:** Heavy, tight. Not really burning, more pressure.
**Doctor:** Did it spread anywhere?
**Patient:** My left arm felt a bit funny.
**Doctor:** Any sweating, breathlessness, or feeling sick with it?
**Patient:** A bit breathless, yes.
**Doctor:** Does it come with eating, or with effort?
**Patient:** Effort, I think, now you ask. Not so much with meals.
**Doctor:** What worries you about it?
**Patient:** My father died at 60 of a heart attack.

*Analysis.* The patient's label ("indigestion") nearly misled. Structured questioning (site, character, radiation, provoking factors, relief, associations, ICE) reveals **exertional chest pressure relieved by rest, radiating to the arm, with breathlessness and a family history**: a classic angina story, now possibly crescendo (worsening over 2 weeks), which is a high-risk pattern. The next steps are an ECG, vital signs, risk assessment, and same-day cardiology pathway. Had the doctor simply accepted "indigestion", he might have prescribed an antacid.

#### Case 2: "Dizzy"

*A 72-year-old woman reports dizziness for a week.*

**Doctor:** When you say "dizzy", what is it like?
**Patient:** I feel like the room goes round when I turn over in bed.
**Doctor:** How long does it last?
**Patient:** About 20 seconds. Then it goes.
**Doctor:** Does it happen if you're sitting still?
**Patient:** No. Only when I look up or turn over in bed.
**Doctor:** Any hearing change, ringing in the ears, headache, double vision, weakness or numbness, or difficulty speaking?
**Patient:** No. I feel a bit sick when it's happening.
**Doctor:** Any fainting or palpitations?
**Patient:** No.

*Analysis.* Clarifying the word shows **brief positional vertigo** with no neurological or cardiovascular features, a typical story for **benign paroxysmal positional vertigo (BPPV)**. A positional test (Dix–Hallpike) will confirm it (Chapter 24). Contrast this with "dizzy" meaning presyncope on standing (suggesting orthostatic hypotension), where the investigation and management differ completely.

#### Case 3: The agenda hidden at the end

*A 34-year-old woman attends about "a cough." Part-way through the consultation, the doctor asks the agenda-setting question.*

**Doctor:** So we'll talk about the cough. Is there anything else you wanted to discuss today?
**Patient:** Well... I've been feeling very low for a few weeks. I wasn't going to mention it.
**Doctor:** I'm glad you did. We can talk about both. Shall we start with the cough, so I can check it's nothing serious, and then spend some time on how you're feeling?

*Analysis.* The agenda-setting loop revealed the real concern, which would otherwise have emerged at the door or never. It also protected the diagnosis of the cough, which gets the time it needs, without hurrying the more sensitive topic.

#### Case 4: A collateral history that changes the diagnosis

*A 78-year-old man is brought in "confused for two days". The patient says he is fine.*

His daughter reports that he has fallen twice recently, is more sleepy in the daytime, and has had a headache for a week. He takes warfarin for atrial fibrillation. She adds that his speech has become slower and he seems less alert than "last Sunday."

*Analysis.* The patient's own account is inadequate because of confusion and limited insight. Collateral information offers: **an acute change from baseline (delirium), falls, headache, anticoagulation.** This triad raises the possibility of **chronic subdural haematoma** and makes urgent CT head essential. An approach that took "confusion, probably UTI" would have been a serious error (Chapter 2).

#### Case 5: Quantifying the vague

*A 45-year-old man says he "drinks a bit" and has "lost some weight."*

Follow-up questions: "On a typical week, how many drinks do you have? What do you drink? Is there any binge drinking?" → 8 pints of beer on weekdays plus bottle of wine on weekend (~ 50+ units). "How much weight, over how long?" → 8 kg in 3 months, unintentional, with reduced appetite and early satiety.

*Analysis.* "A bit" and "some weight" concealed both alcohol-related harm and a worrying unintentional weight loss, a red flag requiring a structured investigation (Chapter 30). Without quantification, both would have been missed or normalised.

### 6.12 Common pitfalls

- **Interrupting early.** It reduces information and satisfaction.
- **Asking only closed questions.** The patient's story is lost.
- **Leading questions.** Produce false positives.
- **Accepting patient or previous labels uncritically.**
- **Not clarifying vague terms.**
- **Forgetting the patient's ideas, concerns and expectations.**
- **A history that goes into the notes but not into the thinking.** Always generate hypotheses before the examination.
- **Not asking about medication, alcohol, recreational drugs, or herbal products.**
- **Neglecting collateral information** when the patient cannot give a reliable history.
- **Rushing to reassure.**

---

### 6.13 Key points

- The history is the most powerful diagnostic tool; it usually generates the leading diagnosis.
- Start open, listen without interrupting, set the agenda, and explore the patient's ideas, concerns and expectations.
- Funnel from open to focused to closed questions; avoid leading and multiple questions.
- Characterise every important symptom systematically (SOCRATES, OLDCARTS, OPQRST), build a timeline, and quantify vague terms.
- Ask hypothesis-driven questions and document pertinent negatives; use red-flag questions routinely.
- Clarify ambiguous words ("dizzy", "tired", "indigestion") and ask what patients mean by diagnostic labels.
- Obtain collateral history when the patient cannot give a reliable account.
- Summarise and check understanding.

### 6.14 Self-assessment

1. What does SOCRATES stand for, and what is it used for?
   *Answer: Site, Onset, Character, Radiation, Associations, Time course, Exacerbating/relieving factors, Severity; used for systematically characterising pain.*

2. Why should you avoid asking "You don't have any chest pain, do you?"
   *Answer: It is a leading question; it prompts the answer "no" and may suppress true positives.*

3. List three different meanings of "dizzy" and one distinguishing question for each.
   *Answer: vertigo (spinning?), presyncope (feeling you will faint, worse on standing?), disequilibrium (unsteady while walking?).*

4. A patient says, "I had a heart attack last year." What should you do?
   *Answer: Ask what happened: symptoms, tests (ECG, troponin, angiogram), treatment (stents, surgery), medications, follow-up. A lay label may or may not reflect a documented myocardial infarction.*

5. Why is the question "Is there anything else?" worth repeating?
   *Answer: It surfaces additional concerns early, avoids late disclosure at the end of the consultation, and helps with prioritisation and time management.*

6. In Case 4, which findings made delirium from a structural cause more likely than a urinary infection?
   *Answer: Falls, headache, anticoagulant therapy, increasing drowsiness and slowed speech, acute change from baseline.*
