## Chapter 25. Abdominal Pain

### Learning objectives

After studying this chapter you should be able to:

1. Explain the mechanisms of visceral, parietal and referred abdominal pain and use them to localise disease.
2. Generate a differential diagnosis by anatomical region, and identify the "can't-miss" causes of acute abdominal pain.
3. Take a history and perform an examination that separate surgical from non-surgical causes, and recognise peritonitis, obstruction, ischaemia and haemorrhage.
4. Select investigations rationally (blood tests, urinalysis, pregnancy test, ECG, radiography, ultrasound, CT) and interpret scoring systems such as the Alvarado score.
5. Recognise how the presentation changes in older adults, pregnant women, children, immunosuppressed patients and post-operative patients.
6. Approach chronic and recurrent abdominal pain, including red flags and functional disorders (Rome IV).
7. Provide safe analgesia, early senior input and effective safety-netting.

---

### 25.1 The problem of abdominal pain

Abdominal pain accounts for a large share of emergency attendances and primary-care consultations. The causes range from self-limiting gastroenteritis to ruptured aortic aneurysm. Three points shape the approach:

1. **Most patients do not have a surgical emergency, but a few have a disease that will kill them in hours.** The task is to identify the few quickly and without over-investigating the many.
2. **Abdominal pain does not always originate in the abdomen.** Myocardial infarction, pneumonia, pulmonary embolism, diabetic ketoacidosis, testicular torsion, herpes zoster and spinal disease can all present with it.
3. **Presentations are atypical in the groups at highest risk.** Older, pregnant, immunosuppressed and very young patients may have catastrophic disease with minimal signs.

The standard of care today is to **give analgesia early** (this does not mask the diagnosis, and withholding it is cruel and unhelpful), to **examine repeatedly** when the diagnosis is uncertain, to **image appropriately**, and to **involve a senior surgeon early** in the unstable or peritonitic patient.

### 25.2 Mechanisms and localisation

#### Types of pain

| Type | Mechanism | Character and localisation |
|---|---|---|
| **Visceral** | Stretch, spasm, inflammation or ischaemia of hollow or solid organs; transmitted by autonomic (sympathetic) afferents, bilateral and segmental | Dull, cramping, colicky, poorly localised, felt in the **midline**; patient restless and may writhe; often with nausea, vomiting, sweating |
| **Parietal (somatic)** | Irritation of the parietal peritoneum or abdominal wall; transmitted by somatic spinal nerves | **Sharp, constant, well-localised**, worse with movement, coughing; patient lies still; guarding and rebound |
| **Referred** | Convergence of visceral and somatic afferents at the same spinal segment | Pain felt at a **distant site** of the same dermatome (e.g. shoulder tip with diaphragmatic irritation) |

#### Embryological pattern of visceral pain

Visceral pain is felt in the midline in a region corresponding to the embryological origin of the organ:

| Embryological gut | Organs | Pain felt in |
|---|---|---|
| **Foregut** | Stomach, duodenum, liver, biliary tract, pancreas | Epigastrium |
| **Midgut** | Small intestine, appendix, ascending colon, proximal transverse colon | Periumbilical region |
| **Hindgut** | Distal transverse colon, descending and sigmoid colon, rectum | Hypogastrium (suprapubic) |

This explains the classic **migration of appendicitis** pain: initially periumbilical (visceral, midgut) and then localised to the right iliac fossa (parietal) as the inflamed appendix irritates the parietal peritoneum.

#### Classic referred pain

| Site of referred pain | Source |
|---|---|
| **Right shoulder tip** | Diaphragmatic irritation (cholecystitis, liver abscess, subphrenic collection) |
| **Left shoulder tip (Kehr sign)** | Splenic rupture, pancreatitis, gastric perforation |
| **Right infrascapular region** | Biliary colic |
| **Back, between the scapulae** | Pancreas, aorta, oesophagus |
| **Loin to groin** | Ureteric colic |
| **Testis** | Ureter (renal colic), appendix |
| **Medial thigh** | Obturator hernia, ovary |
| **Epigastrium** | Inferior myocardial infarction |

### 25.3 The differential diagnosis by location

#### Right upper quadrant

Biliary colic, acute cholecystitis, **cholangitis**, hepatitis, liver abscess, hepatic congestion (heart failure), Budd–Chiari syndrome, Fitz-Hugh–Curtis syndrome (perihepatitis in pelvic inflammatory disease), duodenal ulcer, right lower lobe pneumonia, pulmonary embolism, right pyelonephritis or stone, hepatic flexure colon pathology, herpes zoster, appendicitis (high-lying, especially in pregnancy).

#### Epigastrium

Dyspepsia, gastritis, **peptic ulcer disease (and perforation)**, **pancreatitis**, biliary disease, **inferior myocardial infarction**, **aortic dissection/aneurysm**, oesophageal disease, gastric cancer, **early appendicitis**, pericarditis, DKA.

#### Left upper quadrant

Splenic infarct, rupture or abscess, gastric pathology, pancreatic tail disease, left lower lobe pneumonia, left pyelonephritis or stone, splenic flexure colon pathology, myocardial infarction.

#### Periumbilical

Early appendicitis, small bowel obstruction, gastroenteritis, mesenteric ischaemia, inflammatory bowel disease, pancreatitis, **aortic aneurysm**, umbilical hernia.

#### Right iliac fossa

**Appendicitis**, mesenteric adenitis, Crohn's disease (terminal ileitis), caecal pathology (diverticulitis, carcinoma), Meckel's diverticulitis, right ureteric stone, **ovarian pathology (cyst, torsion, tubo-ovarian abscess)**, **ectopic pregnancy**, pelvic inflammatory disease, endometriosis, inguinal or femoral hernia, psoas abscess, testicular torsion (referred), Yersinia ileitis.

#### Left iliac fossa

**Diverticulitis**, constipation, ischaemic colitis, inflammatory bowel disease, colon cancer, left ureteric stone, ovarian pathology, ectopic pregnancy, hernia, irritable bowel syndrome.

#### Suprapubic

Cystitis, urinary retention, prostatitis, pelvic inflammatory disease, endometriosis, fibroids, ovarian cysts, ectopic pregnancy, sigmoid colon disease, **early pregnancy complications**, rectal pathology.

#### Flank and loin

Renal colic, pyelonephritis, renal infarct, perinephric abscess, retroperitoneal haemorrhage, **aortic aneurysm (leaking)**, musculoskeletal, herpes zoster.

#### Diffuse

Peritonitis (perforation, ruptured appendix, pancreatitis), bowel obstruction, **mesenteric ischaemia**, gastroenteritis, DKA, porphyria, sickle cell crisis, familial Mediterranean fever, lead poisoning, opioid withdrawal, functional (IBS), adrenal insufficiency, hypercalcaemia, uraemia, abdominal wall pain.

#### Extra-abdominal causes (not to be missed)

| System | Conditions |
|---|---|
| **Cardiac** | Inferior MI, pericarditis, aortic dissection, heart failure (hepatic congestion), endocarditis (splenic infarcts) |
| **Respiratory** | Lower lobe pneumonia, pleurisy, pulmonary embolism, pneumothorax |
| **Metabolic and endocrine** | Diabetic ketoacidosis, adrenal crisis, hypercalcaemia, uraemia, porphyria, hyperthyroidism, lead poisoning |
| **Haematological** | Sickle cell crisis, leukaemia, haemolysis |
| **Neurological and spinal** | Herpes zoster, radiculopathy, tabes dorsalis |
| **Genitourinary** | Testicular torsion, epididymitis, urinary retention |
| **Toxic** | Alcohol, drug withdrawal, heavy metal poisoning, black widow spider bite |
| **Systemic inflammatory** | Henoch–Schönlein purpura (IgA vasculitis), familial Mediterranean fever, SLE, vasculitis |

### 25.4 The "can't-miss" list in acute abdominal pain

| Condition | Why it is dangerous | Clue |
|---|---|---|
| **Ruptured or leaking abdominal aortic aneurysm** | Exsanguination; mortality high | Older man, smoker, hypertension; sudden severe abdominal/back/flank pain, collapse, pulsatile expansile mass; mimics renal colic |
| **Perforated viscus** (ulcer, diverticulum, cancer, appendix) | Peritonitis, sepsis | Sudden severe pain, rigidity, absent bowel sounds, free air |
| **Mesenteric ischaemia** (acute) | Gut infarction; mortality 50% or more with delay | Severe pain out of proportion to signs; AF, vascular disease, hypotension; raised lactate (late) |
| **Strangulated bowel obstruction or hernia** | Bowel necrosis | Colicky then constant pain, vomiting, distension, irreducible tender hernia |
| **Volvulus** (sigmoid, caecal, gastric) | Ischaemia, perforation | Distension, constipation, elderly or neuropsychiatric patient |
| **Ectopic pregnancy** | Haemorrhage, death | Woman of reproductive age; amenorrhoea; shock |
| **Ovarian or testicular torsion** | Loss of organ | Sudden severe unilateral pain; vomiting; boys and adolescents |
| **Acute appendicitis** | Perforation | Migratory pain, anorexia, RIF tenderness |
| **Acute cholangitis** | Sepsis | Charcot triad: fever, jaundice, RUQ pain; Reynolds pentad adds hypotension and confusion |
| **Severe acute pancreatitis** | Organ failure | Epigastric pain to the back, vomiting; shock; Cullen/Grey Turner signs |
| **DKA** | Metabolic emergency | Diabetes, abdominal pain with vomiting, Kussmaul breathing, ketone smell |
| **Myocardial infarction, pulmonary embolism, aortic dissection** | Cardiovascular catastrophe | Epigastric pain with risk factors; abnormal ECG |
| **Spontaneous bacterial peritonitis** | Sepsis in cirrhosis | Ascites, fever, mild abdominal pain, encephalopathy |
| **Perforated or toxic colitis** (megacolon, ischaemic) | Perforation | Bloody diarrhoea, distension, systemic toxicity |
| **Necrotising pancreatitis/intra-abdominal sepsis** | Mortality | Persistent fever, deterioration |

### 25.5 History

Use the framework of Chapter 6 (SOCRATES) and focus on discriminating features.

#### Pain characteristics

| Feature | Question | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| **Onset** | Sudden (seconds–minutes), rapid (hours), gradual (days)? | Sudden: perforation, rupture, torsion, embolism, stone; rapid: obstruction, inflammation; gradual: infection, malignancy, chronic disease |
| **Site and shift** | Where did it start? Has it moved? | Migration (periumbilical → RIF): appendicitis |
| **Character** | Colicky (waves with pain-free intervals), constant, burning, tearing, sharp? | Colic: obstructed hollow viscus, ureter or bile duct (though biliary "colic" is usually constant); constant: inflammation, peritonitis, ischaemia; tearing: dissection |
| **Severity** | 0–10; compare with other pains | **Pain out of proportion to examination:** mesenteric ischaemia |
| **Radiation** | Back, shoulder, groin, testis | See referred pain |
| **Timing** | Duration of episodes; relation to meals, defecation, micturition, menstruation | Meals: peptic ulcer (duodenal: relief or night pain; gastric: worse with eating), biliary (fatty meal, 30–60 minutes), mesenteric angina (post-prandial, weight loss); menstruation: endometriosis, dysmenorrhoea; defecation: colonic |
| **Exacerbating/relieving** | Movement, coughing, position, food, antacids, vomiting, passing flatus, leaning forward | Movement worsens peritonitis; lying still; leaning forward relieves pancreatitis; vomiting may relieve obstruction temporarily; antacids relieve peptic pain |
| **Behaviour** | Writhing and unable to get comfortable vs lying still | Colic (ureteric, biliary, intestinal) vs peritonitis |

#### Associated symptoms

- **Gastrointestinal:** anorexia (strongly associated with appendicitis), nausea, vomiting (bilious, faeculent, haematemesis), diarrhoea (blood, mucus, volume), constipation, **obstipation (no stool or flatus)**, jaundice, change in stool or urine colour, weight loss, dysphagia, indigestion.
- **Genitourinary:** dysuria, frequency, haematuria, urethral discharge, scrotal pain.
- **Gynaecological:** last menstrual period, irregular bleeding, vaginal discharge, contraception, possibility of pregnancy, previous ectopic, pelvic infection, IUD.
- **General:** fever, rigors, sweats, weight loss, malaise.
- **Cardiorespiratory:** chest pain, breathlessness, cough, palpitations, syncope.

#### Background

- **Past history:** previous abdominal surgery (adhesions, hernia), similar episodes, known gallstones, ulcers, diverticular disease, IBD, aneurysm, cancer, cardiovascular disease, atrial fibrillation (embolic mesenteric ischaemia), diabetes, HIV, sickle cell disease, porphyria, familial Mediterranean fever.
- **Medications:** NSAIDs, aspirin, corticosteroids, anticoagulants, opioids (constipation, sphincter of Oddi spasm), antibiotics (*C. difficile*), chemotherapy, oestrogen (thrombosis), anticholinergics, laxatives.
- **Alcohol, smoking, drug use.**
- **Travel, diet, sick contacts, food poisoning.**
- **Family history:** IBD, coeliac disease, cancer, aneurysm.
- **Social:** safeguarding concerns (abuse, trauma).

### 25.6 Examination

(See Chapter 14 for detail.) In an acute presentation:

1. **First look at the patient's general appearance and vital signs.** Tachycardia, hypotension, fever, tachypnoea, confusion, mottling, low urine output, delayed capillary refill: signs of shock or sepsis. Provide resuscitation concurrent with assessment.
2. **Posture and behaviour:** restless writhing (colic) vs still (peritonitis).
3. **Hydration and perfusion.**
4. **Abdomen:** inspection (distension, scars, hernias, Cullen/Grey Turner signs), auscultation (bowel sounds, bruits), percussion (tympany, dullness, percussion tenderness), palpation (light then deep: tenderness, guarding, rigidity, masses, pulsatile aorta), specific signs (Murphy, Rovsing, psoas, obturator), hernial orifices, genitalia (testes in males), rectal examination when indicated, pelvic examination in women when indicated.
5. **Other systems:** chest, heart, legs (DVT), skin (rash: HSP, zoster), signs of liver disease.
6. **Urine dipstick and pregnancy test** (do not leave to the end).

**Peritonitis** is a clinical diagnosis: rigid abdomen, guarding, percussion or rebound tenderness, silence, patient lies still. Its presence indicates the need for early surgical review and, usually, imaging or laparotomy.

**Diagnostic accuracy** of single signs is limited, so **repeated examination** (ideally by the same clinician) and the evolution of signs are valuable (Chapter 5).

### 25.7 Investigations

Tests should be targeted to the leading and can't-miss hypotheses (Chapter 4).

#### Bedside tests

- **Urine dipstick** (blood: stone or infection; leucocytes/nitrites: infection; ketones and glucose: DKA; protein; bilirubin).
- **Pregnancy test** in all women of reproductive age.
- **Capillary glucose and ketones.**
- **ECG** in epigastric pain, older patients, or those with cardiac risk factors or AF.
- **Point-of-care ultrasound:** free fluid, aortic diameter (AAA), gallstones, hydronephrosis, ectopic pregnancy, inferior vena cava, cardiac function.

#### Blood tests

| Test | Use |
|---|---|
| **Full blood count** | Infection (leucocytosis; also can be low in sepsis), anaemia (bleeding), platelets |
| **CRP** | Inflammation (non-specific; rises over 6–12 hours); trend helpful |
| **U&E, creatinine** | Dehydration, AKI, electrolytes |
| **Liver function tests** | Biliary obstruction, hepatitis |
| **Lipase** (preferred) or amylase | Pancreatitis (diagnosis requires pain, lipase >3 times the upper limit, or typical imaging; any two of three) |
| **Lactate / blood gas** | Ischaemia, sepsis, DKA; a normal lactate does **not** exclude mesenteric ischaemia |
| **Glucose, ketones** | DKA |
| **Coagulation, group and save/cross-match** | Bleeding, pre-operative |
| **Troponin** | Cardiac cause |
| **Blood cultures** | Sepsis, cholangitis, abscess |
| **Calcium, triglycerides** | Pancreatitis causes |
| **Others:** β-hCG (serum), HIV, hepatitis serology, coeliac serology, calprotectin (chronic). |

White cell count and CRP are neither sensitive nor specific for appendicitis individually, but a **normal WBC and CRP together make appendicitis unlikely**, particularly if the symptoms have lasted more than 24 hours.

#### Imaging

| Modality | Role |
|---|---|
| **Erect chest X-ray** | Free air under the diaphragm (perforation), lower lobe pneumonia, pleural effusion; about 70–80% of perforations show free air |
| **Abdominal X-ray (supine)** | Limited value; may show bowel obstruction (dilated loops: small bowel >3 cm, colon >6 cm, caecum >9 cm), toxic megacolon, volvulus (coffee-bean sign), calcified stones, constipation; low sensitivity for most conditions. Not recommended as a routine in acute abdominal pain |
| **Ultrasound** | First-line for RUQ pain (gallstones, cholecystitis, bile duct dilatation), pelvic pathology (ectopic pregnancy, ovarian cyst, torsion), suspected appendicitis in children and young women, hydronephrosis, AAA, free fluid |
| **CT abdomen/pelvis with IV contrast** | Most sensitive for most acute pathologies in adults: appendicitis, diverticulitis, perforation, obstruction, abscess, ischaemia (CT angiography), pancreatitis and complications, aortic pathology, renal stones (non-contrast low-dose CT). Use judiciously in young patients and in pregnancy because of radiation |
| **MRI** | Pregnancy (appendicitis if ultrasound is equivocal), MRCP for biliary disease, inflammatory bowel disease |
| **Contrast studies, endoscopy** | Specific indications (gastrointestinal bleeding, suspected perforation, obstruction) |
| **Diagnostic laparoscopy** | When diagnosis remains unclear in a deteriorating patient |

#### Scoring systems

- **Alvarado score for appendicitis** (maximum 10): migration of pain to RIF (1), anorexia (1), nausea/vomiting (1), tenderness in RIF (2), rebound tenderness (1), elevated temperature ≥37.3 °C (1), leucocytosis >10 (2), left shift (neutrophilia) (1). Interpretation (varies by setting): ≤4 unlikely; 5–6 possible, observe or image; ≥7 probable, surgical review. Alternative: AIR score and Paediatric Appendicitis Score. Scores support decisions but do not replace clinical judgement and imaging in uncertain cases.
- **Ranson, Glasgow-Imrie, BISAP, APACHE II** for pancreatitis severity.
- **Tokyo Guidelines** for cholecystitis and cholangitis.
- **Rockall and Glasgow-Blatchford** for GI bleeding (Chapter 27).
- **Early warning scores (NEWS2)** for general deterioration.

### 25.8 Management principles (brief)

- **Resuscitate first:** IV access, fluids, oxygen, monitor. For peritonitis and sepsis: antibiotics within the first hour for shock; cultures; early senior input.
- **Analgesia:** titrated IV opioid (e.g. morphine) and/or paracetamol, and NSAID for renal colic if not contraindicated. Analgesia does not impair diagnostic accuracy in adults or children and should not be withheld.
- **Nil by mouth** if surgery may be needed; **IV fluids**; correct electrolytes; **antiemetics**; **nasogastric tube** for obstruction with vomiting.
- **Early surgical review** for peritonitis, obstruction, ischaemia, perforation, haemorrhage, or uncertain diagnosis with deterioration.
- **Antibiotics** for confirmed or likely intra-abdominal infection.
- **Do not delay surgery** in the unstable patient with a ruptured aneurysm or perforation for further imaging.
- **Observe and re-examine** at defined intervals if the diagnosis is uncertain, with clear instructions and criteria for escalation.

### 25.9 Special populations

#### Older adults

- The abdominal wall is lax; peritonitic signs are blunted. **Fever, leucocytosis and pain may be minimal even with perforation, appendicitis, cholecystitis or ischaemia.** Confusion, falls, reduced intake, or hypotension may be the first sign.
- Common serious causes: **mesenteric ischaemia, AAA, perforated diverticulitis, bowel obstruction (adhesions, hernia, malignancy, volvulus), cholecystitis and cholangitis, appendicitis (with higher perforation rates), peptic ulcer perforation (NSAIDs), cancer.**
- A **low threshold for CT** is appropriate. Mortality is much higher than in younger adults.
- Consider **medications** (anticoagulants, NSAIDs, steroids, opioids) and cardiac causes.

#### Pregnancy

- **First trimester:** ectopic pregnancy, miscarriage, hyperemesis; always exclude ectopic pregnancy in a woman of reproductive age with pain.
- **Later pregnancy:** appendicitis (the appendix moves upward, so pain may be in the right flank or RUQ; the abdominal wall is stretched, masking guarding), cholecystitis, pancreatitis, pre-eclampsia/HELLP (epigastric or RUQ pain), placental abruption, uterine rupture, round ligament pain, pyelonephritis, ovarian torsion.
- Use **ultrasound and MRI first**; CT only when necessary and justified. Involve obstetric and surgical teams.

#### Children

- **Common causes by age:** infants: colic, intussusception (6–36 months: intermittent colicky pain, drawing up legs, "redcurrant jelly" stool, a sausage-shaped mass), malrotation with volvulus (bilious vomiting is an emergency), pyloric stenosis (projectile vomiting at 2–8 weeks), hernia; preschool and school age: gastroenteritis, constipation, mesenteric adenitis, appendicitis, urinary tract infection, pneumonia, tonsillitis; adolescents: appendicitis, ovarian pathology, testicular torsion (always examine the genitalia), dysmenorrhoea, pelvic inflammatory disease, IBD, DKA.
- Observe behaviour: a child who plays, jumps or laughs is unlikely to have peritonitis; a child who refuses to move and is pale is concerning.
- Use ultrasound first for suspected appendicitis, intussusception, and pyloric stenosis.

#### Immunosuppressed patients

- Steroids, chemotherapy, transplant immunosuppression, HIV, and biologic therapy blunt symptoms and signs. Atypical infections (CMV colitis, neutropenic enterocolitis/typhlitis, fungal infections) and perforation can occur with minimal findings. **Low threshold for CT and early surgical input.**

#### Post-operative patients

- Pain beyond the expected, fever, ileus, tachycardia: consider anastomotic leak, abscess, bowel obstruction, bleeding, ischaemia, **missed injury**, pancreatitis, line sepsis, DVT/PE, wound complications.

#### Patients with cirrhosis and ascites

- **Spontaneous bacterial peritonitis (SBP)** can occur with mild pain or only confusion; diagnostic paracentesis if ascites is present and the patient has any change. Neutrophils >250/µL in ascitic fluid is diagnostic.

### 25.10 Chronic and recurrent abdominal pain

Chronic abdominal pain (lasting more than 3 months, or recurrent) is common. The approach differs: the emphasis is on **red flags**, **pattern recognition**, and **limited, purposeful tests.**

#### Red flags suggesting organic disease

- Age >50 with new symptoms
- Unintentional weight loss
- Rectal bleeding or melaena, iron-deficiency anaemia
- Persistent vomiting, dysphagia
- Nocturnal symptoms waking the patient
- Fever
- Palpable mass, lymphadenopathy, hepatomegaly
- Abnormal blood tests (raised CRP, anaemia, abnormal liver tests, raised faecal calprotectin)
- Family history of colorectal cancer, IBD, coeliac disease
- Persistent diarrhoea (>4 weeks), steatorrhoea
- Progressive course

#### Common causes

| Category | Examples |
|---|---|
| **Functional** | **Irritable bowel syndrome (IBS), functional dyspepsia, functional abdominal pain syndrome** |
| **Upper GI** | Peptic ulcer, GORD, gastritis, *H. pylori*, gastroparesis, cancer |
| **Hepatobiliary and pancreatic** | Gallstones, chronic pancreatitis, sphincter of Oddi dysfunction, biliary dyskinesia |
| **Intestinal** | IBD (Crohn's, ulcerative colitis), coeliac disease, lactose intolerance, SIBO, chronic mesenteric ischaemia, diverticular disease, colonic cancer, constipation, chronic intestinal pseudo-obstruction |
| **Gynaecological** | Endometriosis, adhesions, fibroids, pelvic congestion, ovarian cysts, chronic pelvic inflammatory disease |
| **Urological** | Recurrent stones, chronic prostatitis, interstitial cystitis |
| **Musculoskeletal and neurological** | **Abdominal wall pain** (anterior cutaneous nerve entrapment syndrome: Carnett sign positive), hernias, myofascial pain, spinal disease, abdominal migraine (children), herpes zoster neuralgia |
| **Metabolic** | Porphyria, lead poisoning, hypercalcaemia, adrenal insufficiency, familial Mediterranean fever |
| **Psychological factors** | Anxiety, depression, history of abuse, somatic symptom disorder |

#### Rome IV criteria for IBS

**Recurrent abdominal pain on average at least 1 day per week in the last 3 months, associated with two or more of:** (1) related to defecation; (2) associated with a change in stool frequency; (3) associated with a change in stool form (appearance). Criteria should be fulfilled for the last 3 months with symptom onset at least 6 months before diagnosis. Subtypes: IBS-C, IBS-D, IBS-M, IBS-U.

IBS is a **positive clinical diagnosis**, in the absence of red flags; a limited set of tests (full blood count, CRP, coeliac serology, faecal calprotectin in those with diarrhoea) is appropriate to exclude mimics such as coeliac disease and IBD. Treatment: reassurance and explanation, dietary modification (regular meals, fibre adjustment, low FODMAP under dietetic guidance), antispasmodics, laxatives or loperamide, tricyclic antidepressants, CBT or gut-directed hypnotherapy.

#### Investigation of chronic pain

Order tests according to the leading hypotheses: FBC, CRP, ferritin, coeliac serology, liver and renal function, thyroid, glucose, *H. pylori* test (stool antigen or breath test) in dyspepsia, faecal calprotectin, stool studies, ultrasound, upper endoscopy (dyspepsia with alarm features or age >50 new onset), colonoscopy (alarm features), CT/MR enterography, mesenteric imaging when ischaemia is suspected, gynaecological assessment (laparoscopy in endometriosis).

### 25.11 Worked cases

#### Case 1: Appendicitis, classic and atypical

**Classic:** *A 19-year-old man has 18 hours of periumbilical pain that has moved to the right iliac fossa, with anorexia and one vomit. Temperature 37.9 °C. Tenderness with guarding at McBurney's point; Rovsing sign positive. WBC 14, CRP 60. Alvarado score 9.*
→ Acute appendicitis. Analgesia, fluids, antibiotics, surgery.

**Atypical:** *A 76-year-old woman presents with 3 days of vague lower abdominal discomfort, mild confusion, and reduced appetite. Temperature 37.2 °C, mild RIF tenderness without guarding. WBC 11.*
→ Subtle, but older patients have higher rates of perforation. A **CT abdomen** shows a perforated appendix with an abscess. Lesson: do not rely on classical signs in older patients; image early.

#### Case 2: Gallstone disease spectrum

*A 48-year-old woman had episodes of epigastric/RUQ pain after fatty meals, lasting 1–3 hours (biliary colic). Now she has 24 hours of constant RUQ pain, fever and vomiting. Murphy sign positive, temperature 38.4 °C, WBC 16, mild rise in ALT. Ultrasound: gallstones, thickened gallbladder wall, pericholecystic fluid.*
→ **Acute cholecystitis** (Tokyo grade I–II). Fluids, analgesia, antibiotics, early laparoscopic cholecystectomy.

*Two weeks later, a similar patient presents with fever, jaundice, RUQ pain (Charcot's triad), hypotension and confusion (Reynolds pentad).*
→ **Acute cholangitis** with sepsis: emergency resuscitation, IV antibiotics, **urgent biliary decompression (ERCP)**.

#### Case 3: Sudden severe pain in an older man

*A 72-year-old smoker with hypertension has sudden severe left flank and abdominal pain. He is pale, sweaty, BP 84/50, pulse 120. A tender pulsatile abdominal mass is felt.*
→ **Ruptured AAA.** This can be mistaken for renal colic; the key is hypotension, a pulsatile mass, and risk factors. Immediate vascular surgical team, massive haemorrhage protocol, permissive hypotension, theatre. A bedside ultrasound confirms; do not delay for CT if unstable.

#### Case 4: Pain out of proportion

*A 79-year-old woman with atrial fibrillation (not anticoagulated) has 8 hours of severe, constant, diffuse abdominal pain. The abdomen is soft, with only mild tenderness. Vomiting and a loose bloody stool. HR 112 irregular, BP 100/60, lactate 4.5, WBC 22.*
→ **Acute mesenteric ischaemia** (embolic, superior mesenteric artery). **CT angiography** shows an SMA occlusion. Resuscitation, heparin, urgent vascular/surgical review for embolectomy or bowel resection. Mortality is high, so delay is the enemy.

#### Case 5: Abdominal pain with a positive pregnancy test

*A 27-year-old woman has 12 hours of lower abdominal pain with light vaginal spotting; LMP was 7 weeks ago; she is lightheaded. HR 108, BP 100/62, pain and tenderness in the left iliac fossa, cervical motion tenderness. Urine β-hCG positive.*
→ **Ectopic pregnancy** until proved otherwise (ruptured if unstable). Two large-bore IV lines, cross-match, urgent gynaecology and transvaginal ultrasound; surgery if unstable or free fluid is present.

#### Case 6: The diabetic with "gastroenteritis"

*A 22-year-old woman with type 1 diabetes has 24 hours of abdominal pain and vomiting. She is breathing deeply and rapidly; her breath smells of acetone. Blood glucose 28 mmol/L (504 mg/dL), venous pH 7.15, ketones 5.5 mmol/L.*
→ **Diabetic ketoacidosis** presenting with abdominal pain. Treat with fluids, insulin, potassium; look for the precipitant (infection, missed insulin, pregnancy, MI); do not operate on the basis of abdominal pain until DKA has been treated and the abdomen reassessed.

#### Case 7: Boy with abdominal pain

*A 13-year-old boy has 4 hours of severe lower abdominal pain, vomiting, and no urinary symptoms. The abdomen is mildly tender in the lower region. You examine the genitalia: the left testis is high-riding, swollen, very tender, and has an absent cremasteric reflex.*
→ **Testicular torsion.** Emergency surgical exploration (within 6 hours for best salvage; delay increases the risk of testicular loss). Lesson: **always examine the testes in boys and men with lower abdominal pain.**

#### Case 8: Chronic abdominal pain with alarm features

*A 58-year-old man has had 4 months of dull upper abdominal pain, early satiety and a 7 kg weight loss. Examination shows pallor and a firm epigastric mass. Haemoglobin 92 g/L, ferritin low.*
→ **Gastric cancer** until proved otherwise. Urgent upper GI endoscopy with biopsy and CT staging. These features (age >50, weight loss, anaemia, mass) exclude a functional diagnosis.

#### Case 9: IBS

*A 31-year-old woman has had 2 years of cramping lower abdominal pain several days per week, relieved by defecation, with alternating constipation and loose stools. No weight loss, bleeding, nocturnal symptoms, or family history of GI disease. Examination, FBC, CRP, coeliac serology and faecal calprotectin are normal.*
→ **IBS** (Rome IV). Explain the diagnosis positively, give dietary and lifestyle advice, treat symptoms, and arrange follow-up. Re-evaluate if alarm features develop.

### 25.12 Common pitfalls

- **Withholding analgesia** until the surgeon has seen the patient.
- **Missing a pregnancy test** in a woman of reproductive age.
- **Failing to examine the testes** and hernial orifices.
- **Missing extra-abdominal causes** (MI, pneumonia, DKA, PE).
- **Relying on normal WBC/CRP, a normal lactate, or normal abdominal X-ray** to exclude serious disease.
- **Attributing pain in an older patient to constipation or UTI** without full evaluation.
- **Treating pain out of proportion** as anxiety or drug-seeking.
- **Failing to consider AAA in an older man** with flank pain.
- **Accepting a diagnosis of gastroenteritis** while the patient has peritonism or persistent localised pain.
- **Failing to repeat the examination** or to define a plan for deterioration.
- **Not imaging an older or immunosuppressed patient** with unexplained abdominal pain.
- **Diagnosing IBS in the presence of red flags**, or failing to follow up.

---

### 25.13 Key points

- Understand visceral, parietal and referred pain; embryology explains why appendicitis pain migrates.
- Exclude the dangerous causes first: ruptured AAA, perforation, ischaemia, strangulation, ectopic pregnancy, torsion, cholangitis, DKA, MI.
- Peritonitis is a clinical diagnosis; early senior surgical input is essential.
- Give analgesia early; analgesia does not mask diagnosis.
- Choose investigations to answer a question: urine dipstick, pregnancy test, ECG, lipase, lactate, ultrasound, CT.
- Older, pregnant, immunosuppressed and very young patients have atypical presentations; maintain a low threshold for imaging and senior review.
- Chronic abdominal pain: look for red flags; IBS is a positive diagnosis by Rome IV criteria.
- Document the working diagnosis, alternatives, and a clear safety-net and follow-up plan.

### 25.14 Self-assessment

1. Why does appendicitis pain start around the umbilicus and move to the right iliac fossa?
   *Answer: Early visceral pain from the midgut-derived appendix is felt in the periumbilical region; as inflammation reaches the parietal peritoneum the pain becomes somatic and localises to the RIF.*

2. Name Charcot's triad and Reynolds' pentad.
   *Answer: Charcot's triad: fever, jaundice, RUQ pain (ascending cholangitis); Reynolds' pentad adds hypotension and confusion.*

3. In which conditions should you suspect "pain out of proportion to examination findings"?
   *Answer: Acute mesenteric ischaemia (and early strangulated obstruction, compartment syndromes, some other ischaemic states).*

4. List the Rome IV criteria for IBS.
   *Answer: Recurrent abdominal pain at least 1 day/week in the last 3 months, associated with two or more of: related to defecation; change in stool frequency; change in stool form; onset ≥6 months before diagnosis.*

5. Why should the testes be examined in a boy with lower abdominal pain?
   *Answer: Testicular torsion may present as abdominal pain; salvage depends on early surgery.*

6. Give three reasons why a normal white cell count does not exclude a surgical abdomen.
   *Answer (any three): leucocytosis may be absent early or in the elderly or immunosuppressed; sepsis may cause leucopenia; ischaemia and some perforations may not raise the count; the test is non-specific.*
