## Chapter 11. Vital Signs as Diagnostic Tools

### Learning objectives

After studying this chapter you should be able to:

1. Measure temperature, pulse, blood pressure, respiratory rate and oxygen saturation accurately, and avoid common technical errors.
2. Interpret each vital sign in context, including the effects of age, pregnancy, fitness and drugs.
3. Recognise patterns across vital signs (shock, sepsis, respiratory failure, raised intracranial pressure, tamponade).
4. Calculate and interpret the shock index and an early warning score such as NEWS2.
5. Understand why trends matter more than single values, and why "normal" vital signs do not exclude serious illness.
6. Apply age-appropriate reference ranges for children.

---

### 11.1 Why vital signs matter

Vital signs are the cheapest, fastest and most reproducible measurements in medicine. They are also the most neglected. Reviews of patients who suffered cardiac arrest or unplanned ICU admission in hospital repeatedly show that **abnormal vital signs were recorded hours before the event, and not acted upon.** The sign was measured; the meaning was not read.

Treat vital signs as **diagnostic tests** with strengths and limits:

- They detect **physiological derangement**, not specific diseases.
- They are most useful as **trends** over time.
- They must be **interpreted in context**: a heart rate of 55 is excellent in a marathon runner and an emergency in a patient who was 90 an hour ago and is now hypotensive.
- **Measure them yourself** if the patient is sick. Do not rely on a figure recorded by someone else an hour ago.

The classic "vital signs" are temperature, pulse, blood pressure, respiratory rate, and (now routinely) oxygen saturation. Level of consciousness, urine output, capillary glucose and pain score are also essential in the acutely ill, and many early warning scores include them.

### 11.2 Temperature

#### Measurement

| Site | Comments |
|---|---|
| **Tympanic (infrared)** | Quick and convenient; operator technique affects accuracy (pull the pinna to straighten the canal); can differ from core temperature |
| **Oral** | Reasonably accurate if the patient has not recently eaten, drunk, or smoked; affected by mouth breathing |
| **Axillary** | Convenient, safe in children; reads lower than core (about 0.5 °C) and is relatively insensitive |
| **Temporal artery (forehead) scanner** | Quick but variable; not reliable for screening in the unwell |
| **Rectal** | Close to core temperature; use in neonates and young children with caution, and when accuracy is vital |
| **Core (oesophageal, bladder, pulmonary artery)** | Used in critical care and hypothermia |

Use the same site and device for trends, and note which was used.

#### Definitions

- **Normal** body temperature has a range (approximately 36.1–37.2 °C), with diurnal variation of about 0.5–1 °C (lowest in early morning, highest in late afternoon). Older adults have lower baselines.
- **Fever** is a regulated rise in the hypothalamic set point: generally ≥ 38.0 °C (some definitions use 37.8 °C or ≥ 38.3 °C for specific contexts such as "fever of unknown origin" or neutropenic fever).
- **Hyperpyrexia**: > 41.5 °C.
- **Hyperthermia**: unregulated rise in temperature (set point is normal); causes include heat stroke, malignant hyperthermia, neuroleptic malignant syndrome, serotonin syndrome, thyrotoxic crisis, drugs (amphetamines, MDMA), and anticholinergic toxicity. It does not respond to antipyretics and needs cooling and treatment of the cause.
- **Hypothermia**: < 35 °C (mild 32–35, moderate 28–32, severe < 28 °C).

#### Interpretation

- **Absence of fever does not exclude infection.** Older adults, neonates, immunosuppressed patients, uraemic patients, and those on steroids or antipyretics may mount no fever. **Hypothermia in sepsis carries a worse prognosis than fever.**
- **Fever and relative bradycardia** (pulse lower than expected for the temperature; a rise of 1 °C normally raises the pulse by about 10 beats/min) is a classic clue to typhoid, Legionnaires' disease, psittacosis, brucellosis, drug fever and factitious fever.
- **Fever pattern** (continuous, intermittent, remittent, relapsing) is a weak diagnostic clue in modern practice, except in specific situations such as malaria (cyclical in some species) and Pel–Ebstein fever in some lymphomas (rare).
- **Rigors** (shaking chills with a rapid rise in temperature) suggest bacteraemia, malaria, cholangitis, pyelonephritis, or drug reactions.
- **Persistent fever** beyond 3 weeks, with no cause found after initial investigation, defines fever of unknown origin (Chapter 21).
- **Hypothermia** in the unwell: sepsis, hypothyroidism, hypoglycaemia, adrenal insufficiency, exposure, drugs, alcohol, and in the old, even a modest cold environment.

### 11.3 Pulse (heart rate)

#### Technique

Palpate the **radial pulse** with the pads of two or three fingers. Count for **30 seconds** (× 2) or a full minute if irregular. Assess rate, rhythm, and, when appropriate, character and volume (carotid or brachial pulses are better for character). Compare both radial pulses, and radial with femoral if you suspect coarctation of the aorta (radio-femoral delay). If irregular, auscultate the apex and count the **apical rate**, since some beats may not generate a palpable pulse (the pulse deficit in atrial fibrillation).

#### Rate

- **Normal adult:** 60–100 beats/min (athletes may have 40–50 normally).
- **Tachycardia (> 100):** physiological (exertion, anxiety, pain, fever, pregnancy, caffeine) or pathological (hypovolaemia, sepsis, hypoxia, anaemia, hyperthyroidism, arrhythmia, drugs such as salbutamol or atropine, PE, myocardial ischaemia, heart failure, alcohol withdrawal, hypoglycaemia).
- **Bradycardia (< 60):** athletes, sleep, drugs (beta-blockers, digoxin, verapamil, diltiazem, amiodarone), hypothyroidism, hypothermia, raised intracranial pressure (as part of Cushing's response), sick sinus syndrome, heart block, inferior MI, vagal stimulation, hyperkalaemia.

**Warning:** A normal heart rate does not exclude shock. Beta-blockers, pacemakers, autonomic dysfunction, and some young athletes can blunt tachycardia; in a young fit patient, the heart rate may rise late. In a patient with a **relative** tachycardia (higher than their baseline), ask why.

#### Rhythm

| Finding | Common causes |
|---|---|
| **Regular** | Sinus rhythm, flutter with fixed block, supraventricular tachycardia, ventricular tachycardia, complete heart block |
| **Regularly irregular** | Ectopic beats in a pattern (bigeminy), second-degree heart block with fixed ratio, sinus arrhythmia (normal variation with respiration, especially in the young) |
| **Irregularly irregular** | Atrial fibrillation (the most common), multifocal atrial tachycardia, atrial flutter with variable block, frequent ectopy |

Palpation of an irregularly irregular pulse is sensitive for atrial fibrillation but not specific; confirm with an ECG (Chapter 65).

#### Character and volume

- **Small volume, thready:** shock, severe aortic stenosis, heart failure, tamponade.
- **Collapsing (water-hammer):** aortic regurgitation, patent ductus arteriosus, hyperdynamic states (anaemia, thyrotoxicosis, pregnancy, sepsis). Test: raise the arm vertically and feel for a palpable sharp "tap" at the wrist.
- **Slow-rising (anacrotic):** aortic stenosis (best felt at the carotid).
- **Bisferiens:** mixed aortic stenosis and regurgitation, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy.
- **Pulsus alternans:** alternate strong and weak beats in a regular rhythm; severe left ventricular failure.
- **Pulsus paradoxus:** exaggerated fall in systolic pressure (> 10 mmHg) during inspiration; cardiac tamponade, severe asthma, COPD exacerbation, constrictive pericarditis, tension pneumothorax.

### 11.4 Blood pressure

#### Technique (to avoid errors)

1. The patient should be **seated, relaxed, with the arm at heart level, feet flat, back supported, not talking**, after 3–5 minutes of rest; no caffeine, nicotine, or exercise in the preceding 30 minutes; empty bladder.
2. Use the **correct cuff size.** The bladder should encircle about 80% of the arm; a cuff that is too small gives falsely high readings (and too large, falsely low). Obese or muscular arms need large cuffs.
3. Palpate the brachial artery, place the cuff with the centre of the bladder over it, and inflate. First, estimate the systolic by palpation (the pressure at which the radial pulse disappears), then re-inflate to 20–30 mmHg above that value. Deflate at about 2–3 mmHg/second; systolic is the first Korotkoff sound (phase I), diastolic the disappearance (phase V).
4. **Measure both arms at the first visit;** use the arm with the higher reading thereafter. A consistent difference > 10–15 mmHg raises the possibility of subclavian stenosis, aortic coarctation, or **aortic dissection** (if acute).
5. In older adults, diabetics, and patients on antihypertensives, **measure standing blood pressure** after 1 and 3 minutes (Chapter 10).
6. Repeat the measurement if the reading is high; use **ambulatory (24-hour) or home monitoring** to diagnose hypertension and exclude white-coat effect.
7. Automated devices are convenient but may be inaccurate in atrial fibrillation or very low pressures; confirm manually when necessary.

#### Interpretation

- **Hypertension** (office): commonly defined as ≥ 140/90 mmHg (some guidelines use 130/80), confirmed on repeated measurements and by ambulatory or home monitoring. **Hypertensive emergencies** (severe elevation with acute organ damage) are rarer than asymptomatic severe hypertension and require different management (Chapter 45).
- **Hypotension:** systolic < 90–100 mmHg or a fall of ≥ 40 mmHg from baseline. Patients with chronic hypertension may be shocked at "normal" readings.
- **Pulse pressure** (systolic − diastolic):
  - **Wide** (> 60 mmHg): aortic regurgitation, arteriosclerosis in the elderly, hyperdynamic states (sepsis, thyrotoxicosis, anaemia, pregnancy, arteriovenous fistula, patent ductus).
  - **Narrow** (< 25% of the systolic): aortic stenosis, tamponade, severe heart failure, hypovolaemia.
- **Mean arterial pressure (MAP)** = diastolic + one-third of pulse pressure; in critical care, a MAP of ≥ 65 mmHg is a common minimum target for organ perfusion.
- **Orthostatic hypotension:** a fall in systolic of ≥ 20 mmHg or diastolic ≥ 10 mmHg within 3 minutes of standing.
- **Cushing's triad** (hypertension with wide pulse pressure, bradycardia, irregular breathing) signals raised intracranial pressure and impending herniation (Chapter 41).

**Pregnancy:** blood pressure typically falls in the first and second trimesters, then rises towards term; hypertension in pregnancy needs special attention (Chapter 57).

### 11.5 Respiratory rate

**Respiratory rate (RR)** is the most sensitive and most poorly documented vital sign. It is an early marker of deterioration in sepsis, pneumonia, pulmonary embolism, metabolic acidosis, heart failure, and pain. Studies repeatedly find that charted respiratory rates are often copied or estimated, with unrealistic values such as "16" for everyone.

#### Technique

Count the breaths **for a full 60 seconds, ideally without the patient being aware** (continue to hold the wrist after taking the pulse). In infants and sick children, count for a full minute because breathing is irregular. Observe depth, pattern, effort, and use of accessory muscles.

#### Normal and abnormal

- **Adult normal:** 12–20 breaths/min at rest.
- **Tachypnoea:** ≥ 22 is part of qSOFA; ≥ 25 is high-risk in NEWS2.
- **Bradypnoea:** ≤ 8–10: opioids, sedatives, head injury, hypothermia, exhaustion (a worrying pre-arrest sign), neuromuscular failure.

#### Patterns

| Pattern | Description | Causes |
|---|---|---|
| **Kussmaul** | Deep, rapid, sighing | Metabolic acidosis (DKA, renal failure, poisoning) |
| **Cheyne–Stokes** | Waxing and waning depth with apnoeic pauses | Heart failure, stroke, high altitude, brain injury, end of life |
| **Biot's (ataxic)** | Irregular with random pauses | Brainstem lesions |
| **Obstructive** | Prolonged expiration, wheeze, pursed lips | Asthma, COPD |
| **Stridor** | Harsh inspiratory noise | Upper airway obstruction: emergency |
| **Paradoxical (see-saw) chest–abdominal movement** | Chest drawn in as abdomen moves out | Airway obstruction, diaphragmatic fatigue, spinal cord injury, flail chest |
| **Tachypnoea with clear chest and normal saturation** | | Metabolic acidosis, pulmonary embolism, anxiety, pain, sepsis, salicylate poisoning |

A rising respiratory rate with falling oxygen saturation is serious. A **falling** respiratory rate in an exhausted patient with severe respiratory distress is **pre-terminal**.

### 11.6 Oxygen saturation

**Pulse oximetry** estimates the percentage of haemoglobin saturated with oxygen (SpO₂). It is non-invasive and continuous but has recognised limitations.

- **Target saturation:** 94–98% for most patients; **88–92%** for those at risk of hypercapnic respiratory failure (severe COPD, obesity hypoventilation, chest wall disease) unless otherwise specified.
- **Pitfalls:**
  - Poor peripheral perfusion (cold, shock, Raynaud's), movement, nail varnish or false nails, bright light, and dark skin pigmentation can cause inaccurate readings; pulse oximeters may overestimate saturation in people with darker skin, which can lead to missed hypoxaemia.
  - **Carbon monoxide** binds to haemoglobin and the oximeter reads it as oxyhaemoglobin (falsely normal).
  - **Methaemoglobinaemia** gives readings that tend towards ~85%.
  - Severe anaemia: saturation may be normal despite reduced oxygen content.
  - Oximetry does not detect **hypercapnia**, and does not measure ventilation; a normal saturation on oxygen does not mean the patient is breathing adequately.
- **Always record whether the patient is on air or oxygen**, and the flow rate or delivery device.
- A **normal saturation does not exclude respiratory disease** (a patient in early pneumonia may compensate with tachypnoea), and **a low saturation requires evaluation**, not just oxygen.
- Use **arterial or venous blood gases** for pH, PaCO₂, and bicarbonate, and when the oximeter reading seems unreliable.

### 11.7 Consciousness, urine output, glucose and pain

- **Level of consciousness.** Use AVPU (Alert, Voice, Pain, Unresponsive), or ACVPU with the addition of "new Confusion". New confusion is a sensitive indicator of hypoxia, hypoperfusion, sepsis, hypoglycaemia, or drug effects. The Glasgow Coma Scale is described in Chapter 15.
- **Urine output.** Normal is at least 0.5 mL/kg/hour. Oliguria (< 0.5 mL/kg/h for 6 hours) is an early signal of hypovolaemia or renal impairment and is one of the KDIGO criteria for acute kidney injury.
- **Capillary blood glucose.** Check in any unwell, drowsy, confused, or seizing patient: hypoglycaemia is quickly reversible and devastating if missed.
- **Pain score.** Often called the "fifth vital sign"; use a scale appropriate to the patient (numerical, faces, behavioural for cognitively impaired patients). See Chapter 36.
- **Capillary refill time.** Press on the sternum or fingertip for 5 seconds and count how long it takes for colour to return; > 2–3 seconds suggests poor peripheral perfusion (not reliable alone, particularly in the cold or in older patients).

### 11.8 Interpreting vital signs together

#### The shock index

**Shock index (SI) = heart rate ÷ systolic blood pressure.**

- Normal: about 0.5–0.7.
- **> 0.9–1.0** suggests hypovolaemia or shock and a higher risk of deterioration, bleeding or the need for transfusion; it is particularly helpful in trauma, obstetric haemorrhage (an SI > 0.9 is often used as a trigger), gastrointestinal bleeding, ruptured ectopic pregnancy, and sepsis.
- Caveats: beta-blockade, chronic hypertension, and elderly physiology can make SI less reliable; always interpret clinically.

*Example:* HR 120, SBP 90 → SI 1.33 → significant haemodynamic compromise, even though "BP is still 90".

#### Haemodynamic patterns

| Pattern | Vital signs | Typical causes |
|---|---|---|
| **Hypovolaemic shock** | Tachycardia, low BP, narrow pulse pressure, tachypnoea, cool peripheries, delayed capillary refill, low urine output | Haemorrhage, dehydration, burns |
| **Septic (distributive) shock** | Fever or hypothermia, tachycardia, low BP with wide pulse pressure early ("warm shock"), tachypnoea, altered mentation | Sepsis, anaphylaxis, neurogenic shock |
| **Cardiogenic shock** | Tachycardia (or bradycardia), low BP, narrow pulse pressure, raised JVP, pulmonary oedema, cold and clammy | MI, severe arrhythmia, decompensated heart failure, valve rupture |
| **Obstructive shock** | Hypotension, tachycardia, raised JVP, absent breath sounds (tension pneumothorax), muffled heart sounds (tamponade), or sudden hypoxia (massive PE) | Tamponade, tension pneumothorax, massive PE |
| **Raised intracranial pressure** | Hypertension with widening pulse pressure, bradycardia, irregular respiration, reduced consciousness | Intracranial haemorrhage, mass, hydrocephalus, brain oedema |

#### Early warning scores

Because deterioration is often gradual and spread across several parameters, no single abnormal value triggers alarm. **Aggregate early warning scores** (NEWS2, MEWS, PEWS in children, MEOWS in pregnancy) convert vital signs into a number with defined response thresholds. NEWS2 is widely used in UK hospitals and many others.

**NEWS2 parameters (adult, abbreviated):**

| Parameter | 3 | 2 | 1 | 0 | 1 | 2 | 3 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Respiratory rate | ≤ 8 | | 9–11 | 12–20 | | 21–24 | ≥ 25 |
| SpO₂ (scale 1) | ≤ 91 | 92–93 | 94–95 | ≥ 96 | | | |
| Air or oxygen | | Oxygen | | Air | | | |
| Systolic BP | ≤ 90 | 91–100 | 101–110 | 111–219 | | | ≥ 220 |
| Pulse | ≤ 40 | | 41–50 | 51–90 | 91–110 | 111–130 | ≥ 131 |
| Consciousness | | | | Alert | | | Confusion/Voice/Pain/Unresponsive |
| Temperature (°C) | ≤ 35.0 | | 35.1–36.0 | 36.1–38.0 | 38.1–39.0 | ≥ 39.1 | |

(A separate scale 2 for SpO₂ applies to patients with target 88–92%. Use the version approved in your institution.)

**Typical response bands:**

- **0–4:** low risk; routine monitoring (4–6 hourly or per policy); any single parameter scoring 3 triggers urgent ward-based review.
- **5–6:** medium risk; urgent clinical review and increased monitoring.
- **≥ 7:** high risk; emergency assessment by a critical care-competent team, consider transfer to higher level of care.

Scores are **aids**, not replacements for judgement; they are less reliable in children (use paediatric scores), pregnancy, and some chronic conditions. A worried nurse or relative can override a "low" score.

#### qSOFA and sepsis screening

The quick SOFA (qSOFA) identifies patients with suspected infection at higher risk of poor outcome: respiratory rate ≥ 22, altered mentation, systolic BP ≤ 100 mmHg. Two or more criteria prompt assessment for organ dysfunction. It is a prognostic tool, not a diagnostic test, and is less sensitive than early warning scores for detecting sepsis (Chapter 39).

### 11.9 Age, pregnancy and special populations

#### Children

Normal values change with age, and thresholds are lower for concern than in adults because children compensate well until they suddenly decompensate. Approximate reference ranges (verify against local charts):

| Age | Heart rate (beats/min) | Respiratory rate (breaths/min) | Systolic BP (mmHg), lower limit |
|---|---|---|---|
| < 1 year | 110–160 | 30–40 | > 70 |
| 1–2 years | 100–150 | 25–35 | > 72 |
| 2–5 years | 95–140 | 25–30 | > 74 |
| 5–12 years | 80–120 | 20–25 | > 78 |
| > 12 years | 60–100 | 15–20 | > 85 |

Key points: **tachycardia and tachypnoea** are the most sensitive early signs of shock or respiratory compromise in children; **hypotension is a late sign.** Fever is a poor discriminator of serious illness in infants under 3 months (use dedicated guidelines).

#### Pregnancy

Heart rate rises by 10–20 beats/min, blood pressure falls in the second trimester, and the respiratory rate may rise slightly. Lying supine in late pregnancy can cause hypotension from aortocaval compression: use left lateral tilt. Use obstetric early warning scores (MEOWS) and take rising blood pressure seriously (pre-eclampsia).

#### Older adults

Fever may be blunted or absent; baseline blood pressure and heart rate may be altered by drugs; postural hypotension is common; the respiratory rate rises in infections even without fever. A modest rise in heart rate or respiratory rate, or new confusion, may be the only sign of sepsis.

#### Athletes and drug effects

Resting bradycardia and low blood pressure are normal in athletes. Beta-blockers and rate-limiting calcium-channel blockers blunt tachycardia; steroids and antipyretics mask fever; sedatives and opioids slow respiration.

### 11.10 Trends, documentation and acting on abnormal values

1. **Chart vital signs on a graphical observation chart,** so trends are visible. A pulse rising from 80 to 105 to 120 over 6 hours, with BP falling from 130 to 105, is more concerning than any single value.
2. **Compare with the patient's baseline,** which may be stored in prior records or known by relatives.
3. **Re-measure abnormal values** with correct technique, but do not use "repeat until normal" as a strategy. If the patient looks unwell, act.
4. **Act on an abnormal sign:** consider causes (pain, fever, anxiety, hypovolaemia, bleeding, sepsis, drugs), examine, and escalate according to local protocol.
5. **Record the time, the device, and what the patient was doing.**
6. **Communicate** abnormal results in a structured form (SBAR, Chapter 9).
7. **Trust your clinical instinct.** If the patient looks unwell despite "normal" values, ask for a senior opinion.

### 11.11 Worked cases

#### Case 1: "Normal" vital signs in a bleeding patient

*A 68-year-old man on atenolol for hypertension is admitted with black stools for two days and dizziness on standing. Pulse 82, BP 112/68, RR 16, SpO₂ 97%, temperature 36.4 °C. He looks pale and his hands are cool.*

**Reasoning.** The pulse of 82 is reassuring only if he were not on a beta-blocker. His usual BP is 150/85, so a reading of 112/68 represents a substantial fall. Shock index = 82/112 = 0.73 (borderline), but with the drug effect and baseline considered, he is probably significantly hypovolaemic. Orthostatic measurements: lying 112/68; standing 88/54 with presyncope. His haemoglobin (checked) is 72 g/L.

**Plan.** Large-bore IV access, bloods including group and save/cross-match, fluid resuscitation, blood transfusion as indicated, hold atenolol and antihypertensives, urgent endoscopy after resuscitation, consider proton pump inhibitor.

**Lesson.** Vital signs must be interpreted against the patient's baseline and drugs. "Normal" does not mean safe.

#### Case 2: Shock index in obstetric bleeding

*A 29-year-old woman, 20 minutes after delivery, has ongoing vaginal bleeding. Estimated blood loss 900 mL. Pulse 112, BP 108/64, RR 22.*

**Reasoning.** SI = 112/108 = 1.04, indicating significant haemodynamic compromise even though the BP is "normal". Pregnancy-related physiology (increased blood volume) can mask early hypovolaemia until a large amount of blood has been lost.

**Plan.** Activate the obstetric haemorrhage protocol: uterine massage, uterotonics, IV access x2, tranexamic acid, fluids and blood, check for the cause (tone, trauma, tissue, thrombin), and call the senior obstetrician and anaesthetist.

#### Case 3: NEWS2 and sepsis

*A 78-year-old woman, post-operative day 2 after hip replacement, has: RR 24, SpO₂ 93% on air, SBP 98, pulse 112, new confusion, temperature 38.6 °C.*

**NEWS2 score:** RR 24 = 2; SpO₂ 93% = 2; air = 0; SBP 98 = 2; pulse 112 = 2; new confusion = 3; temperature 38.6 = 1. **Total = 12 → high risk.**

**Reasoning.** Emergency review is required. She meets the criteria for suspected sepsis with organ dysfunction (hypotension, confusion, hypoxaemia). Possible sources: chest, urine, wound, joint, line sites, cellulitis, and Clostridioides difficile. Pulmonary embolism and haemorrhage are alternative or concurrent causes.

**Plan.** Sepsis pathway within the hour: lactate, blood cultures, urine, wound and sputum samples, broad-spectrum antibiotics according to local policy, IV fluids, oxygen, senior review, and consideration of critical care. Source control as needed.

#### Case 4: Hypothermia and the elderly

*An 85-year-old woman is found at home on a cold morning, drowsy, with a temperature of 33.8 °C, pulse 48, and BP 96/54. There is no history of exposure to extreme cold; the heating was on, but she lives alone. Skin is dry, and she has puffy hands and slow reflexes.*

**Reasoning.** The causes of hypothermia in the elderly include exposure, infection (sepsis can cause hypothermia), hypothyroidism (myxoedema), hypoglycaemia, adrenal insufficiency, drugs and alcohol, and hypoglycaemia. The combination of bradycardia, dry skin, puffy features and delayed reflexes raises concern about **myxoedema coma** (a medical emergency), although sepsis must be excluded in parallel.

**Plan.** Gentle rewarming (passive external, with a warmed environment; avoid rapid rewarming), cardiac monitoring (arrhythmia risk), glucose, blood gas, TSH/free T4, cortisol, cultures, and treatment of the cause. Do not forget to check for **infection** even though she is afebrile: sepsis presents with hypothermia in the old.

#### Case 5: Blood pressure in both arms

*A 56-year-old man presents with sudden severe tearing chest pain radiating to the back. He is sweating and anxious. BP is 168/96 in the right arm and 124/70 in the left. Pulse 98.*

**Reasoning.** The marked inter-arm difference with tearing pain suggests **acute aortic dissection** affecting the origin of the left subclavian artery. The pretest probability is raised by hypertension and abrupt, maximal-at-onset pain. Neither a normal ECG nor a normal chest X-ray excludes dissection.

**Plan.** Urgent CT aortography (or transoesophageal echocardiography if unstable), analgesia, control of heart rate and blood pressure (IV beta-blocker first, with a target systolic of 100–120 mmHg), avoid anticoagulants and thrombolytics until MI is excluded, urgent cardiothoracic or vascular surgical consultation.

**Lesson.** Measuring BP in both arms takes one minute and may rescue the diagnosis.

#### Case 6: Tachypnoea in a child

*A 14-month-old boy has had a cough and fever for 3 days. He is feeding poorly. RR 56 (counted over a full minute while calm), HR 168, temperature 38.8 °C, SpO₂ 92%. He has subcostal recession and nasal flaring.*

**Reasoning.** For a 1-year-old, a respiratory rate of 56 is well above the normal range (25–35). Together with recession, nasal flaring, hypoxia and poor feeding, he has significant lower respiratory tract disease (bronchiolitis or pneumonia). Hypoxia and tachypnoea predict the need for admission.

**Plan.** Oxygen to keep saturations in the target range, assess hydration and feeding, consider nasogastric feeding, supportive care, chest X-ray only if indicated, and close monitoring with a paediatric early warning score. Parents need clear safety-netting if managed at home.

### 11.12 Common pitfalls

- **Recording rather than reading.** Charting vital signs without responding.
- **Estimated or copied respiratory rates.**
- **Single measurements instead of trends.**
- **Wrong cuff size or technique,** leading to misdiagnosis of hypertension.
- **Using only one arm** on the first assessment.
- **Treating the number rather than the patient** (and the reverse).
- **Believing a normal pulse excludes shock** in patients on beta-blockers or in young, fit patients.
- **Ignoring the baseline blood pressure** in chronic hypertension.
- **Assuming no fever means no infection,** particularly in the elderly and immunosuppressed.
- **Trusting pulse oximetry** in poor perfusion, carbon monoxide poisoning, or in darker skin without confirming.
- **Not using early warning scores,** or using them without escalating.

---

### 11.13 Key points

- Vital signs are diagnostic tests: measure accurately, interpret in context, and watch the trend.
- Count the respiratory rate for a full minute; it is the most sensitive and most neglected sign.
- Check blood pressure in both arms at first assessment; use the correct cuff size and technique.
- Fever is not always present in infection; hypothermia in sepsis signals a worse prognosis.
- The shock index (HR ÷ SBP) and early warning scores help identify deterioration early.
- Know the effect of drugs, age, pregnancy and fitness on vital signs.
- Pulse oximetry has important limitations (perfusion, carbon monoxide, skin pigmentation, no information on CO₂).
- Normal vital signs do not exclude serious illness; if the patient looks unwell, escalate.

### 11.14 Self-assessment

1. How do you calculate the shock index, and what value is concerning?
   *Answer: HR ÷ SBP; normal ~0.5–0.7; > 0.9–1.0 suggests significant compromise.*

2. Name three conditions that cause pulsus paradoxus.
   *Answer: Cardiac tamponade, severe asthma, COPD exacerbation (also constrictive pericarditis, tension pneumothorax).*

3. A patient with severe COPD has a target saturation of 88–92%. Why?
   *Answer: Excess oxygen can worsen hypercapnia in patients prone to CO₂ retention; the aim is to avoid both hypoxia and hyperoxia.*

4. Compute the NEWS2 score: RR 22, SpO₂ 95% on air, SBP 105, pulse 95, alert, temperature 37.2 °C.
   *Answer: RR 22 = 2; SpO₂ 95 = 1; air = 0; SBP 105 = 1; pulse 95 = 1; alert = 0; temp = 0; total = 5 (medium risk, urgent review).*

5. Which conditions can cause fever with relative bradycardia?
   *Answer: Typhoid fever, Legionnaires' disease, psittacosis, brucellosis, drug fever, factitious fever.*

6. A patient has BP 168/96 in the right arm and 124/70 in the left, with tearing chest pain. What is the leading concern?
   *Answer: Acute aortic dissection.*
