## Chapter 12. Cardiovascular Examination

### Learning objectives

After studying this chapter you should be able to:

1. Perform a complete cardiovascular examination in a logical sequence: general inspection, hands, pulse, blood pressure, face, neck (JVP and carotids), precordium, and peripheral signs.
2. Describe the rate, rhythm, character and volume of the pulse, and recognise abnormal pulse patterns.
3. Measure and interpret the jugular venous pressure and its waveform.
4. Locate and characterise the apex beat, heaves and thrills.
5. Auscultate the heart systematically, identify normal and abnormal heart sounds, and describe murmurs by timing, location, radiation, intensity and quality.
6. Recognise the clinical signs of the major valve lesions, heart failure, pericardial disease, and infective endocarditis.
7. Integrate examination findings into a diagnosis and decide on the next investigation.

---

### 12.1 Approach and preparation

The cardiovascular examination is a sequence of small observations that, taken together, answer four questions:

1. **Is the heart rate and rhythm normal?** (pulse)
2. **Is the circulation adequate, and what is the filling pressure?** (blood pressure, perfusion, JVP, oedema)
3. **Is there structural heart disease?** (murmurs, heart sounds, apex beat, heaves)
4. **Is there evidence of systemic consequences or causes?** (hands, eyes, peripheral signs)

#### Preparation

- Introduce yourself, explain what you are doing, obtain consent, and arrange a **chaperone** if you are examining a woman's chest (Chapter 19) or whenever appropriate.
- **Expose the chest** while maintaining dignity (a gown or sheet to cover the breasts).
- Position the patient **reclining at 45°**, head supported on a pillow, turned slightly away from the side you are examining.
- Good lighting; a quiet room. Warm your stethoscope.
- Stand on the **patient's right side.**

---

### 12.2 General inspection

Begin at the end of the bed (Chapter 10).

- Is the patient **breathless at rest**? Sitting forward? **Orthopnoeic** (needing pillows)?
- **Colour:** pallor, cyanosis, malar flush (the "mitral facies": pinkish-purple cheeks in mitral stenosis, caused by low cardiac output and vasoconstriction), jaundice.
- **Habitus:** cachexia (cardiac cachexia in advanced heart failure), obesity, tall with long limbs and arachnodactyly (Marfan), short stature and webbed neck (Turner or Noonan syndrome), Down syndrome (AV septal defects).
- **Equipment:** oxygen, GTN spray, pacemaker or defibrillator box, telemetry, infusion pumps.
- **Scars:** midline sternotomy (CABG, valve surgery), left thoracotomy (mitral valvotomy, coarctation repair), pacemaker pocket below the clavicle, saphenous vein harvest scar on the leg, radial artery harvest scar on the forearm, groin scars (catheterisation).
- **Chest wall deformities:** pectus excavatum (compresses the heart, may produce a murmur), pectus carinatum, kyphoscoliosis.
- **Visible pulsation:** the apex beat, a pulsating aorta, visible carotid pulsations ("Corrigan sign" in severe aortic regurgitation).

### 12.3 The hands

| Finding | Significance |
|---|---|
| Cold, clammy, blue hands | Poor perfusion, shock |
| Warm, bounding | Vasodilation (sepsis, thyrotoxicosis, anaemia, aortic regurgitation) |
| Clubbing | Cyanotic congenital heart disease, infective endocarditis |
| Splinter haemorrhages | Infective endocarditis (also trauma, vasculitis) |
| Osler nodes (painful, pulp of finger or toe), Janeway lesions (painless, palms and soles) | Infective endocarditis |
| Nicotine staining | Smoking, coronary risk |
| Xanthomata over tendons | Familial hypercholesterolaemia |
| Arachnodactyly | Marfan syndrome |
| Tremor, sweaty palms | Thyrotoxicosis (consider atrial fibrillation) |
| Pallor of palmar creases | Anaemia (high-output state or angina provoked by anaemia) |
| Capillary refill time (press for 5 seconds on the nail bed or sternum; normal refill <2 s) | Perfusion |

### 12.4 The pulse

#### Rate and rhythm

Palpate the **radial pulse** with the pads of two or three fingers. Count for **30 seconds and double** (or 60 seconds if irregular).

- **Normal adult resting rate:** 60–100 beats/min (athletes may be lower).
- **Tachycardia:** >100/min; **bradycardia:** <60/min.
- **Rhythm:** regular, regularly irregular (e.g. ventricular ectopics every third beat; second-degree heart block with a fixed ratio), or **irregularly irregular** (atrial fibrillation, or frequent random ectopics).

**Pulse deficit:** In AF, some ventricular beats are too weak to produce a palpable radial pulse. Compare apical rate (auscultation) with radial rate; the difference is the deficit.

#### Pulse character and volume

Judged best at the **carotid** (with caution, one side at a time, never massaging) or brachial artery, since the radial pulse is a distorted version of the central waveform.

| Character | Description | Causes |
|---|---|---|
| **Normal** | Smooth upstroke, gradual descent | – |
| **Slow-rising (anacrotic)** | Slow upstroke, low volume, often with a palpable shudder | Aortic stenosis |
| **Collapsing (water-hammer)** | Abrupt upstroke, rapid fall; felt best when the arm is raised | Aortic regurgitation; also patent ductus arteriosus, thyrotoxicosis, anaemia, fever, pregnancy |
| **Bisferiens** | Two systolic peaks | Mixed aortic valve disease; hypertrophic cardiomyopathy |
| **Pulsus alternans** | Alternating strong and weak beats | Severe left ventricular failure |
| **Pulsus paradoxus** | Exaggerated fall (>10 mmHg) in systolic pressure on inspiration | Cardiac tamponade, severe asthma, COPD exacerbation, constrictive pericarditis, tension pneumothorax |
| **Small volume, thready** | Weak, fast | Shock, hypovolaemia, severe heart failure, mitral stenosis |
| **Large volume, bounding** | Strong | Hyperdynamic circulation, CO₂ retention (bounding pulse with warm hands and flap), aortic regurgitation |

#### Radio-radial and radio-femoral delay

- **Radio-radial delay** (the two radial pulses differ in timing or volume): subclavian artery stenosis, aortic dissection, aortic arch disease, supravalvular aortic stenosis.
- **Radio-femoral delay:** coarctation of the aorta. Palpate the radial and femoral pulses simultaneously. Normally they occur at the same moment. A delay of the femoral pulse suggests coarctation (check for hypertension in the arms and low pressure in the legs).

#### Other pulses

Palpate brachial, carotid, femoral, popliteal, posterior tibial and dorsalis pedis pulses (see 12.12).

---

### 12.5 Blood pressure

(See Chapter 11 for technique.) For the cardiovascular examination specifically:

- **Measure in both arms** at least once. A difference >10–15 mmHg suggests subclavian or aortic disease (dissection, atherosclerosis, coarctation).
- **Measure postural BP** if there is dizziness or syncope.
- **Pulse pressure** (systolic minus diastolic): normally ~40 mmHg. **Wide** (>60 mmHg): aortic regurgitation, thyrotoxicosis, anaemia, sepsis (vasodilation), arteriosclerosis in the elderly. **Narrow** (<25 mmHg or <25% of systolic): aortic stenosis, cardiac tamponade, severe left ventricular failure, hypovolaemia.
- **Pulsus paradoxus:** inflate the cuff above the systolic pressure, deflate slowly, note the pressure at which the first Korotkoff sounds are heard only in expiration, and then the pressure at which they are heard throughout the respiratory cycle. A difference greater than 10 mmHg is abnormal.

---

### 12.6 The face and eyes

Look for:

- **Anaemia** (conjunctival pallor).
- **Jaundice** (haemolysis from a prosthetic valve, or hepatic congestion).
- **Xanthelasmata** and **corneal arcus** (below 45 years, suggests hyperlipidaemia).
- **Malar flush** (mitral stenosis).
- **Conjunctival petechiae** (infective endocarditis).
- **Retinal changes** on fundoscopy (hypertensive retinopathy, Roth spots in endocarditis).
- **Central cyanosis** of the tongue and lips.
- **Dental hygiene** (poor dentition is a source of endocarditis; high-arched palate in Marfan syndrome).
- **Features of Turner syndrome, Noonan syndrome, or Down syndrome.**

### 12.7 The jugular venous pressure (JVP)

The JVP is the single most useful bedside indicator of **right atrial pressure and central venous volume.**

#### Technique

1. Position the patient at 45° (adjust to see the highest pulsation), with the head turned slightly to the left and the neck muscles relaxed. Good lighting with tangential illumination helps.
2. Find the **internal jugular vein** (IJV), which lies deep to the sternocleidomastoid and is seen as a diffuse, double-waveform, non-palpable pulsation. The external jugular vein is superficial and less reliable.
3. **Distinguish from the carotid:** the JVP has a double (biphasic) waveform; it is not palpable; it is obliterated by light pressure at the base of the neck; it changes with respiration and with posture; and it rises with abdominal pressure (hepatojugular reflux).
4. **Measure** the vertical height of the top of the pulsation above the **sternal angle** (the angle of Louis). Add 5 cm (the approximate distance from the sternal angle to the centre of the right atrium) to obtain the estimated central venous pressure. A JVP **>3 cm above the sternal angle** (i.e. >8 cm H₂O) is raised.

#### Waveform

| Wave | Event |
|---|---|
| **a wave** | Atrial contraction (just before the carotid pulse and the first heart sound) |
| **c wave** | Tricuspid valve bulging into the right atrium during early ventricular systole (rarely visible) |
| **x descent** | Atrial relaxation |
| **v wave** | Passive atrial filling during ventricular systole (tricuspid valve closed) |
| **y descent** | Tricuspid valve opens and the atrium empties into the ventricle |

#### Abnormal JVP patterns

| Finding | Cause |
|---|---|
| **Raised JVP** | Right heart failure (including secondary to left heart disease), fluid overload, pulmonary hypertension, pericardial tamponade or constriction, tricuspid stenosis or regurgitation, superior vena cava obstruction (raised but non-pulsatile, with facial plethora and distended chest veins) |
| **Low JVP** | Hypovolaemia (haemorrhage, dehydration, sepsis) |
| **Giant "a" waves** | Pulmonary hypertension, pulmonary stenosis, tricuspid stenosis (increased resistance to atrial emptying) |
| **Cannon "a" waves** | Atrial contraction against a closed tricuspid valve (complete heart block, ventricular tachycardia, ventricular ectopics, junctional rhythm) |
| **Absent "a" waves** | Atrial fibrillation |
| **Large "v" waves** | Tricuspid regurgitation (the JVP may oscillate with each beat, lifting the ear lobe) |
| **Steep "y" descent** | Constrictive pericarditis (Friedreich sign), severe tricuspid regurgitation |
| **Slow "y" descent** | Tricuspid stenosis, tamponade |
| **Kussmaul sign** | Paradoxical *rise* in JVP on inspiration (constrictive pericarditis, restrictive cardiomyopathy, right ventricular infarction, severe right heart failure) |

#### Hepatojugular (abdominojugular) reflux

Press firmly over the right upper quadrant of the abdomen for 10 seconds while the patient breathes normally. A sustained rise in the JVP of more than 3 cm (that does not fall during the compression) suggests elevated left or right heart filling pressures and is a sign of heart failure. It is useful when the JVP is borderline.

---

### 12.8 The carotid pulse

Palpate one carotid at a time, medially to the sternocleidomastoid, at the level of the thyroid cartilage, gently (avoid carotid sinus massage and avoid compressing both sides). Auscultate for **bruits** first if carotid disease is suspected.

Assess character (see 12.4) and listen for a **carotid bruit** (stenosis) or radiation of an aortic murmur.

---

### 12.9 The precordium: inspection and palpation

#### Inspection

Scars, chest deformities, visible pulsations, skin lesions, pacemaker.

#### Palpation of the apex beat

The **apex beat** is the lowest and most lateral point at which the cardiac impulse can be felt. In health, it lies in the **fifth intercostal space, in the mid-clavicular line** (about 9 cm from the midline in an adult), and is felt as a gentle tap, about 2–3 cm in diameter. It may be impalpable in obesity, emphysema, pericardial effusion, dextrocardia, and chest-wall abnormalities.

- Place your hand flat to find it, then localise it with the fingertips.
- Count the space by finding the sternal angle (2nd rib) and then counting down.

| Apex finding | Cause |
|---|---|
| **Displaced** laterally and/or inferiorly | Left ventricular dilatation (cardiomyopathy, heart failure, mitral regurgitation, aortic regurgitation); but also mediastinal shift (pneumothorax, fibrosis, scoliosis) |
| **Heaving** (sustained, forceful but not displaced) | Left ventricular hypertrophy from pressure overload (aortic stenosis, hypertension) |
| **Thrusting** (hyperdynamic, displaced) | Volume overload (aortic and mitral regurgitation) |
| **Tapping** (palpable first heart sound) | Mitral stenosis |
| **Double impulse** (palpable atrial contraction before systole) | Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy; LV hypertrophy |
| **Dyskinetic (diffuse, out-of-phase)** | Left ventricular aneurysm, large infarction |

#### Heaves and thrills

- **Parasternal (right ventricular) heave:** place the heel of the hand over the lower left sternal edge. A sustained lift indicates right ventricular hypertrophy (pulmonary hypertension, pulmonary stenosis, mitral stenosis, ASD).
- **Thrill:** a palpable murmur (vibration), felt as a "purring" under the palm. It indicates a **grade 4 murmur or louder.** Palpate each valve area (aortic, pulmonary, tricuspid, mitral). Also feel for a palpable P₂ (pulmonary hypertension).

### 12.10 Auscultation

#### Equipment and technique

- Use the **diaphragm** (high-pitched sounds: S1, S2, systolic murmurs of AS, MR, aortic regurgitation, friction rubs) and the **bell** (low-pitched sounds: S3, S4, mitral stenosis murmur). Apply the bell lightly.
- Listen in each area for at least several cardiac cycles, while palpating the carotid pulse to time systole and diastole.
- Listen in a **quiet room**, with the patient holding breath in expiration for some manoeuvres.

#### The auscultatory areas (approximate)

| Area | Location | Lesion best heard |
|---|---|---|
| **Aortic** | 2nd right intercostal space, right sternal edge | Aortic stenosis |
| **Pulmonary** | 2nd left intercostal space, left sternal edge | Pulmonary stenosis, flow murmurs |
| **Erb's point** | 3rd left intercostal space | Aortic regurgitation, VSD |
| **Tricuspid** | 4th–5th left intercostal space, left sternal edge | Tricuspid regurgitation, VSD |
| **Mitral (apex)** | 5th intercostal space, mid-clavicular line | Mitral regurgitation, stenosis |

However, sounds are produced by blood flow and can be heard far from the valve of origin. **Listen over the entire precordium,** the axilla, the carotids and the back.

#### Heart sounds

| Sound | Origin | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **S1** | Closure of the mitral and tricuspid valves, onset of systole | Loud: mitral stenosis, short PR interval, tachycardia, thin patient. Soft: mitral regurgitation, long PR interval, heart failure, obesity. Variable intensity: AF, complete heart block |
| **S2** | Closure of the aortic (A₂) and pulmonary (P₂) valves | Splits on inspiration (A₂ precedes P₂); **physiological split** (normal). Loud A₂: hypertension; soft or absent: severe aortic stenosis. Loud P₂: pulmonary hypertension |
| **Wide split S2** | Delayed P₂ | Right bundle branch block, pulmonary stenosis, mitral regurgitation |
| **Fixed split S2** | No variation with respiration | Atrial septal defect |
| **Reversed (paradoxical) split** | Delayed A₂ | Left bundle branch block, severe aortic stenosis, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy |
| **S3** (ventricular gallop, "Kentucky") | Rapid ventricular filling in early diastole | Normal in children and young adults; in older adults indicates **heart failure**, volume overload, mitral regurgitation |
| **S4** (atrial gallop, "Tennessee") | Atrial contraction into a stiff ventricle, late diastole | Left ventricular hypertrophy, hypertension, aortic stenosis, ischaemia, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (absent in AF) |
| **Opening snap** | Sudden opening of a stenotic mitral valve in early diastole | Mitral stenosis (an early OS indicates severe disease) |
| **Ejection click** | Opening of a stenotic or bicuspid aortic or pulmonary valve | Bicuspid aortic valve, pulmonary stenosis |
| **Mid-systolic click** | Prolapse of the mitral valve | Mitral valve prolapse (may be followed by late systolic murmur) |
| **Pericardial rub** | Friction between pericardial layers; scratchy, triphasic, heard best leaning forward in expiration | Pericarditis |
| **Prosthetic valve sounds** | Metallic closing click | Mechanical valve. A muffled click suggests thrombosis or malfunction |

### 12.11 Murmurs

A murmur is produced by turbulent blood flow. Describe it by:

1. **Timing** in the cardiac cycle (systolic or diastolic; early, mid, late, or pan/holo).
2. **Location** where it is loudest.
3. **Radiation** (axilla, carotids, back).
4. **Intensity:** graded on the **Levine scale** (grade 1: barely audible; 2: soft; 3: moderately loud; 4: loud with a thrill; 5: very loud, audible with the stethoscope partly off the chest; 6: audible without the stethoscope). Grade 4 or greater is accompanied by a palpable thrill.
5. **Quality and shape** (crescendo, decrescendo, crescendo-decrescendo (diamond-shaped), plateau).
6. **Pitch.**
7. **Variation** with respiration, posture and manoeuvres.

Intensity does not reliably reflect severity (for example, a very severe aortic stenosis with low cardiac output may be quiet).

#### Systolic murmurs

| Murmur | Characteristics | Associated signs |
|---|---|---|
| **Aortic stenosis** | Ejection systolic (crescendo-decrescendo), loudest at the right 2nd intercostal space, radiates to the carotids; louder in expiration | Slow-rising pulse, narrow pulse pressure, soft or absent A₂, heaving apex, S4, thrill; **angina, syncope, heart failure** (late). Severe if the murmur peaks late |
| **Mitral regurgitation** | Pansystolic (holosystolic), blowing, loudest at the apex, radiates to the axilla | Displaced thrusting apex, soft S1, S3; AF; signs of heart failure |
| **Tricuspid regurgitation** | Pansystolic at the left lower sternal edge; louder on inspiration (Carvallo sign) | Raised JVP with large v waves, pulsatile liver, ascites, peripheral oedema |
| **Ventricular septal defect** | Harsh pansystolic at the left lower sternal edge, with a thrill | Small defects have loud murmurs (maladie de Roger); large ones cause heart failure and pulmonary hypertension |
| **Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy** | Ejection systolic at the left sternal edge; **louder with Valsalva or standing** (decreased venous return), quieter on squatting | Jerky pulse, double apical impulse, S4; risk of sudden death in young patients |
| **Mitral valve prolapse** | Mid-systolic click followed by late systolic murmur; earlier with standing | Often benign |
| **Pulmonary stenosis** | Ejection systolic at the left 2nd intercostal space, ejection click, wide split S2 | Right ventricular heave |
| **Flow murmur (innocent)** | Soft (grade 1–2), mid-systolic, short, changes with position, no thrill, no other abnormal signs | Children, young adults, pregnancy, anaemia, fever, thyrotoxicosis |

#### Diastolic murmurs (always pathological)

| Murmur | Characteristics | Associated signs |
|---|---|---|
| **Aortic regurgitation** | Early diastolic, high-pitched, decrescendo; best heard at the left sternal edge with the patient **leaning forward and in held expiration** | Collapsing (water-hammer) pulse, wide pulse pressure, displaced thrusting apex; Corrigan (visible carotid pulsation), de Musset (head nodding), Quincke (capillary pulsation in nail beds), Duroziez (femoral to-and-fro murmur); **Austin Flint murmur** (a mid-diastolic apical rumble) |
| **Mitral stenosis** | Low-pitched, rumbling mid-diastolic with presystolic accentuation (if in sinus rhythm), best at the apex with the **bell, in the left lateral position, in expiration** | Loud S1, opening snap, tapping apex, malar flush, small-volume irregular pulse (AF), signs of pulmonary hypertension and right heart failure |
| **Pulmonary regurgitation** | Early diastolic at the left sternal edge (Graham Steell murmur in pulmonary hypertension) | Right heart signs |
| **Tricuspid stenosis** | Mid-diastolic, left lower sternal edge, louder on inspiration | Giant a waves, rare |

#### Continuous murmurs

**Patent ductus arteriosus:** machinery murmur beneath the left clavicle, continuous through S2. Also arteriovenous fistulae and venous hum (disappears when lying flat or compressing the neck veins).

#### Manoeuvres that help

| Manoeuvre | Effect |
|---|---|
| **Inspiration** | Increases right-sided murmurs (TR, PS) |
| **Expiration** | Increases left-sided murmurs, especially AR |
| **Left lateral position** | Brings out mitral stenosis |
| **Sitting forward, held expiration** | Brings out aortic regurgitation and pericardial rub |
| **Valsalva / standing** | Reduces preload: HCM murmur louder; most other murmurs softer; MVP click earlier |
| **Squatting / passive leg raise** | Increases preload: HCM murmur softer |
| **Handgrip (isometric)** | Increases afterload: MR and VSD murmurs louder; AS and HCM softer |
| **Exercise** | Brings out mitral stenosis |

### 12.12 Peripheral examination

#### Signs of heart failure

- **Left-sided:** breathlessness, orthopnoea, paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnoea, basal crackles, pleural effusions, S3, displaced apex, pulsus alternans.
- **Right-sided:** raised JVP, hepatic congestion (tender pulsatile hepatomegaly), ascites, peripheral oedema (pitting, ankles first, sacrum in the bedbound), hepatojugular reflux.

Examine the **lung bases** (crackles), the **abdomen** (hepatomegaly, ascites), and the **legs and sacrum** (oedema).

#### The peripheral vascular examination

- **Pulses:** brachial, radial, femoral, popliteal, posterior tibial, dorsalis pedis. Grade as absent, reduced, normal or bounding.
- **Bruits:** auscultate over the carotids, abdominal aorta, renal arteries, femoral arteries.
- **Aortic aneurysm:** palpate for an expansile (not merely transmitted) pulsatile mass, just above the umbilicus.
- **Signs of peripheral arterial disease:** cool, pale, hairless limbs, shiny skin, dependent rubor, delayed capillary refill, ulcers (punched-out, painful, at pressure points or toes), reduced or absent pulses, femoral bruits. **Ankle–brachial pressure index (ABPI)** = ankle systolic pressure ÷ brachial systolic pressure: normal 1.0–1.4; <0.9 indicates peripheral arterial disease; <0.5 severe; >1.4 suggests non-compressible (calcified) vessels.
- **Signs of venous disease:** varicose veins, oedema, lipodermatosclerosis, haemosiderin staining, venous ulcers (shallow, on the medial malleolus).
- **Acute limb ischaemia (the 6 Ps):** Pain, Pallor, Pulselessness, Paraesthesia, Paralysis, Perishing cold. An emergency.
- **Deep vein thrombosis:** unilateral calf swelling, tenderness and pitting oedema (see Wells score, Chapter 70).

#### Endocarditis screen

Fever, new or changing murmur, splinter haemorrhages, Osler nodes, Janeway lesions, Roth spots, conjunctival petechiae, splenomegaly, haematuria, clubbing (late), and embolic phenomena.

### 12.13 Putting it together: common syndromes

| Syndrome | Key bedside findings |
|---|---|
| **Heart failure with volume overload** | Breathlessness, orthopnoea, tachycardia, raised JVP, displaced apex, S3, crackles, effusions, hepatomegaly, ascites, peripheral oedema, cool peripheries |
| **Aortic stenosis** | Slow-rising pulse, narrow pulse pressure, heaving apex, ejection systolic murmur radiating to the carotids, soft A₂ |
| **Aortic regurgitation** | Collapsing pulse, wide pulse pressure, thrusting displaced apex, early diastolic murmur, peripheral signs |
| **Mitral stenosis** | Malar flush, irregularly irregular pulse (AF), tapping apex, loud S1, opening snap, mid-diastolic rumble |
| **Mitral regurgitation** | Displaced thrusting apex, pansystolic murmur radiating to the axilla, S3 |
| **Cardiac tamponade** (Beck triad) | Hypotension, raised JVP (with absent y descent), muffled heart sounds; plus tachycardia, pulsus paradoxus |
| **Constrictive pericarditis** | Raised JVP with Kussmaul sign and steep y descent, pericardial knock, ascites and oedema disproportionate to breathlessness |
| **Pericarditis** | Pleuritic, positional chest pain relieved by sitting forward, friction rub |
| **Pulmonary embolism** | Tachycardia, tachypnoea, hypoxia, raised JVP in massive PE, loud P₂, right ventricular heave, unilateral leg swelling |
| **Aortic dissection** | Tearing chest or back pain, blood pressure difference between arms, new aortic regurgitation murmur, pulse deficits, neurological signs |
| **Infective endocarditis** | Fever, new murmur, embolic and immunological stigmata |
| **Atrial fibrillation** | Irregularly irregular pulse, absent a waves, pulse deficit, variable S1 |
| **Hypertensive heart disease** | Sustained BP elevation, heaving apex, S4, retinopathy |

### 12.14 Worked cases

#### Case 1: Syncope in an elderly man

*A 76-year-old man collapsed while climbing the stairs. Over the past 3 months he has had exertional breathlessness and chest tightness. Pulse 72 regular, slow-rising and low volume; BP 110/90 mmHg (narrow pulse pressure). The apex is undisplaced but heaving; there is a systolic thrill at the right second intercostal space. Auscultation: soft, single second heart sound; harsh ejection systolic murmur (grade 4/6) radiating to the carotids, peaking late.*

**Reasoning.** The triad of **exertional syncope, angina and breathlessness** with slow-rising pulse, narrow pulse pressure, heaving apex, thrill, soft A₂ and a late-peaking ejection systolic murmur strongly indicates **severe calcific aortic stenosis.** Symptomatic severe AS has a poor prognosis without valve replacement (average survival around 2–3 years after the onset of syncope, 1–2 years with heart failure).

**Next steps.** ECG, chest X-ray, **echocardiography** (valve area, gradient, LV function), cardiology/heart-team referral for valve replacement (surgical or transcatheter). Avoid vasodilators and strenuous exertion in the meantime.

#### Case 2: Breathlessness and ankle swelling

*A 68-year-old woman with previous myocardial infarction has progressive breathlessness over 3 weeks, needs three pillows, and has swollen ankles. Pulse 98 irregular (AF), BP 108/72. JVP 6 cm above the sternal angle with positive hepatojugular reflux. Apex beat displaced to the anterior axillary line. S3 gallop. Bibasal crackles. Tender hepatomegaly. Pitting oedema to the knees.*

**Reasoning.** Decompensated biventricular **heart failure** with volume overload (raised JVP, S3, crackles, hepatomegaly, oedema), probably ischaemic cardiomyopathy with new AF as a precipitant.

**Next steps.** ECG, chest X-ray, BNP/NT-proBNP, echocardiography, urea and electrolytes, thyroid function, full blood count, troponin. Treat with IV loop diuretic, oxygen if hypoxic, rate control, review of precipitants (ischaemia, infection, non-adherence, anaemia, NSAIDs).

#### Case 3: A new murmur with fever

*A 34-year-old man with a history of injecting drug use has had 10 days of fever, night sweats and malaise. Temperature 38.8 °C, pulse 108. He has splinter haemorrhages and a tender nodule on the pulp of a finger. A new pansystolic murmur at the left lower sternal edge, louder on inspiration. JVP shows prominent v waves. The spleen tip is palpable. Urine dipstick: blood ++.*

**Reasoning.** Fever + new murmur + embolic and immunological phenomena (splinters, Osler node, haematuria, splenomegaly) = **infective endocarditis** until proved otherwise. Injecting drug users usually have right-sided (tricuspid) endocarditis, with *Staphylococcus aureus* the commonest organism; tricuspid regurgitation produces the murmur and v waves; septic pulmonary emboli may cause cough and pleuritic pain.

**Next steps.** At least three sets of blood cultures from separate venepunctures *before* antibiotics (if the patient is stable), echocardiography (transthoracic; transoesophageal if needed), ECG (conduction abnormalities suggest aortic root abscess), inflammatory markers, renal function, chest X-ray. Modified Duke criteria guide diagnosis. Empirical antibiotics after cultures; early involvement of infectious diseases and cardiology.

#### Case 4: Beck's triad

*A 55-year-old man with advanced lung cancer is dyspnoeic and cold, with pulse 124, BP 82/60. His JVP is 8 cm above the sternal angle without a visible y descent. The heart sounds are distant. BP falls by 18 mmHg on inspiration.*

**Reasoning.** Hypotension, raised JVP, muffled heart sounds, tachycardia and pulsus paradoxus = **cardiac tamponade** (malignant pericardial effusion). This is a clinical diagnosis requiring urgent action.

**Next steps.** Urgent bedside echocardiography, IV fluid bolus for temporary support, and **urgent pericardiocentesis** (ideally echo-guided). Avoid diuretics and avoid intubation with positive pressure if possible.

#### Case 5: Blood pressure differential and chest pain

*A 52-year-old man with hypertension has sudden tearing chest pain radiating to the interscapular region. BP 182/100 in the right arm, 140/80 in the left. Soft early diastolic murmur at the left sternal edge, not previously documented. Pulse 100.*

**Reasoning.** Sudden severe tearing pain, inter-arm BP difference, a new aortic regurgitation murmur and hypertension: **acute aortic dissection** (probably Stanford type A, involving the ascending aorta).

**Next steps.** Resuscitation area, IV access, analgesia, cautious blood pressure and heart-rate control (e.g. IV beta-blocker first), urgent CT angiography of the aorta (or transoesophageal echocardiography if unstable), immediate cardiothoracic surgical referral. **Do not give anticoagulants or thrombolytics.**

#### Case 6: The innocent murmur

*A 22-year-old woman presents for a pre-employment medical. She is asymptomatic. Pulse 68 regular, BP 112/68. JVP not raised. Apex beat normal. A soft (grade 2/6), short mid-systolic murmur at the left sternal edge, louder when supine and quieter when sitting, with normal S1 and S2 and no thrill, no click, no radiation.*

**Reasoning.** Features of an **innocent (flow) murmur:** soft, short, systolic, no associated abnormal signs, positional variation, asymptomatic. No investigation is needed if the history and examination are otherwise normal; echocardiography should be considered if any red flag exists (symptoms, family history of cardiomyopathy or sudden death, diastolic or pansystolic murmur, grade ≥3, thrill, abnormal sounds).

*Lesson.* Distinguishing innocent from pathological murmurs prevents both under- and over-investigation.

---

### 12.15 Common pitfalls

- **Counting the pulse for too short a time** in an irregular rhythm.
- **Checking only the radial pulse** for character; use the carotid or brachial pulse.
- **Measuring the JVP with the patient at the wrong angle** or confusing it with the carotid pulse.
- **Not looking at the neck in good light, with the head slightly turned.**
- **Failing to time the murmur** (palpate the carotid at the same time).
- **Using the bell and diaphragm incorrectly.**
- **Not repositioning the patient** (left lateral for mitral stenosis, forward and expiration for aortic regurgitation).
- **Forgetting the peripheral signs** (endocarditis stigmata, oedema, hepatomegaly).
- **Equating intensity with severity.**
- **Missing atrial fibrillation** (check the pulse for regularity routinely, particularly in patients with unexplained stroke, breathlessness or palpitations).
- **Dismissing a murmur** in a febrile patient with risk factors.
- **Relying on auscultation alone** when echocardiography is indicated.

---

### 12.16 Key points

- Follow a fixed sequence: general inspection, hands, pulse, blood pressure, face, JVP and carotids, precordium (inspect, palpate, auscultate), then peripheral signs.
- Assess rate, rhythm, character and volume of the pulse; check for radio-femoral delay and inter-arm BP difference.
- The JVP (height above the sternal angle, waveform, hepatojugular reflux) gives the filling pressure of the right heart.
- Describe murmurs by timing, location, radiation, intensity, quality and variation; diastolic murmurs are always pathological.
- Use positioning and manoeuvres to bring out specific murmurs.
- Combine signs to recognise syndromes: heart failure, valve lesions, tamponade, endocarditis and dissection.
- Normal examination does not exclude significant cardiac disease; know when to proceed to ECG and echocardiography.

### 12.17 Self-assessment

1. How is the JVP measured, and what height above the sternal angle is abnormal?
   *Answer: Vertical height of the top of the internal jugular pulsation above the sternal angle, with the patient at 45°; more than 3 cm is raised (central venous pressure = height + 5 cm).*

2. Describe the murmur and the pulse of severe aortic stenosis.
   *Answer: Ejection systolic crescendo-decrescendo murmur at the right second intercostal space radiating to the carotids (peaking late in severe disease); slow-rising, low-volume pulse with narrow pulse pressure.*

3. Which position and manoeuvre bring out (a) mitral stenosis and (b) aortic regurgitation?
   *Answer: (a) Left lateral position, bell, in expiration; (b) sitting forward, diaphragm, held expiration.*

4. What does Kussmaul's sign indicate, and give two causes.
   *Answer: A paradoxical rise in the JVP on inspiration; constrictive pericarditis, restrictive cardiomyopathy, right ventricular infarction, severe right heart failure.*

5. List the components of Beck's triad.
   *Answer: Hypotension, raised JVP, muffled (distant) heart sounds.*

6. Name four features that would make a systolic murmur more likely to be pathological than innocent.
   *Answer (any four): symptoms, grade ≥3 or thrill, pansystolic or late systolic, diastolic component, radiation, abnormal S2, ejection click, fixed split S2, abnormal pulse or apex, family history of sudden death or cardiomyopathy, associated cyanosis or clubbing.*
