## Chapter 3. Building a Differential Diagnosis

### Learning objectives

After studying this chapter you should be able to:

1. Create a concise problem representation from the history and examination.
2. Generate a differential diagnosis using anatomical, pathophysiological and VINDICATE-style frameworks.
3. Build an explicit "can't miss" list for any presentation.
4. Rank diagnoses by likelihood, danger and treatability, and state them as leading, alternative and active-exclude diagnoses.
5. Revise the differential as new data arrive, and recognise when the list is too long, too short or wrongly framed.

---

### 3.1 What a differential diagnosis is, and is not

A **differential diagnosis** is a *ranked, purposeful list* of the conditions that could explain the patient's problem, along with a plan to separate them. It is not a brain-dump of every disease that could cause the symptom, and it is not a single diagnosis with a vague "?" next to it.

A good differential has the following properties:

- **Short enough to use**: usually three to six items for a first consultation.
- **Ranked** by what matters: probability, danger and treatability.
- **Anchored in the patient**: age, sex, context and risk factors shape the list.
- **Linked to actions**: each item implies something to do, ask, examine or test.
- **Dynamic**: it changes with every new piece of information.

> A differential is a *tool for deciding the next step*, not a record of your erudition.

---

### 3.2 Step one: the problem representation

Before listing diagnoses, summarise the case in one or two sentences. This **problem representation** compresses the key features and uses *semantic qualifiers*: contrasting descriptors that organise knowledge (acute vs chronic, constant vs intermittent, focal vs diffuse, unilateral vs bilateral, febrile vs afebrile, progressive vs stable).

A well-made problem representation has this shape:

> **[Age] [sex] with [relevant background] presenting with [time course] [key symptom(s) with qualifiers] and [key positive/negative findings].**

**Example 1 (poor):** "Patient with chest pain."

**Example 1 (better):** "A 58-year-old man with hypertension and a 30-pack-year smoking history, with 40 minutes of *acute, severe, central, pressure-like* chest pain radiating to the left arm, associated with sweating and nausea, with *no* pleuritic component or positional change."

The second version immediately evokes a script. It also makes the missing information obvious: Has he had this before? Is the pain still present? What do the vital signs and ECG show?

Why this step matters:

- It forces you to decide what is **important** (not every detail is).
- It helps you notice what **doesn't fit** a candidate diagnosis.
- It makes communication, presentation and handover cleaner.
- It protects against anchoring on one word in the chief complaint.

---

### 3.3 Step two: generate hypotheses with a framework

Frameworks help in two ways: they prompt hypotheses you might forget, and they structure the list so that it is not a random collection. Use whichever fits the problem. Experienced clinicians often combine two.

#### 3.3.1 Anatomical framework

Work through the structures in the affected region, from superficial to deep. It is excellent for pain, swelling, and focal deficits.

**Example: right upper quadrant abdominal pain.** Consider the structures in or near the region:

| Structure | Candidate conditions |
|---|---|
| Liver | Hepatitis, abscess, hepatic congestion, tumour |
| Gallbladder / biliary tree | Biliary colic, cholecystitis, cholangitis, choledocholithiasis |
| Duodenum / stomach | Peptic ulcer, gastritis |
| Pancreas | Pancreatitis |
| Right kidney / ureter | Pyelonephritis, stone |
| Right lung base / pleura | Pneumonia, pulmonary embolism, pleurisy |
| Right colon / appendix | Appendicitis (high-lying), hepatic flexure pathology |
| Abdominal wall / spine | Herpes zoster, muscle strain, rib pathology |
| Referred / other | Right-sided myocardial infarction (inferior), diabetic ketoacidosis |

The last row is important: the anatomical approach should include **referred** and **systemic** causes.

#### 3.3.2 Pathophysiological framework

Ask: "What *processes* could cause this?" It is very useful when the anatomy is not localised (fever, fatigue, weight loss, anaemia) or when you want to cross-check an anatomical list.

Common processes: inflammation / infection; ischaemia and infarction; haemorrhage; obstruction; neoplasia; metabolic / endocrine disturbance; autoimmune; degenerative; toxic / drug-induced; traumatic; psychiatric / functional.

**Example: breathlessness** by mechanism: increased resistance (asthma, COPD), reduced lung compliance (fibrosis, oedema), impaired gas exchange (pneumonia, PE), pump failure (heart failure), reduced oxygen-carrying capacity (anaemia), increased drive (metabolic acidosis, anxiety, pregnancy), neuromuscular weakness (Guillain–Barré), chest wall problems (pneumothorax, effusion, obesity).

#### 3.3.3 The VINDICATE(S) mnemonic

A broad systematic prompt to ensure no category is overlooked:

| Letter | Category | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **V** | Vascular | MI, stroke, aneurysm, embolism, vasculitis |
| **I** | Infectious / Inflammatory | Pneumonia, abscess, sepsis, IBD |
| **N** | Neoplastic | Primary and metastatic cancers, lymphoma |
| **D** | Degenerative / Drugs / Deficiency | Osteoarthritis, dementia; adverse effects; vitamin deficiency |
| **I** | Iatrogenic / Idiopathic / Intoxication | Procedure complications; unknown cause; alcohol, poisoning |
| **C** | Congenital | Structural heart disease, inherited disorders |
| **A** | Autoimmune / Allergic | SLE, rheumatoid arthritis, anaphylaxis |
| **T** | Trauma | Fracture, haematoma, non-accidental injury |
| **E** | Endocrine / Metabolic | Diabetes, thyroid disease, electrolytes |
| **S** | Psychiatric / Social ("Psychogenic") | Depression, anxiety, abuse, social isolation |

Use VINDICATE(S) as a *checklist at the end* ("Have I considered a drug cause? An endocrine one?"), rather than as the main generator. Used mechanically at the start, it produces long lists that are hard to rank.

#### 3.3.4 The "system review" approach

Walk through organ systems when a symptom is non-specific (fatigue, weight loss, fever of unknown origin). It prevents tunnel vision but is inefficient unless you also use probability.

#### 3.3.5 Combining frameworks

For most cases, the best strategy is:

1. Start with **pattern recognition**: what script does this resemble?
2. Add structure with an **anatomical or pathophysiological** list.
3. Check with a **VINDICATE** sweep and a **can't-miss** pass (below).

---

### 3.4 Step three: the "can't miss" list

For every presentation, ask: "What are the diagnoses that, if I miss them, could kill or disable the patient (or cause irreversible harm)?" This is the **can't-miss list** (also known as "rule out worst-case scenario", RWS).

Examples of can't-miss diagnoses by presentation:

| Presentation | Can't-miss diagnoses |
|---|---|
| Chest pain | Acute coronary syndrome, pulmonary embolism, aortic dissection, tension pneumothorax, oesophageal rupture, cardiac tamponade |
| Headache | Subarachnoid haemorrhage, meningitis / encephalitis, intracranial mass or haemorrhage, giant cell arteritis, carbon monoxide poisoning, cerebral venous thrombosis |
| Abdominal pain | Ruptured aortic aneurysm, perforated viscus, mesenteric ischaemia, ectopic pregnancy, appendicitis, bowel obstruction, DKA |
| Back pain | Cauda equina syndrome / cord compression, aortic aneurysm or dissection, vertebral infection, malignancy, fracture |
| Dizziness | Posterior circulation stroke, arrhythmia, severe anaemia, hypoglycaemia |
| Fever | Sepsis, meningitis, endocarditis, malaria (if exposed), neutropenic sepsis |
| Syncope | Arrhythmia, aortic stenosis, PE, GI haemorrhage, ectopic pregnancy |
| Child with fever | Sepsis / meningitis, pneumonia, UTI, Kawasaki disease |

**Key principle.** The can't-miss list is *not* a list of probable diagnoses. It is a list of diagnoses you must **actively consider and deliberately exclude or treat**. You can usually exclude them at low cost with history, examination or one or two tests. When you cannot, you either treat empirically or arrange safe follow-up.

#### Practical method

For each can't-miss diagnosis, answer three questions:

1. **Is there anything in the history or examination that raises it?**
2. **Can I exclude it now, reasonably?** (How? With which finding or test?)
3. **If I cannot exclude it, what is my plan?**

Record the answers in the notes, briefly. This is also good medicolegal practice because it makes your reasoning visible.

---

### 3.5 Step four: rank and organise

A list of eight diagnoses is not yet a plan. Rank them using **three questions**:

1. **Likelihood**: How probable is this given the patient and the setting? (prevalence × fit with features)
2. **Danger**: How bad is the outcome if I miss or delay it?
3. **Treatability / actionability**: Is there an effective treatment that depends on early recognition?

A diagnosis that is likely, dangerous *and* treatable should lead the work-up. A rare, untreatable, non-urgent condition can wait.

A helpful way to present the ranked list is:

- **Leading diagnosis**: the single most probable explanation.
- **Alternative diagnoses**: other plausible explanations.
- **Active exclude (must-not-miss)** diagnoses: lower probability but high stakes.

**Worked example: 70-year-old woman with sudden dyspnoea.**

| Category | Diagnosis | Reasoning |
|---|---|---|
| Leading | Acute decompensated heart failure | Known heart failure, orthopnoea, weight gain, bilateral crackles |
| Alternative | Pneumonia | Cough, but no fever or focal signs |
| Alternative | COPD exacerbation | Smoking history, but no wheeze |
| Active exclude | Pulmonary embolism | Recent hip surgery, tachycardia |
| Active exclude | Acute coronary syndrome as the precipitant | Risk factors; need ECG and troponin |
| Active exclude | Pneumothorax | Sudden onset; examine for hyperresonance, check CXR |

Note that the *plan* is already visible: ECG, troponin, chest X-ray, BNP, a PE risk assessment, and a diuretic if the heart failure picture is confirmed.

### 3.6 Principles for good differentials

#### Occam's razor and Hickam's dictum

- **Occam's razor**: prefer a single unifying diagnosis over several unrelated ones. This is usually right for *young* patients with an acute illness.
- **Hickam's dictum**: "A patient can have as many diseases as he pleases." In *older* patients with multiple comorbidities, two or three concurrent conditions are common (heart failure and pneumonia and anaemia).
- **Saint's triad** (a classic teaching example) shows that unusual combinations do occur: hiatus hernia, gallstones and diverticular disease.

Practical lesson: start with a unifying diagnosis, but if loose ends remain (a finding not explained), ask whether a second diagnosis is present.

#### Think about the patient, not only the symptom

The same symptom has different probabilities in different people.

- **Age.** Headache in a 70-year-old with new onset raises giant cell arteritis and mass lesions; in a 20-year-old, tension-type headache and migraine dominate.
- **Sex.** Abdominal pain in a woman of reproductive age always includes pregnancy-related causes.
- **Context.** Travel, occupation, medications, immunosuppression, recent procedures.
- **Time.** An abrupt onset suggests a vascular or mechanical event; a gradual onset suggests inflammation, neoplasia or degenerative processes.

#### Respect "red flags" and "green flags"

- **Red flags** are findings that raise the probability of serious disease: unexplained weight loss, night pain, fever with back pain, new neurological deficit, haematuria in a smoker, a new murmur with fever.
- **Green flags** (less often discussed) are findings that lower it: a well-looking patient with normal vital signs and a benign examination who has had a stable course.

Neither is definitive. They shift the probability.

#### Include what is *common*, not just what is dangerous

A differential made only of rare and catastrophic diagnoses will lead to over-investigation. Always ask: "What is the most common cause of this in this kind of patient?" In primary care, common causes explain the great majority of presentations.

#### Include the "diagnosis of exclusion" with care

Functional and psychiatric diagnoses (irritable bowel syndrome, anxiety, somatic symptom disorder) are legitimate, common and treatable, but they are best made on *positive* features, not merely when everything else is normal. Keep them on the list from early on, not only as a last resort.

### 3.7 Updating and pruning the differential

The differential should change at each step:

1. **After the history**: some items rise, some fall.
2. **After the examination**: a physical sign might sharply increase one hypothesis (e.g. a tender, rigid abdomen).
3. **After the first investigations**: the result moves probabilities (Chapter 4).
4. **After the response to treatment or the passage of time**.

Warning signs that the differential has gone wrong:

- **It is too long** (more than 8–10 items): you have not prioritised or you lack a clear problem representation.
- **It is too short** (a single diagnosis): you may be anchored.
- **It does not explain all the findings**: either you missed a diagnosis or a finding is incidental.
- **It has not changed in 48 hours** despite new data: you may have stopped thinking.
- **It is framed incorrectly**: e.g. the problem is "acute kidney injury" but the list covers only chronic kidney diseases.

Re-framing is sometimes the biggest step. A patient labelled "chest pain" who really has *exertional dyspnoea* needs a different list. "Dizzy" may mean vertigo, presyncope, disequilibrium or lightheadedness; each leads to a different set of diagnoses (Chapter 24).

### 3.8 Worked cases

#### Case 1: Fatigue in a young woman

*A 29-year-old woman reports 3 months of increasing tiredness. She is vegetarian, has heavy menstrual periods, and sleeps 6–7 hours. No fever, cough or weight change. Examination shows pallor of the conjunctivae; vital signs are normal.*

**Problem representation.** "A 29-year-old woman with 3 months of progressive fatigue, heavy menstruation and a vegetarian diet, with pallor."

**Pathophysiological list.**

- Reduced oxygen-carrying capacity: **iron-deficiency anaemia** (heavy periods, diet), B12/folate deficiency (diet).
- Endocrine: **hypothyroidism**.
- Mood: depression.
- Sleep: insufficient sleep / sleep disorder.
- Infection: chronic (HIV, TB, EBV), unlikely without other features.
- Autoimmune: coeliac disease (also causes iron deficiency), SLE.
- Pregnancy.

**Can't miss.** Pregnancy (always), severe anaemia from occult bleeding, haematological malignancy (look for lymphadenopathy, bruising, fever), coeliac disease (treatable and underdiagnosed).

**Ranking.** Leading: iron deficiency. Alternatives: hypothyroidism, B12 deficiency, depression. Active exclude: pregnancy, other causes of blood loss, coeliac disease.

**Plan.** Full blood count with indices, ferritin, thyroid-stimulating hormone, B12, pregnancy test, consider coeliac serology; treat identified deficiency; assess *why* the iron is low.

#### Case 2: Sudden severe abdominal pain

*A 24-year-old woman has 6 hours of lower abdominal pain, worse on the right, with lightheadedness. Her last menstrual period was 7 weeks ago. Pulse is 112, blood pressure 98/60 mmHg. The abdomen is tender with guarding in the right iliac fossa.*

**Problem representation.** "A 24-year-old woman with acute right-sided lower abdominal pain, amenorrhoea for 7 weeks, tachycardia and borderline hypotension."

**Anatomical list for the right iliac fossa.** Appendix (appendicitis), ovary (torsion, cyst rupture), fallopian tube (ectopic pregnancy), ureter (stone), terminal ileum (Crohn's), pelvic infection (PID), urinary infection.

**Can't miss.** **Ruptured ectopic pregnancy** (haemodynamic instability + amenorrhoea), appendicitis with perforation, ovarian torsion.

**Ranking.** The combination of amenorrhoea and instability makes ectopic pregnancy the **leading diagnosis requiring immediate action** (it is both dangerous and treatable), while appendicitis remains a strong alternative.

**Plan.** Resuscitate (two large-bore IV lines, fluids, crossmatch), urgent pregnancy test (urine or serum β-hCG), bedside ultrasound if available, early gynaecology and surgical involvement. Do not delay resuscitation for confirmation.

*Lesson.* A pregnancy test in every woman of reproductive age with abdominal pain is a cognitive forcing strategy, not an afterthought.

#### Case 3: Headache in a 72-year-old

*A 72-year-old woman has had 2 weeks of a new, persistent right-sided headache with scalp tenderness when brushing her hair, jaw pain on chewing, and morning stiffness in the shoulders. She felt feverish at times and has lost 3 kg.*

**Problem representation.** "A 72-year-old woman with 2 weeks of new unilateral headache, scalp tenderness, jaw claudication, constitutional symptoms and proximal stiffness."

**Differential.**

- Leading: **giant cell arteritis (GCA)**, probably with polymyalgia rheumatica.
- Alternatives: migraine / tension-type (unlikely at this age with new onset), trigeminal neuralgia, cervical spondylosis, sinusitis, temporal lobe mass.
- **Can't miss**: GCA (risk of permanent visual loss), subarachnoid haemorrhage (no thunderclap onset here), intracranial mass, meningitis.

**Plan.** GCA is a **time-critical** diagnosis. Check ESR/CRP and full blood count the same day; start high-dose corticosteroids *without waiting* for biopsy if clinical suspicion is high; refer urgently for temporal artery assessment (biopsy or ultrasound where available); ask about visual symptoms and examine visual acuity and fields.

*Lesson.* An item may be only 40% likely, but if the cost of waiting is blindness, it takes priority.

#### Case 4: A differential that changes

*A 55-year-old man has a 3-day history of fever and cough. Initial differential: pneumonia (leading), acute bronchitis, influenza. On day 5 he improves slightly, but on day 7 he has worsening shortness of breath and sharp pleuritic chest pain on the right. Examination: reduced breath sounds and stony dullness at the right base.*

**Revised problem representation.** "A 55-year-old man with a week of pneumonia-type illness, now with new pleuritic pain and signs of a right pleural effusion."

**Revised differential.** Parapneumonic effusion / empyema (leading), pulmonary embolism with infarction, malignant effusion, heart failure (less likely given unilateral signs).

**Plan.** Chest ultrasound or CXR, diagnostic pleural fluid sampling (pH, glucose, protein, LDH, culture), review of antibiotic cover, early respiratory input.

*Lesson.* A differential made on day 1 is not the differential on day 7. A fresh problem representation is the first step in re-thinking.

### 3.9 Common pitfalls

- **A long list with no ranking.** Generates tests rather than decisions.
- **Omitting the benign, common cause.** Leads to over-investigation.
- **Forgetting the patient's age and sex** (the pregnancy test; GCA in the elderly; testicular torsion in a boy with abdominal pain).
- **Omitting drugs and toxins**: ask about prescribed, over-the-counter, herbal and recreational substances, and alcohol.
- **Conflating a symptom with a diagnosis** ("dizziness", "chest pain", "confusion" are problems, not diagnoses).
- **Failing to revisit the list** when new data appear.
- **Skipping the "can't miss" pass** because the patient looks well. Many fatal conditions begin with a well-looking patient.

---

### 3.10 Key points

- A differential is a ranked, patient-specific list that guides the next step.
- Start with a precise problem representation using semantic qualifiers.
- Generate hypotheses using anatomical, pathophysiological and VINDICATE(S) frameworks; combine them with pattern recognition.
- Always build a **can't-miss list** and decide, for each item, how it will be excluded or treated.
- Rank by **likelihood, danger and treatability**.
- Use Occam's razor in the young and Hickam's dictum in the multimorbid.
- Revise the differential at each new piece of information.

### 3.11 Self-assessment

1. Rewrite as a problem representation: "Man, 60, with cough."
   *Answer (example): "A 60-year-old male smoker with a 6-week history of productive cough, 5 kg weight loss and one episode of haemoptysis."* (any version with qualifiers, time course and key positives/negatives is acceptable).

2. List four can't-miss diagnoses for a headache.
   *Answer: subarachnoid haemorrhage; meningitis/encephalitis; giant cell arteritis; intracranial mass or haemorrhage; cerebral venous thrombosis; carbon monoxide poisoning.*

3. A woman of reproductive age has acute abdominal pain. Which investigation should precede almost all others?
   *Answer: A pregnancy test.*

4. When is Hickam's dictum more useful than Occam's razor?
   *Answer: In older patients with multiple chronic conditions, where two or more concurrent diagnoses are common.*

5. A diagnosis is estimated at 5% probability but would cause blindness within days if untreated. Does it belong in the active work-up?
   *Answer: Yes. Because of high stakes and treatability, it merits active exclusion even at low probability.*
