# Chapter 66. Chest and Abdominal X-ray

## Learning objectives

After studying this chapter you should be able to:

1. Describe the five basic radiographic densities and explain how projection, rotation, inspiration and penetration affect interpretation.
2. Apply a systematic approach to the chest X-ray (airway, breathing, circulation, diaphragm, everything else / bones / soft tissues / tubes and lines) and avoid satisfaction of search.
3. Recognise consolidation, collapse, pleural effusion, pneumothorax (including tension), pulmonary oedema, interstitial disease, masses and nodules, hilar abnormalities, cardiomegaly and mediastinal widening.
4. Identify the signs that matter: air bronchogram, silhouette sign, Golden S sign, deep sulcus sign, Kerley B lines, bat's-wing opacification, continuous diaphragm sign.
5. Check the position of lines and tubes (endotracheal, nasogastric, central venous catheter, chest drain, pacemaker leads) and recognise complications.
6. Interpret the abdominal X-ray: bowel gas pattern, dilated small and large bowel, free gas, calcifications, masses, foreign bodies and the limits of the investigation.
7. Decide when a plain film is appropriate, when a CT or ultrasound is the better first test, and when a normal film must not reassure.
8. Communicate urgent findings and understand radiation exposure, pregnancy precautions and image-quality limitations.

> **Note.** Plain radiographs have limited sensitivity. A normal chest film does not exclude pneumonia, pulmonary embolism, early malignancy or aortic injury; a normal abdominal film does not exclude obstruction, perforation or ischaemia. Imaging requests and interpretations must be tied to the clinical question.

## 1. Principles of plain radiography

### 1.1 The five densities

| Density | Appearance | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Air | Black | Lungs, bowel gas, pneumothorax |
| Fat | Dark grey | Subcutaneous tissue, retroperitoneal fat, properitoneal stripe |
| Soft tissue / fluid | Mid grey | Heart, vessels, muscle, organs, effusion |
| Bone / calcium | White | Ribs, spine, stones, vascular calcification |
| Metal / contrast | Bright white | Pacemakers, sternal wires, barium, tubes |

An edge is visible only where two densities abut. **Silhouette sign**: a lesion of soft-tissue density touching a structure of soft-tissue density (heart, aortic knuckle, diaphragm) obliterates its border, localising the lesion: right middle lobe disease blurs the right heart border; lingula disease blurs the left heart border; lower lobe disease blurs the hemidiaphragm; the aortic knuckle is obscured by left upper lobe disease.

### 1.2 Technique and projection

- **PA erect** (posteroanterior, patient standing, film behind the chest, tube 1.8 m): the standard. The heart is minimally magnified; cardiothoracic ratio (CTR) > 50% is abnormal.
- **AP supine/sitting** (portable): the heart and mediastinum appear magnified, scapulae project over the lungs, the film is usually underinspired. Fluid layers posteriorly (effusion appears as a hazy opacity over the whole hemithorax, not a meniscus); small pneumothoraces may be missed because air collects anteriorly.
- **Lateral view**: localises lesions (retrosternal, retrocardiac space), assesses the posterior costophrenic angles, vertebral bodies (spine sign: vertebrae should become progressively darker towards the diaphragm; an abrupt increase in density suggests lower lobe consolidation or effusion).
- **Lateral decubitus**: demonstrates free pleural fluid (layering ≥ 5–10 mL) and small pneumothoraces in patients who cannot stand.
- **Expiratory film**: increases sensitivity for small pneumothorax and air trapping (foreign body).

### 1.3 Quality checks (RIP)

- **R**otation: medial ends of the clavicles equidistant from the spinous processes. Rotation makes one lung appear whiter (the side rotated away) and distorts the mediastinum.
- **I**nspiration: adequate if 5–6 anterior ribs (or 9–10 posterior) are visible above the diaphragm. Poor inspiration crowds the vessels, mimics basal consolidation and enlarges the heart.
- **P**enetration: the vertebral bodies should just be visible through the heart. Underpenetration whitens the lungs (mimicking oedema or consolidation); overpenetration blackens them (hides small lesions).
- **A**ngulation and **p**atient position, **m**arkers (right/left), **n**ame and date; compare with prior films (the most valuable "test").

## 2. A systematic approach to the chest X-ray

Many errors arise from "satisfaction of search": finding one abnormality and stopping. Use a fixed routine; one example (ABCDE + tubes):

1. **Patient details, projection, quality (RIP)**.
2. **A – Airway**: trachea central? Slightly deviated to the right at the aortic arch is normal. Carina angle (< 80°), main bronchi patency, foreign body.
3. **B – Breathing (lungs and pleura)**: compare zones (upper, middle, lower; look behind the heart and the hila) for symmetry in density and vascularity; pleural lines, effusions, costophrenic angles, lung edges, apices (Pancoast, apical cap).
4. **C – Circulation (heart and mediastinum)**: heart size (CTR < 0.5 on PA), borders, aortic knuckle, mediastinal width (< 8 cm on PA erect), hilar position (left hilum higher than right by up to 2.5 cm) and density, paratracheal stripe, aortopulmonary window, calcification.
5. **D – Diaphragm**: right higher than left by 1–3 cm; costophrenic and cardiophrenic angles sharp; free air under the diaphragm; hiatus hernia (retrocardiac air-fluid level); gastric bubble.
6. **E – Everything else / edges**: bones (ribs for fractures or lytic lesions, clavicles, scapulae, humeral heads, spine), soft tissues (mastectomy, subcutaneous emphysema, masses), upper abdomen (free air, calcifications), lines and tubes.
7. **Review areas** where lesions hide: **apices, behind the heart, hila, under the diaphragm, rib overlap, lung edges**. Compare both sides in every zone.

**Describe before diagnosing**: location, size, shape, margins, density, associated findings; then interpret in context.

## 3. Lung and pleural abnormalities

### 3.1 Consolidation (airspace opacification)

Alveoli filled with pus, water, blood, cells or mucus. Features: homogeneous or patchy opacity, ill-defined borders unless bounded by a fissure, **air bronchograms** (air-filled bronchi visible against opaque alveoli), silhouette sign, no volume loss (unless collapse coexists).

| Cause | Clues |
|---|---|
| **Pneumonia** (lobar: pneumococcus; bronchopneumonia: patchy, *Staphylococcus*, *Klebsiella*) | Fever, cough, sputum, raised CRP; evolves over days; resolves in 4–6 weeks (slower in the elderly, smokers); follow-up film at 6 weeks in smokers > 50 years to exclude underlying carcinoma |
| **Pulmonary oedema** | Bilateral perihilar, symmetrical (see below) |
| **Haemorrhage** | Haemoptysis, anaemia, vasculitis, Goodpasture; bilateral, rapid change |
| **Aspiration** | Dependent segments: right lower lobe or posterior upper lobe when supine |
| **Organising pneumonia, eosinophilic pneumonia, vasculitis, lymphoma, adenocarcinoma in situ** | Persistent, migratory or non-resolving consolidation despite antibiotics |
| **Pulmonary infarct** | Peripheral wedge-shaped (Hampton hump), pleuritic pain |

**Localising lobes (with silhouette sign)**: RUL – above the horizontal fissure, preserves right heart border; RML – obscures the right heart border; RLL – obscures the right hemidiaphragm; LUL – hazy opacity, obscures left heart border/aortic knuckle in the upper zone; lingula – obscures left heart border; LLL – obscures left hemidiaphragm, "sail sign" behind the heart.

**Radiographic limits**: the chest X-ray misses 10–20% of pneumonias seen on CT, especially early, in neutropenia, dehydration, or when obesity and positioning limit quality. Treat the clinical picture; do not delay antibiotics for a negative film in a patient with a high probability of pneumonia.

### 3.2 Lobar collapse (volume loss)

Signs: **displacement of fissures**, increased density of the collapsed lobe, **shift of hilum, trachea or mediastinum towards the collapse**, elevated hemidiaphragm, crowded ribs, compensatory hyperinflation of the other lobes. Causes: obstructing bronchial carcinoma, mucus plug (post-operative, asthma, ICU), foreign body, extrinsic compression (nodes), tuberculosis.

| Lobe | Appearance |
|---|---|
| **RUL** | Triangular opacity at the right apex, horizontal fissure raised and concave, "Golden S sign" when a central mass causes the collapse (reverse S-shaped fissure) |
| **RML** | Loss of right heart border; thin wedge on lateral view |
| **RLL** | Triangular opacity behind the heart; right hilum depressed; right diaphragm obscured |
| **LUL** | Veil-like haze over the left hemithorax (anterior, on a PA film), "luftsichel" sign (crescent of aerated LLL apex between collapsed LUL and aortic arch), loss of left heart border |
| **LLL** | Triangular retrocardiac opacity ("sail sign"), loss of left diaphragm outline, left hilum lowered |
| **Total lung collapse** | Opaque hemithorax with ipsilateral mediastinal shift (differentiate from a large effusion, which pushes the mediastinum away) |

### 3.3 Pleural effusion

- **Erect PA**: blunting of the costophrenic angle (≥ 200–300 mL), meniscus sign (concave upwards, higher laterally), homogeneous opacity obliterating the hemidiaphragm; large effusions opacify the hemithorax and push the mediastinum to the opposite side (if the mediastinum is central or shifts towards the opacity consider underlying collapse, mesothelioma or bronchial obstruction).
- **Supine AP**: veil-like generalised haze over the lower lung zones; apical capping; need decubitus film or ultrasound.
- **Subpulmonic effusion**: apparent elevated hemidiaphragm with peak shifted laterally, gastric bubble to hemidiaphragm gap increased.
- **Loculated effusion/empyema**: D-shaped opacity with convex edge towards the lung; fixed on decubitus views; CT/ultrasound to guide drainage.
- **Ultrasound** is better than X-ray for detecting and characterising effusions (septations, echogenicity) and for guiding aspiration (chapter 67).
- Causes: see chapter 64 (transudate vs exudate).

### 3.4 Pneumothorax

- **Signs**: visible visceral pleural line (thin white line) with absent lung markings peripherally; on supine films, **deep sulcus sign** (abnormally deep and lucent costophrenic sulcus), double diaphragm contour, sharply defined heart border.
- **Size** (BTS: rim of air < 2 cm = small, ≥ 2 cm at the level of the hilum = large; ACCP: apex-to-cupola distance 3 cm).
- **Tension pneumothorax** is a **clinical diagnosis**: tracheal deviation, hypotension, tachycardia, distended neck veins, hyper-resonance and absent breath sounds, respiratory distress. Do not wait for a radiograph: needle decompression (second intercostal space midclavicular or fourth–fifth intercostal space anterior axillary line) followed by chest drain. Radiographic features if taken: mediastinal shift away, flattened hemidiaphragm, widened rib spaces, collapsed lung.
- **Mimics**: skin folds (extend beyond the chest wall; lung markings continue beyond the line), bullae (concave to the chest wall), scapular edge, clothing/hair braids, medial vessel shadows.
- **Associated**: pneumomediastinum (lucent streaks along the heart and mediastinum, continuous diaphragm sign), subcutaneous emphysema (linear streaks in soft tissue), pneumopericardium.
- **Hydropneumothorax** (air-fluid level) after trauma or drainage.

### 3.5 Pulmonary oedema

**Cardiogenic** features progress with pulmonary venous pressure:
1. **Cardiomegaly**, upper lobe venous diversion (cephalisation, pulmonary venous pressure ~12–18 mmHg).
2. **Interstitial oedema** (18–25): Kerley B lines (short horizontal lines at the lung periphery), peribronchial cuffing, perihilar haze, thickened fissures, subpleural effusion.
3. **Alveolar oedema** (> 25): bilateral perihilar "bat's-wing" opacification, air bronchograms, pleural effusions (bilateral, right > left).

Non-cardiogenic (ARDS, negative-pressure, neurogenic, re-expansion): peripheral or patchy, normal heart size, no Kerley lines or effusions, more asymmetrical; ARDS: bilateral opacities within 1 week of insult. **Differential for bilateral perihilar opacification**: cardiogenic oedema, renal failure/fluid overload, pulmonary haemorrhage, atypical/viral pneumonia (COVID-19: peripheral and lower zone), PCP, aspiration, lymphangitis.

### 3.6 Interstitial and diffuse lung disease

- **Reticular (linear) pattern**: pulmonary fibrosis (basal, subpleural, honeycombing), sarcoidosis, connective tissue disease, asbestosis (with pleural plaques), drug-induced (amiodarone, methotrexate, bleomycin, nitrofurantoin), lymphangitis carcinomatosa (septal lines, hilar nodes).
- **Nodular (miliary)**: TB, histoplasmosis, sarcoidosis, metastases (thyroid, renal, melanoma).
- **Reticulonodular**; **ground-glass** (often needs CT).
- **Upper-zone predominance** ("CASTS"): Coal workers' pneumoconiosis/silicosis, Ankylosing spondylitis, Sarcoidosis, TB, Silicosis, Histiocytosis/Hypersensitivity. **Lower-zone predominance**: IPF, asbestosis, scleroderma, RA, aspiration, drugs.
- **Hyperinflation** (flattened diaphragms, > 7 anterior ribs, long narrow heart, increased retrosternal air): COPD, asthma, bronchiolitis.
- **HRCT** is the investigation for interstitial disease.

### 3.7 Nodules, masses and lung cancer

- **Solitary pulmonary nodule** (≤ 3 cm; > 3 cm = mass): well-defined, round. Benign signs: central, laminated or popcorn calcification, stability for ≥ 2 years (solid nodules); malignant: spiculation, size > 2 cm, upper-lobe location, growth (volume doubling time 30–400 days), cavitation with thick irregular wall. CT is required; follow Fleischner or BTS guidelines for management by size/risk.
- **Cavity**: thick walls (> 15 mm) favour malignancy (squamous carcinoma); thin walls with fluid level: abscess (aspiration, *Staph aureus*, *Klebsiella*, anaerobes), TB (apical), granulomatosis with polyangiitis, septic emboli, infected bullae, fungal ball (mycetoma with air-crescent in a pre-existing cavity).
- **Multiple nodules**: metastases ("cannonball"), abscesses/septic emboli, granulomas, rheumatoid nodules, vasculitis, sarcoid.
- **Hilar enlargement**: bilateral symmetrical (sarcoidosis, lymphoma, TB, pulmonary hypertension—pulmonary arteries enlarge, hila vascular), unilateral (carcinoma, lymphoma, TB, aneurysm); hilar point sign; "lumpy bumpy" lobulated in sarcoid.
- **Pancoast tumour**: apical opacity/cap with rib destruction; Horner syndrome, arm pain.
- **Lung cancer clues on CXR**: persistent consolidation, collapse, hilar mass, pleural effusion, rib destruction, raised hemidiaphragm (phrenic nerve palsy), widening of the carina. **A normal CXR does not exclude lung cancer**: urgent CT chest for haemoptysis, persistent cough, weight loss in smokers (chapters 46, 70, 72).

### 3.8 Pulmonary embolism and other vascular patterns

CXR is mostly normal. Signs: Westermark sign (focal oligaemia), Hampton hump (wedge-shaped peripheral opacity), Fleischner sign (enlarged central pulmonary artery), small effusion, elevated hemidiaphragm, atelectasis. Its main value is to exclude alternative diagnoses (pneumothorax, pneumonia, heart failure). **Pulmonary hypertension**: enlarged central pulmonary arteries with peripheral pruning. **Pulmonary arteriovenous malformation**: nodule with feeding vessels.

### 3.9 Tuberculosis

Primary: consolidation with hilar/paratracheal lymphadenopathy (Ghon focus + nodes = Ghon complex), effusion. Post-primary (reactivation): apical/posterior upper lobe and superior lower lobe consolidation, cavitation, fibrosis with volume loss, tracheal deviation, calcified granulomas; miliary pattern (millet-seed nodules) in disseminated disease; effusion (lymphocytic exudate). HIV with low CD4: atypical, lower zone or normal film. A normal film in a high-risk patient with symptoms: still send sputum.

## 4. Heart, mediastinum and great vessels

### 4.1 Cardiomegaly

CTR > 0.5 on PA erect (maximum transverse cardiac diameter ÷ internal thoracic diameter). Causes: LV dilatation, hypertrophy, pericardial effusion ("water-bottle" globular shape, clear lungs), valvular disease, congenital disease; false positives: AP film, expiratory film, pectus excavatum, obesity, supine position, rotation.

Specific signs: **left atrial enlargement** (double right heart border, splayed carina > 90°, elevated left main bronchus, posterior displacement of the oesophagus), **left ventricular enlargement** (apex displaced inferiorly and laterally), **right ventricular enlargement** (apex lifted, filling the retrosternal space on lateral), **mitral stenosis** (straight left heart border, prominent pulmonary arteries, upper lobe diversion), **boot-shaped heart** (tetralogy of Fallot), **egg-on-side** (transposition), **left-sided pericardial calcification** (constrictive pericarditis), **valve calcification**.

### 4.2 Mediastinal widening and masses

Divide into compartments:
- **Anterior (the 4 Ts)**: Thymoma, Teratoma/germ cell tumours, Thyroid (retrosternal goitre; tracheal deviation), Terrible lymphoma; also aortic aneurysm.
- **Middle**: lymph nodes (lymphoma, sarcoid, TB, metastases), bronchogenic and pericardial cysts, aortic arch pathology.
- **Posterior**: neurogenic tumours (schwannoma, neurofibroma), oesophageal lesions (hiatus hernia, achalasia: air-fluid level, dilated oesophagus), paravertebral abscess, extramedullary haematopoiesis.

**Acute aortic pathology**: widened mediastinum (> 8 cm PA, > 6 cm AP supine), loss of aortic knuckle contour, tracheal/NG tube deviation to the right, left apical cap, left haemothorax, depression of left main bronchus, calcium sign (separation > 5 mm between intimal calcification and outer wall in dissection). **A normal CXR does not exclude dissection or traumatic aortic injury**: the chest X-ray has about 90% sensitivity in dissection when widened mediastinum is present but lower for localised or type A; use CT angiography when suspicion is present (chapter 69, ADD-RS).

### 4.3 Pericardial effusion and tamponade

Globular "water-bottle" heart, loss of normal contours, clear lung fields; the fat pad sign on lateral (separation of epicardial and pericardial fat). Echocardiography is the test.

### 4.4 Pneumomediastinum

Air streaks around the heart/aorta, continuous diaphragm sign, thymic sail sign (children), Naclerio's V sign. Causes: asthma/Valsalva, Boerhaave, trauma, tracheobronchial rupture; if vomiting + chest pain + subcutaneous emphysema consider oesophageal rupture (contrast study/CT).

## 5. Lines, tubes and devices

| Device | Correct position | Common complications |
|---|---|---|
| **Endotracheal tube** | Tip 3–5 cm above the carina (T3–T4 level; clavicle heads); head flexion lowers the tip 2 cm | Right main bronchus intubation (left lung collapse), too high (vocal cord injury), oesophageal placement |
| **Nasogastric tube** | Below the diaphragm, in the stomach, midline, tip below the left hemidiaphragm; **never feed before confirming position**; pH testing (≤ 5.5) or radiograph per policy | Misplacement in the bronchus or lung (pneumothorax, empyema); coiling |
| **Central venous catheter (IJ/subclavian)** | Tip at the SVC/right atrium junction, projecting over the right main bronchus/carina | Pneumothorax, haemothorax, arterial placement, malposition (right atrium, azygos, contralateral vein, internal thoracic), pleural infusion |
| **PICC** | SVC–RA junction | Malposition into internal jugular, subclavian |
| **Chest drain** | Within the pleural space, tip towards the apex (pneumothorax) or base (effusion), side holes within the cavity | Subcutaneous kinking, intraparenchymal, fissure, low placement into the abdomen |
| **Pacemaker/ICD leads** | RA lead right atrial appendage; RV lead apex/septum, angled inferiorly and to the left; LV lead in the coronary sinus branch | Lead perforation, fracture, displacement (Twiddler syndrome), pneumothorax, infection |
| **Intra-aortic balloon pump** | Tip 1–2 cm below the left subclavian (aortic knuckle) | Migration |
| **Swan–Ganz catheter** | In the right or left main pulmonary artery, not > 2 cm beyond the hilum | Pulmonary infarction, rupture |
| **Sternotomy wires** | Intact vertical line | Broken wires, sternal dehiscence |
| **Prosthetic valves** | Mechanical valve rings (mitral at lower left, aortic upper right, relative to each other) | |

After any line or tube insertion, review the film for the tip position and for pneumothorax before use (urgent lines excepted).

## 6. The abdominal X-ray (AXR)

### 6.1 Role and limits

The AXR has a **low diagnostic yield** (≈ 10% abnormal and often non-specific) and delivers 0.7 mSv (≈ 35 chest X-rays). It is **not indicated** for undifferentiated abdominal pain, suspected perforation (use erect chest film or CT), appendicitis, pancreatitis or routine screening. Recognised uses: suspected **bowel obstruction** (as an initial investigation in some settings), **toxic megacolon** in colitis (monitoring), **foreign body ingestion or body packers**, **renal tract stones** (KUB when ultrasound/low-dose CT unavailable; only 60–70% of stones radio-opaque), checking the position of devices, constipation in children (limited), and follow-up of known disease. In most acute abdominal presentations, **CT** (or ultrasound in children and pregnancy) is the first imaging test.

### 6.2 Systematic review

1. **Identify** the film: supine (usual) vs erect, name, date, markers.
2. **Gas pattern**: stomach (left upper), small bowel (central, valvulae conniventes cross the entire lumen, normal diameter < 3 cm), large bowel (peripheral, haustral folds do not cross the entire width, normal < 6 cm; caecum < 9 cm). Rigler's sign (both sides of the bowel wall seen: free gas), Rigler's triad of gallstone ileus.
3. **Abnormal gas**: free intraperitoneal gas (best on an **erect chest film**: crescent under the diaphragm; supine AXR: Rigler's sign, football sign, outlined falciform ligament, gas in the right upper quadrant); portal venous gas (branching peripheral lucencies: bowel ischaemia, poor prognosis); biliary gas (pneumobilia: post-sphincterotomy, gallstone ileus, fistula); intramural gas (pneumatosis intestinalis: necrotising enterocolitis, ischaemia, immunosuppression); retroperitoneal gas (mottled around the kidney: duodenal perforation, retroperitoneal appendix); emphysematous pyelonephritis/cholecystitis.
4. **Soft tissue**: psoas margins, renal outlines (the liver edge, spleen, kidneys), bladder, enlarged organs, masses displacing bowel; ascites (ground-glass haze, centralised bowel, bulging flanks, "dog's ears").
5. **Calcification**: renal stones (staghorn, radio-opaque), pancreatic calcification (chronic pancreatitis), gallstones (10–20% radio-opaque), phleboliths (pelvic, round with central lucency), vascular calcification (aortic aneurysm), porcelain gallbladder, adrenal, lymph node, uterine fibroids, appendicolith, mesenteric nodes.
6. **Bones**: lower ribs, lumbar spine, pelvis, sacroiliac joints (sclerosis in sacroiliitis), hips (fractures, metastases: sclerotic prostate, lytic myeloma), Paget's.
7. **Foreign bodies and tubes**: ingested objects, drains, stents, surgical clips, IUCD (position), contraceptive devices, packets of drugs (body packers: multiple oval densities with halo), button batteries (double ring: urgent removal).

### 6.3 Bowel obstruction patterns

**Small bowel obstruction (SBO)**: dilated loops > 3 cm centrally with a "stepladder" arrangement, valvulae conniventes, multiple air-fluid levels on erect film (levels at different heights in the same loop, > 2.5 cm width), collapsed/gasless colon; causes: adhesions (most common), hernia (inguinal, femoral, incisional), neoplasm, Crohn disease, gallstone ileus (Rigler's triad: SBO + pneumobilia + ectopic gallstone), volvulus, intussusception. Complete obstruction vs partial; signs of strangulation (clinical: continuous pain, fever, peritonism, tachycardia, lactate) need CT and surgical review. **CT with IV contrast is the standard** for suspected SBO to identify cause, transition point and ischaemia.

**Large bowel obstruction (LBO)**: dilated colon (> 6 cm; caecum > 9 cm), proximal distribution depending on the competent ileocaecal valve (closed loop → caecal perforation risk, urgent), haustral markings, absent rectal gas. Causes: colorectal carcinoma (the commonest), sigmoid volvulus, diverticular stricture, pseudo-obstruction (Ogilvie syndrome: massive caecal/colonic dilatation with no mechanical cause, elderly, post-operative/critical illness; treat with neostigmine and decompression).

**Volvulus**: *sigmoid* – "coffee-bean" or "inverted U" loop arising from the pelvis toward the right upper quadrant, with the apex above T10, no haustra in the twisted loop; "northern exposure" sign; *caecal* – dilated caecum displaced to the left upper quadrant, "comma" or kidney-bean shape, with small bowel loops and collapsed distal colon; both need decompression/surgery.

**Ileus (paralytic)**: generalised gaseous distension of both small and large bowel without transition point, post-operative, electrolyte disturbance (K), sepsis, drugs (opioids, anticholinergics), pancreatitis, peritonitis; a **sentinel loop** (focal ileus adjacent to inflamed organ: pancreatitis, appendicitis, cholecystitis).

**Toxic megacolon**: transverse colon > 6 cm with loss of haustra and mucosal islands ("thumbprinting") in severe colitis (ulcerative colitis, *C. difficile*, infectious colitis): fever, tachycardia, abdominal tenderness, raised CRP; high risk of perforation. Daily AXR or CT; urgent surgical review.

**Pneumoperitoneum** (perforation): on an erect chest film, free gas under the diaphragm (1 mL sufficient on a good erect film; 10–15 mL for detection in many cases); best seen after the patient sits upright 10 minutes. CT is more sensitive. Do not delay surgical referral if clinical peritonitis is present.

### 6.4 Renal and urinary tract on AXR

KUB for suspected stones: look along the course of the ureters (tips of transverse processes, sacroiliac joints, pelvic brim, ischial spine) and bladder area; distinguish from phleboliths (round, lucent centre, below the ischial spine) and calcified nodes. Non-contrast low-dose CT KUB is the first-line test for renal colic (sensitivity ~95%) with ultrasound in pregnancy and children.

### 6.5 Paediatric considerations

Intussusception (target sign/crescent sign on ultrasound; AXR may show soft tissue mass and paucity of gas), necrotising enterocolitis (pneumatosis, portal gas), pyloric stenosis (ultrasound), malrotation with volvulus (urgent upper GI contrast study: "double bubble" in duodenal atresia). Keep radiation low: ultrasound first, use shielding judiciously and ALARA.

## 7. When to choose another test

| Question | Plain film adequate? | Preferred |
|---|---|---|
| Suspected pneumonia, heart failure, pneumothorax | Yes (first-line) | CT if unclear or complicated; lung ultrasound at bedside |
| Haemoptysis, suspected lung cancer, interstitial disease, PE | Limited | CT chest (HRCT, CTPA) |
| Pleural effusion | Detects | Ultrasound characterises and guides drainage |
| Acute abdomen, suspected perforation | Poor | Erect chest film only if CT unavailable; CT abdomen/pelvis |
| Bowel obstruction | Initial | CT with IV contrast |
| Renal colic | Poor | Low-dose non-contrast CT; ultrasound in pregnancy/children |
| Foreign body, body packing | Useful | CT for packers |
| Aortic dissection, traumatic aortic injury | Poor | CT angiography |
| Fracture | Yes | CT/MRI for complex or occult fractures |
| Dyspnoea in pregnancy | CXR acceptable (fetal dose negligible, ~0.0005–0.01 mGy) | Shield abdomen; ultrasound, V/Q or CTPA per protocol |

**Radiation**: chest X-ray ≈ 0.02 mSv (about 3 days of background); AXR ≈ 0.7 mSv; CT abdomen/pelvis ≈ 8–10 mSv. Justification and optimisation (ALARA). Always ask about pregnancy before abdominal/pelvic imaging in women of reproductive age.

## Worked cases

### Case 1: "Pneumonia" that never clears

**Presentation.** Mr A., 66, ex-smoker, 3 weeks of cough and low-grade fever. CXR: right upper zone consolidation. Treated with amoxicillin, improved symptomatically, but the repeat film at 6 weeks shows persisting opacity with volume loss: raised horizontal fissure, a convex bulge at the hilum ("Golden S sign").

**Reasoning.** Persistent consolidation after adequate treatment, volume loss and a Golden S sign indicate right upper lobe collapse around a central obstructing lesion: bronchial carcinoma until proven otherwise.

**Action.** Urgent CT chest with contrast (extending to adrenals and liver), bronchoscopy and biopsy, staging per lung cancer pathway; smoking cessation; MDT.

**Lessons.** Follow-up films matter in smokers > 50 years. Collapse + hilar mass = cancer until excluded.

### Case 2: Breathless after a central line

**Presentation.** Ms B., 58, post-op in ICU, right IJ central line inserted this morning, now SpO₂ 88%, BP 90/50, HR 130, diminished breath sounds on the right. Portable AP film: right lung edge with absent peripheral markings, deep right sulcus, trachea shifted left, depressed diaphragm.

**Reasoning.** Iatrogenic tension pneumothorax (line insertion, ventilation). Clinical diagnosis: do not delay for the film.

**Action.** Immediate needle decompression (2nd intercostal space mid-clavicular line or 5th anterior axillary), then chest drain; repeat film to confirm lung re-expansion and line position (tip at SVC–RA junction).

**Lessons.** Tension pneumothorax is treated before imaging; check the tip and complications after every line.

### Case 3: Orthopnoea and a "big heart"

**Presentation.** Mr C., 72, 2 weeks of worsening dyspnoea, orthopnoea, ankle swelling. CXR (PA): CTR 0.62, upper lobe diversion, Kerley B lines, bilateral perihilar haze, small bilateral effusions.

**Reasoning.** Interstitial progressing to early alveolar pulmonary oedema with cardiomegaly and effusions: acute decompensated heart failure.

**Action.** Oxygen if hypoxic, IV loop diuretic (furosemide 40–80 mg), vasodilator if hypertensive, NIV if respiratory distress; BNP/NT-proBNP, ECG, troponin, echo; search for precipitants (ischaemia, arrhythmia, non-adherence, infection, renal failure). Repeat CXR is rarely needed; clinical response guides therapy.

**Lessons.** Radiographic signs lag clinical improvement/worsening by hours to days.

### Case 4: Abdominal distension and vomiting after surgery

**Presentation.** Mrs D., 70, previous hysterectomy, 36 hours of colicky abdominal pain, bilious vomiting, distension, absolute constipation. AXR: dilated central small bowel loops 4.5 cm with valvulae conniventes across the lumen, stepladder pattern, no gas in the colon or rectum.

**Reasoning.** Small bowel obstruction, probably adhesional. Strangulation features absent (no fever, no peritonism, lactate 1.4).

**Action.** Nil by mouth, nasogastric decompression, IV fluids and electrolytes, analgesia, antiemetic; CT abdomen with IV contrast (transition point, closed loop, ischaemia); surgical review; water-soluble contrast (Gastrografin) challenge for partial adhesive SBO; serial examination; operate early if peritonism, increasing lactate, closed loop.

**Lessons.** AXR confirms, but CT defines cause and complications.

### Case 5: The elderly patient with a "coffee bean"

**Presentation.** Mr E., 82, care-home resident, chronic constipation, antipsychotics, 2 days of progressive distension and pain, no flatus. AXR: massive dilated loop with a coffee-bean shape rising from the pelvis to the right upper quadrant, absent haustra, loss of the rectal gas.

**Reasoning.** Sigmoid volvulus (older, institutionalised, neuropsychiatric drugs). Risk of ischaemia and perforation.

**Action.** Exclude peritonitis; resuscitate; urgent flexible sigmoidoscopy with rectal tube decompression if no ischaemia; elective sigmoid resection (high recurrence ~50–60%); emergency laparotomy (Hartmann's) if gangrene or perforation; CT if diagnosis unclear.

**Lessons.** The coffee-bean sign is distinctive; decompress and plan definitive surgery.

### Case 6: Sudden severe abdominal pain with a "normal" abdominal film

**Presentation.** Mr F., 55, ulcer history, sudden severe epigastric pain, rigid abdomen, tachycardic. Supine AXR: no clear abnormality. Erect chest film (sitting 10 minutes): crescent of gas under the right hemidiaphragm.

**Reasoning.** Perforated peptic ulcer: pneumoperitoneum. Free gas is easily missed on supine AXR; the erect chest film is more sensitive.

**Action.** NBM, IV fluids, broad-spectrum antibiotics, PPI IV, analgesia, urgent surgical consultation (laparoscopic repair); CT abdomen if stable and diagnosis unclear; Boey score/risk assessment.

**Lessons.** Request an erect chest X-ray (or CT) for suspected perforation.

### Case 7: Lines in the wrong place

**Presentation.** Ms G., 80, intubated after stroke, new nasogastric tube; the nurse wants to start feeds. Portable film: the NG tube passes down the right main bronchus and ends in the right lower zone; ETT tip 1 cm above the carina.

**Reasoning.** The NG tube is in the right lung, with risk of pneumonitis and pneumothorax if feed is instilled. ETT is low and risks right main bronchus intubation with left lung collapse on advancement.

**Action.** Withdraw and re-site the NG tube; confirm by radiograph (tip below the diaphragm, in the stomach) before any feed or drug; pull back the ETT 2 cm; recheck. Incident reporting per policy ("never event" if feed administered).

**Lessons.** No feeding until position is confirmed; read the tubes on every ICU film.

## Common pitfalls

1. **Satisfaction of search**: stopping after the first abnormality.
2. **Ignoring technique**: over/underpenetration, rotation, poor inspiration, AP supine film.
3. **Reading in isolation**: not comparing with previous films or clinical findings.
4. **Dismissing a normal film** in PE, early pneumonia, aortic injury, cancer.
5. **Missing review areas**: apices, retrocardiac region, hila, below the diaphragm, behind ribs.
6. **Waiting for imaging in tension pneumothorax.**
7. **Misdiagnosing skin folds as pneumothorax and vice versa.**
8. **Relying on AXR for perforation or undifferentiated pain.**
9. **Not confirming line/tube position** before use (NG feeds, central lines, ETT).
10. **Not repeating the film** for persistent consolidation in smokers.
11. **Ordering AXR** when CT or ultrasound is the better first test; radiation unawareness.
12. **Misreading a hiatus hernia, bullae or pleural thickening** as pathology/mass.
13. **Overcalling cardiomegaly** on AP/expiratory films.
14. **No urgent communication** of critical findings (pneumothorax, free gas, misplaced tube).

---

## Key points

- Use a fixed, systematic method and check technical quality first.
- The silhouette sign localises lesions; air bronchograms indicate airspace disease; volume loss distinguishes collapse from consolidation.
- Effusion (meniscus, blunting) vs pneumothorax (visceral pleural line, absent markings): ultrasound and CT refine; tension pneumothorax is a clinical emergency.
- Pulmonary oedema evolves from cephalisation to Kerley B lines to bat's-wing opacification.
- Persisting consolidation or a mass needs CT and cancer exclusion.
- Check lines and tubes on every film; never feed through an unconfirmed NG tube.
- AXR is limited: use it for obstruction, foreign body or monitoring; use erect chest film or CT for perforation; CT is preferred in most acute abdomen presentations.
- A normal plain film never overrides a strong clinical suspicion.

## Self-assessment

**Questions**

1. How do you judge adequate inspiration, rotation and penetration on a chest film?
2. What does the silhouette sign mean, and which lobe obscures the right heart border?
3. List the signs of lobar collapse and the Golden S sign.
4. How does a pleural effusion look on an erect and a supine film?
5. A patient has hypotension, tracheal deviation and absent breath sounds after a central line. What do you do?
6. Describe the radiographic stages of cardiogenic pulmonary oedema.
7. What are the features distinguishing small from large bowel on AXR, and the normal diameters?
8. What is the best plain film to demonstrate free intraperitoneal air?
9. Name three indications and three non-indications for AXR.
10. Where should the tip of an endotracheal tube and of a right IJ central line lie?

**Answers**

1. Inspiration: 5–6 anterior (9–10 posterior) ribs above the diaphragm; rotation: medial clavicular ends equidistant from the spinous processes; penetration: vertebral bodies just visible behind the heart.
2. Loss of an edge when two soft-tissue densities touch; right middle lobe consolidation obscures the right heart border (lingula for the left).
3. Fissure displacement, increased density, shift of hilum/trachea/mediastinum towards the collapse, raised hemidiaphragm, crowded ribs, compensatory hyperinflation; Golden S sign: right upper lobe collapse around a central mass, causing the reverse S-shaped fissure.
4. Erect: blunted costophrenic angle with meniscus; large effusion opacifies the hemithorax and pushes the mediastinum away; supine: generalised haze over the lower zones without a meniscus.
5. Tension pneumothorax: immediate needle decompression then chest drain, no imaging first; oxygen, then confirm with a film.
6. Cardiomegaly with upper lobe venous diversion → interstitial oedema (Kerley B lines, peribronchial cuffing, perihilar haze) → alveolar oedema (bat's-wing opacities, air bronchograms, effusions).
7. Small bowel: central, diameter < 3 cm, valvulae conniventes cross the lumen; large bowel: peripheral, < 6 cm (caecum < 9 cm), haustra don't cross the whole width.
8. Erect chest X-ray (or left lateral decubitus film); CT is more sensitive.
9. Indications: suspected obstruction, toxic megacolon follow-up, foreign body/packers, stones (KUB) when other options unavailable, tube position. Non-indications: undifferentiated abdominal pain, suspected perforation/appendicitis/pancreatitis, routine screening.
10. ETT tip 3–5 cm above the carina; IJ central line tip at the SVC/right atrial junction (over the carina/right main bronchus), with no pneumothorax.
