# Part III: Physical Examination Skills

## Chapter 10. General Inspection

### Learning objectives

After studying this chapter you should be able to:

1. Perform and present a structured "end-of-the-bed" assessment in the first minute of an examination.
2. Recognise the signs of acute distress and the "unwell-looking" patient, and know when to stop and act.
3. Assess nutrition, hydration and body habitus, and interpret the limits of each sign.
4. Identify and distinguish pallor, jaundice, central and peripheral cyanosis, and clubbing.
5. Examine the hands and nails and relate findings to systemic disease.
6. Grade and interpret oedema by distribution and character.
7. Examine lymph nodes systematically and describe abnormal nodes.
8. Recognise characteristic facies and body odours that point towards diagnoses.

---

### 10.1 The value of looking

Careful inspection costs nothing, needs no equipment and often yields more diagnostic information per minute than any other part of the examination. Experienced clinicians form a firm impression of how ill a patient is within seconds of seeing them, and studies of "gestalt" assessment show that this impression, particularly in children and acutely unwell adults, predicts serious illness better than many individual signs.

The central rule of inspection is: **look before you touch, and look at the whole patient before you look at the part.** Many clinicians fail not through lack of knowledge but because they head straight for the stethoscope while the patient in front of them is cyanosed, drowsy, or in pain.

Principles:

1. **Wash your hands, introduce yourself, obtain consent, and ensure privacy and adequate exposure** (with dignity).
2. **Position the patient** appropriately: usually reclined at 45° for the general survey, with good light and the patient exposed as far as necessary.
3. **Stand at the end of the bed first** and observe for 30–60 seconds without speaking.
4. **Work from general to specific:** whole patient → hands → arms → face → neck → then the system of interest.
5. **Compare sides.**
6. **Describe, don't just label.** Record what you see ("3 cm firm, non-tender, mobile node in the right anterior cervical chain") before interpreting.

### 10.2 The first minute: the end-of-the-bed assessment

Before you speak, ask yourself:

| Domain | What to observe |
|---|---|
| **Overall impression** | Does the patient look well, unwell, or dying? Comfortable at rest? |
| **Consciousness and behaviour** | Alert, drowsy, agitated, confused? Interacting appropriately? |
| **Breathing** | Rate, effort, use of accessory muscles, ability to speak in full sentences, audible wheeze or stridor, pursed-lip breathing |
| **Colour** | Pale, flushed, jaundiced, cyanosed, mottled? |
| **Posture and position** | Sitting forward (tripod position in severe airway obstruction), lying still (peritonitis), restless and writhing (colic), curled up (meningism, abdominal pain), upright because of orthopnoea |
| **Hydration and nutrition** | Cachexia, obesity, dry mucosa, sunken eyes |
| **Skin** | Rash, sweating, bruising, scars |
| **Pain behaviour** | Grimacing, guarding, protecting a limb |
| **Surroundings** | Oxygen, drips, drains, sick bowls, inhalers, walking aids, sputum pot, medication, call bell, relatives |

The surroundings are often neglected, yet they carry information: a sputum pot with blood-stained sputum, a urinary catheter bag with dark urine, a walking frame, a medication box, a pile of flowers.

#### The "unwell-looking" patient

Some features should immediately override your plan for a leisurely examination and trigger an ABCDE assessment (Chapter 37):

- Laboured or noisy breathing, or inability to complete sentences.
- Cyanosis, mottled or grey skin, cold peripheries.
- Agitation, drowsiness or new confusion.
- Severe pain, rigidity or silence combined with pallor and sweating.
- Rapid deterioration reported by relatives or nurses.

Trust your first impression; verify it with measurements (Chapter 11), and escalate promptly if it is alarming.

### 10.3 Consciousness and mental state at a glance

Observe: Is the patient orientated? Do their answers make sense? Is speech slurred, slow, or pressured? Is there evidence of self-neglect or hallucination? Brief, structured tools such as AVPU (Alert, responds to Voice, responds to Pain, Unresponsive) or the Glasgow Coma Scale (Chapter 15, 33) quantify what you see. Detailed assessment follows in Chapters 15 and 20.

### 10.4 Nutritional status and body habitus

#### Undernutrition and cachexia

Look for temporal and supraclavicular wasting, prominent facial bones, loss of subcutaneous fat, muscle wasting (quadriceps, thenar eminence, interossei), loose-fitting clothes or belts, and peripheral oedema (which can mask weight loss).

- **Measure** weight and height, calculate **BMI** (kg/m²), and compare with the patient's previous weights. **Unintentional weight loss** of more than 5% in 6 months, or more than 10% over a longer period, is clinically significant.
- Use a validated screening tool (such as MUST or NRS-2002) in hospitalised and older patients.
- In children, plot on growth charts; mid-upper arm circumference (MUAC) is a quick screening tool in low-resource settings (Chapter 59).

Causes of weight loss include malignancy, chronic infection (tuberculosis, HIV), gastrointestinal disease (malabsorption, inflammatory bowel disease, dysphagia), endocrine disease (hyperthyroidism, diabetes, adrenal insufficiency), chronic organ failure (cardiac cachexia, COPD, renal), psychiatric illness (depression, eating disorders), and social or economic deprivation (Chapter 30).

#### Obesity

Record BMI and **waist circumference**; central fat distribution carries a higher metabolic risk. In some populations, risk thresholds for BMI are lower. Look for associated features: acanthosis nigricans (dark velvety skin in the axillae and neck: insulin resistance), striae (Cushing syndrome), hirsutism (polycystic ovary syndrome), neck circumference and crowded pharynx (obstructive sleep apnoea), and intertrigo.

#### Height and proportions

Short stature, tall stature, and unusual proportions (arm span greater than height in Marfan syndrome) may point towards endocrine or genetic conditions.

### 10.5 Hydration

Clinical assessment of hydration is imperfect, but combining several features is useful.

| Sign | Interpretation and caveats |
|---|---|
| Dry mucous membranes, dry tongue | Moderately sensitive, but mouth-breathing and oxygen therapy also dry the mouth |
| Reduced skin turgor | Reliable in children; **unreliable in older adults** because of loss of skin elasticity |
| Sunken eyes, sunken fontanelle (infants) | Suggests significant dehydration |
| Low JVP, flat neck veins when supine | Suggests hypovolaemia |
| Tachycardia, postural hypotension | Suggests volume depletion (or autonomic dysfunction, drugs) |
| Prolonged capillary refill (> 2–3 s) | Non-specific; affected by cold and age |
| Reduced urine output, dark urine | Suggests hypovolaemia (or acute kidney injury) |
| Axillary dryness | Less reliable but sometimes helpful |

**Postural (orthostatic) blood pressure** is a simple test: measure supine after 5 minutes' rest, then at 1 and 3 minutes standing. A fall in systolic blood pressure of 20 mmHg or more, or diastolic of 10 mmHg or more, defines orthostatic hypotension. A rise in pulse without a fall in pressure may indicate early volume depletion; a fall in pressure without a heart rate rise suggests autonomic failure or drugs (beta-blockers).

**Overhydration** is suggested by peripheral and sacral oedema, raised JVP, basal crackles, and ascites. Fluid status is best assessed by a combination of examination findings, weight trends, and fluid balance charts.

### 10.6 Pallor

**Pallor** is paleness of the skin or mucous membranes. It is most reliably assessed in the **conjunctivae** (pull down the lower eyelid), **tongue and oral mucosa**, **nail beds**, and **palmar creases** (stretch the hand gently; creases that are as pale as the surrounding skin suggest haemoglobin < 70–80 g/L).

Cautions:

- Skin pallor depends on skin pigmentation and on circulatory state: shock, cold and vasoconstriction cause pallor without anaemia. Conversely, a patient with anaemia can appear flushed after exertion or in a warm room.
- In dark-skinned patients, rely on mucous membranes, conjunctivae, and nail beds.
- Clinical assessment of pallor is only moderately accurate; a low-resource clinic may use it as a screening tool, but confirm with haemoglobin when possible.

Associated findings point to the cause: koilonychia (iron deficiency), glossitis and angular stomatitis (iron, B12, folate deficiency), jaundice and splenomegaly (haemolysis), petechiae and bruises (marrow failure), lymphadenopathy (lymphoma, leukaemia), bone tenderness (myeloma, leukaemia).

---

### 10.7 Jaundice

**Jaundice** is yellow discolouration of skin and mucosa due to raised serum bilirubin. It is best seen in **natural daylight** in the **sclerae** and under the tongue. It is clinically evident when serum bilirubin exceeds about 40 µmol/L (2.5 mg/dL).

- **Carotenaemia** (from large intakes of carrots or similar) yellows the skin, particularly the palms and soles, but **not the sclerae**.
- **Pseudojaundice** and some drugs (mepacrine) can also colour the skin.

Think in three anatomical groups:

| Type | Mechanism | Clues |
|---|---|---|
| **Pre-hepatic** (haemolytic) | Increased red cell breakdown | Mild jaundice, pallor, splenomegaly, normal urine and stool colour (urine contains no bilirubin: "acholuric") |
| **Hepatic** (hepatocellular) | Liver cell dysfunction (hepatitis, cirrhosis, drugs, alcohol) | Spider naevi, palmar erythema, ascites, flapping tremor, hepatomegaly or small liver, fever and anorexia in acute hepatitis |
| **Post-hepatic** (obstructive/cholestatic) | Blocked bile flow (gallstones, pancreatic or biliary cancer, strictures) | Dark urine, pale stools, pruritus, scratch marks; palpable gallbladder (Courvoisier's law) suggests malignant obstruction; fever and rigors with RUQ pain (cholangitis) |

Examine for stigmata of chronic liver disease (Chapter 14), and measure bilirubin fractions, liver enzymes and imaging as appropriate.

---

### 10.8 Cyanosis

**Cyanosis** is blue-purple discolouration of skin or mucosa due to increased concentration of **deoxygenated haemoglobin** (more than about 5 g/dL in capillary blood; or the presence of abnormal haemoglobin such as methaemoglobin).

#### Central cyanosis

Seen in the **tongue and lips (mucous membranes)** and in the warm peripheries. Reflects inadequate arterial oxygenation or abnormal haemoglobin.

Causes:

- Lung disease with hypoxaemia (severe pneumonia, COPD, pulmonary oedema, pulmonary embolism, interstitial lung disease).
- Right-to-left cardiac shunts (cyanotic congenital heart disease, Eisenmenger syndrome).
- High altitude.
- Methaemoglobinaemia or sulfhaemoglobinaemia (drugs, toxins; the oxygen saturation on pulse oximetry reads a misleading ~85%, and the blood is "chocolate brown").
- Hypoventilation (opioids, neuromuscular disease).

#### Peripheral cyanosis

Seen in the **fingers, toes, nose and ears** but **not** the tongue; the extremities are cold. Caused by slow blood flow through the capillaries with excessive oxygen extraction: cold exposure, heart failure, shock, peripheral vascular disease, Raynaud phenomenon.

#### Caveats

- Cyanosis is a **late and unreliable sign**. Anaemic patients may be severely hypoxic and not appear cyanosed (insufficient total haemoglobin), whereas polycythaemic patients may look cyanosed with only mild hypoxia.
- Poor lighting, skin pigmentation and make-up affect detection.
- Always measure oxygen saturation (and arterial blood gas when indicated); remember pulse oximetry's limitations (carbon monoxide poisoning gives falsely normal readings; cold, poor perfusion, and nail varnish affect them).

### 10.9 Hands and nails

The hands are a valuable window on systemic disease. A systematic hand examination takes only a minute:

**Inspect:** temperature, colour, sweat; nails; palmar creases; palmar erythema; skin; muscle bulk (thenar, hypothenar, interossei); joint swelling; deformity; tremor; tendon contractures.

#### Clubbing

**Definition.** Loss of the normal angle between nail and nail fold (the profile sign, > 180°), increased nail-fold fluctuance ("sponginess"), and eventually bulbous swelling of the fingertips.

**How to detect:**

1. Look from the side: normally there is an angle of about 160° between the nail and the nail fold; in clubbing it is lost.
2. **Schamroth's window test:** place the dorsal surfaces of the terminal phalanges of corresponding fingers of both hands together. Normally a diamond-shaped gap is visible; in clubbing it is obliterated.
3. Feel the nail bed for sponginess.

**Causes:**

| System | Examples |
|---|---|
| Respiratory | Lung cancer (non-small cell), bronchiectasis, lung abscess, empyema, pulmonary fibrosis, cystic fibrosis |
| Cardiac | Cyanotic congenital heart disease, infective endocarditis |
| Gastrointestinal | Inflammatory bowel disease (Crohn's disease more than ulcerative colitis), cirrhosis, coeliac disease |
| Other | Thyroid acropachy, familial/idiopathic |

Clubbing that develops over weeks in an adult, particularly with pain in the wrists and ankles (hypertrophic pulmonary osteoarthropathy), warrants a search for lung cancer. Lone clubbing without systemic disease is often familial.

#### Other nail signs

| Sign | Description | Suggests |
|---|---|---|
| **Koilonychia** | Spoon-shaped nails | Iron deficiency (also trauma, some skin disease) |
| **Leukonychia** | White nails (total or partial) | Hypoalbuminaemia, cirrhosis, kidney disease, chemotherapy; small white spots are usually trauma |
| **Terry's nails** | Mostly white with a narrow pink band at the tip | Cirrhosis, heart failure, ageing |
| **Muehrcke's lines** | Paired transverse white bands | Hypoalbuminaemia |
| **Beau's lines** | Transverse grooves | Severe systemic illness, chemotherapy, in the weeks preceding |
| **Splinter haemorrhages** | Thin longitudinal dark lines under the nail | Trauma (common), infective endocarditis, vasculitis, severe illness |
| **Onycholysis** | Separation of nail from bed | Psoriasis, fungal infection, thyrotoxicosis, trauma |
| **Pitting** | Small pits in the nail plate | Psoriasis, alopecia areata, eczema |
| **Yellow nails** | Thickened, yellow, slow-growing | Chronic respiratory disease, lymphoedema ("yellow nail syndrome"), fungal infection |
| **Nail fold infarcts, periungual erythema** | | Vasculitis, connective tissue disease |
| **Tar staining** | Yellow-brown discolouration of fingers | Smoking |
| **Azure lunulae** | Blue lunulae | Wilson disease, drugs |

#### Palms and other hand signs

- **Palmar erythema**: liver disease, pregnancy, thyrotoxicosis, rheumatoid arthritis, polycythaemia.
- **Dupuytren's contracture**: thickening and contracture of the palmar fascia (ring finger most often); associated with alcohol, diabetes, smoking, and family history.
- **Pallor of palmar creases**: anaemia.
- **Flapping tremor (asterixis)**: ask the patient to extend arms with hands dorsiflexed for 30 seconds. A sudden loss of tone causing irregular flapping suggests hepatic encephalopathy, uraemia, hypercapnia, or heart failure.
- **Fine tremor**: thyrotoxicosis, anxiety, beta-agonists, alcohol withdrawal.
- **Coarse tremor and resting tremor**: Parkinson disease (Chapter 15).
- **Janeway lesions** (painless macules on palms/soles) and **Osler's nodes** (painful nodules on pulp of digits): infective endocarditis.
- **Heberden's and Bouchard's nodes, deformities**: osteoarthritis and rheumatoid arthritis (Chapter 16).
- **Cold, clammy, mottled hands**: shock.
- **Thenar wasting**: median nerve compression (carpal tunnel).
- **Small hand muscle wasting**: ulnar nerve, T1 lesions, motor neurone disease.
- **Xanthomata** (tendon deposits): hyperlipidaemia.

### 10.10 Oedema

**Oedema** is a palpable swelling due to excess interstitial fluid.

#### Examination

- Inspect for swelling, skin changes (shiny, tight, discoloured, ulceration).
- **Pitting test:** press firmly with a thumb over a bony surface (the medial malleolus, shin, or sacrum) for 5–10 seconds. Pitting that remains after release indicates pitting oedema.
- Grade: 1+ (mild, barely perceptible, disappears quickly) to 4+ (deep pitting, persistent, with obvious swelling). Avoid over-precise grading; **record the extent** ("pitting oedema to mid-shin bilaterally").
- Note whether it is **unilateral or bilateral, symmetrical or asymmetrical, pitting or non-pitting, tender or painless**, and distribution (feet, legs, sacral, scrotal, facial, generalised, anasarca).
- Measure calf circumference 10 cm below the tibial tuberosity if you suspect deep vein thrombosis.

#### Interpretation by pattern

| Pattern | Possible causes |
|---|---|
| **Bilateral pitting leg oedema** | Heart failure (with raised JVP), venous insufficiency, nephrotic syndrome, cirrhosis/hypoalbuminaemia, drugs (amlodipine, NSAIDs, steroids, pioglitazone), pregnancy, prolonged sitting, hypothyroidism (non-pitting more characteristic) |
| **Unilateral leg swelling** | Deep vein thrombosis, cellulitis, ruptured Baker's cyst, local venous obstruction (tumour), lymphoedema, trauma |
| **Periorbital and facial oedema** (especially in the morning) | Nephrotic or nephritic syndrome, allergic reaction/angioedema, SVC obstruction, hypothyroidism |
| **Sacral oedema** | Bedbound patients with heart failure or hypoalbuminaemia |
| **Generalised oedema (anasarca)** | Severe heart failure, nephrotic syndrome, severe hypoalbuminaemia, liver failure |
| **Non-pitting oedema** | Lymphoedema (Stemmer sign: inability to pinch the skin at the base of the second toe), myxoedema, lipoedema |
| **Arm swelling with facial plethora and distended neck veins** | Superior vena cava obstruction |

Associated signs are decisive: raised JVP and basal crackles (heart failure); ascites and spider naevi (liver disease); frothy urine and periorbital swelling (nephrotic syndrome); warm, red, tender skin (cellulitis).

### 10.11 Lymph nodes

Lymph nodes are found throughout the body, and examination of the accessible groups is part of general assessment, essential in suspected infection, lymphoma, leukaemia, metastatic cancer and unexplained fever or weight loss.

#### Technique

1. Expose adequately; ensure the patient is relaxed.
2. Stand **behind** the patient for the neck (or in front, depending on preference), and use the pads of the second to fourth fingers in small, **circular, gentle motions**, feeling against underlying tissue.
3. Examine **bilaterally and systematically:**
   - **Head and neck chain:** occipital, post-auricular, pre-auricular, tonsillar (jugulodigastric), submandibular, submental, anterior cervical (along sternomastoid), posterior cervical (behind sternomastoid), supraclavicular.
   - **Axillae:** support the patient's arm and examine the central, anterior (pectoral), posterior (subscapular), lateral and apical groups.
   - **Epitrochlear** (above the medial epicondyle).
   - **Inguinal:** horizontal (upper) and vertical (lower) groups.
   - **Popliteal** (if indicated).
4. Examine for **hepatosplenomegaly** when generalised lymphadenopathy is found.

#### Describe any node found

**Site, size (in cm), number, consistency (soft, rubbery, firm, hard), tenderness, mobility (mobile or fixed to skin or deeper structures), matting, overlying skin changes** (erythema, warmth, ulceration, sinus).

#### Interpretation

| Feature | Suggests |
|---|---|
| Small (< 1 cm), soft, mobile, non-tender, "shotty" | Often normal or reactive; common in neck, groin |
| Tender, warm, recent onset | Infection or inflammation drained by that node |
| Rubbery, discrete, non-tender, in neck or axilla | Lymphoma (Hodgkin) possible |
| Hard, irregular, fixed, non-tender | Metastatic carcinoma |
| Matted nodes | Tuberculosis, lymphoma, metastases |
| Generalised lymphadenopathy | Infectious mononucleosis, HIV, lymphoma, leukaemia, sarcoidosis, SLE, drugs (phenytoin), secondary syphilis, TB |
| **Left supraclavicular (Virchow's) node** | Intra-abdominal malignancy (e.g. gastric, pancreatic); Troisier's sign |
| Right supraclavicular node | Mediastinal or lung/oesophageal disease |
| Epitrochlear nodes | Infection of the hand/arm, lymphoma, sarcoid, secondary syphilis |

Nodes **larger than about 1–2 cm**, persistent for **more than 4–6 weeks**, hard, fixed, or associated with fever, night sweats, weight loss, hepatosplenomegaly or supraclavicular location warrant further investigation (ultrasound, imaging, excisional or core biopsy). **Avoid fine-needle aspiration** as the first step if lymphoma is suspected, because tissue architecture matters; discuss with a specialist.

Always look for the **drainage area** for a local source of infection or malignancy (scalp, ears, throat and teeth for cervical nodes; breast, arm and hand for axillary nodes; legs, genitalia, perineum and anus for inguinal nodes).

### 10.12 Characteristic facies and odours

Some diagnoses are made from across the room:

| Facies | Features | Condition |
|---|---|---|
| **Cushingoid** | Moon face, plethora, acne, hirsutism, supraclavicular fat, thin skin, bruising | Cushing syndrome (endogenous or steroid-induced) |
| **Acromegalic** | Coarse features, prominent brow and jaw, large nose, thick lips, large hands | Acromegaly |
| **Hypothyroid** | Puffy face, periorbital oedema, dry coarse skin and hair, loss of the outer third of the eyebrows, slow movements | Hypothyroidism |
| **Hyperthyroid** | Stare, lid retraction, lid lag, exophthalmos, sweating, tremor | Thyrotoxicosis, Graves disease |
| **Mitral facies** | Malar flush with dusky cheeks | Mitral stenosis, low cardiac output |
| **Parkinsonian (masked)** | Expressionless, reduced blinking | Parkinson disease |
| **Myopathic** | Ptosis, expressionless, inability to smile fully | Myotonic dystrophy, myasthenia, facioscapular disease |
| **Nephrotic/nephritic** | Puffy periorbital oedema | Nephrotic syndrome, glomerulonephritis |
| **Butterfly rash** | Erythema over cheeks and nasal bridge, sparing the nasolabial folds | Systemic lupus erythematosus |
| **Facial plethora with distended neck veins** | | SVC obstruction, polycythaemia |
| **Down syndrome facies** | Upslanting palpebral fissures, epicanthic folds, flat nasal bridge | Trisomy 21 |
| **Facial asymmetry** | Droop with forehead sparing/non-sparing | Stroke (UMN) vs Bell palsy (LMN) |

**Odours** (breath and body) are an under-used source of information:

- **Fruity/acetone** breath: diabetic ketoacidosis, starvation ketosis.
- **Ammoniacal/urinous (uraemic fetor):** advanced renal failure.
- **Musty, sweetish (fetor hepaticus):** liver failure.
- **Alcohol:** intoxication (also consider other causes of confusion; do not assume).
- **Foul sputum or breath:** lung abscess, bronchiectasis, anaerobic infections, dental disease.
- **Faecal smell on breath:** bowel obstruction.
- **Garlic:** organophosphate or arsenic poisoning.
- **Bitter almonds:** cyanide (not everyone can smell it).

### 10.13 Worked cases

#### Case 1: The first minute

*A 63-year-old man is brought to the emergency department by his daughter, who says that he has been "not himself" for two days. From the end of the bed you see that he is sitting upright, breathing quickly with pursed lips, speaking in short phrases, with a dusky blue tinge to his lips. A sputum pot at his side contains yellow-green sputum. He is wearing a medic-alert bracelet reading "COPD" and holds a blue inhaler.*

**Reasoning.** The end-of-bed assessment already gives the working diagnosis (exacerbation of COPD, possibly with pneumonia), signals severity (cyanosis, short phrases, pursed-lip breathing), and tells you what to do first: **stop, assess ABCDE, give controlled oxygen with a target saturation of 88–92%, nebulised bronchodilators, and obtain a blood gas** before launching into a full systematic examination. Hands may show tar staining and a flapping tremor (CO₂ retention).

#### Case 2: Clubbing and weight loss

*A 67-year-old man, a former shipyard worker and smoker for 40 years, reports 4 months of cough, weight loss of 7 kg, and aching in the wrists. Examination: cachexia, finger clubbing with loss of the nail-fold angle and a positive Schamroth sign, tar-stained fingers, a left supraclavicular node 2 cm in size, hard and fixed.*

**Reasoning.** Clubbing of recent onset with wrist pain suggests hypertrophic pulmonary osteoarthropathy and underlying lung cancer. The hard, fixed left supraclavicular node (Virchow-type) suggests metastatic spread. Asbestos exposure also raises the possibility of mesothelioma. Plan: chest X-ray and CT thorax/abdomen, biopsy of the node (accessible tissue gives a diagnosis and stage with minimal morbidity), urgent referral to the lung cancer pathway.

**Lesson.** Inspection of hands and neck, performed in two minutes, steers the whole investigation.

#### Case 3: Jaundice with a palpable gallbladder

*A 72-year-old woman presents with 3 weeks of painless yellowing of the skin, dark urine, pale stools, itching and 6 kg weight loss. She is deeply jaundiced with scratch marks on the arms. On abdominal examination, there is a smooth, non-tender, pear-shaped mass in the right upper quadrant moving with respiration.*

**Reasoning.** Jaundice with dark urine, pale stools and pruritus indicates obstructive (post-hepatic) jaundice. A palpable, non-tender gallbladder in a jaundiced patient suggests that the obstruction is not due to gallstones, because chronic stone disease usually leaves a fibrotic, non-distensible gallbladder (**Courvoisier's law**, which is a guide with exceptions). Weight loss increases the suspicion of **carcinoma of the head of the pancreas** or a distal biliary or ampullary tumour. Plan: liver function tests, clotting, ultrasound followed by CT pancreas protocol, and referral to a hepatobiliary team.

**Lesson.** Look at the skin, the scratch marks, the urine; then palpate. The combination is a pattern, and the pattern guides the work-up.

#### Case 4: Unilateral leg swelling

*A 48-year-old woman returns from a 14-hour flight and notices that her left leg is swollen and aching. She is on oral contraceptives. Examination: left calf circumference 3.5 cm greater than the right, pitting oedema to the ankle, tenderness along the deep veins of the calf, dilated superficial veins. Pulse 94, SpO₂ 98%, no chest pain or breathlessness.*

**Reasoning.** Unilateral swelling with a clear risk profile (flight, hormone therapy) is deep vein thrombosis until proved otherwise. Apply the Wells score for DVT (Chapter 70): calf swelling > 3 cm, pitting oedema, collateral superficial veins, tenderness along deep veins put her in the "likely" category; proceed directly to compression ultrasound (or empirical anticoagulation if imaging will be delayed, per local policy). Consider cellulitis, ruptured Baker's cyst, and muscle injury, but do not wait for them to declare themselves.

#### Case 5: Generalised lymphadenopathy

*A 22-year-old student presents with a sore throat, fatigue and fever for 10 days. Examination: exudative tonsillitis, tender bilateral posterior cervical lymphadenopathy, small axillary and inguinal nodes, a palpable spleen tip and a faint macular rash after taking amoxicillin.*

**Reasoning.** The generalised lymphadenopathy, splenomegaly and rash after amoxicillin strongly suggest **infectious mononucleosis (Epstein–Barr virus)**. Confirm with a full blood count and film (atypical lymphocytes) and heterophile antibody or EBV serology. Advise to avoid contact sports for several weeks because of the risk of splenic rupture. The alternative diagnoses to keep in mind are HIV seroconversion, cytomegalovirus, toxoplasmosis, and, if the nodes persist, lymphoma.

#### Case 6: Subtle clues

*A 55-year-old woman is referred for "chronic fatigue." A look at her face shows puffiness around the eyes, thin outer eyebrows and dry hair. She speaks slowly, and the handshake reveals cold, dry hands. There is non-pitting swelling of the lower legs.*

**Reasoning.** The facies and associated features suggest **hypothyroidism**. Check TSH and free T4; consider anaemia, hypercholesterolaemia and hyponatraemia. Ask about weight gain, constipation, cold intolerance, menstrual changes, and depression.

### 10.14 Common pitfalls

- **Going straight to the stethoscope** without looking at the patient.
- **Missing the patient's surroundings,** which contain clues.
- **Over-relying on skin turgor** in older adults.
- **Relying on cyanosis** to detect hypoxia (late, unreliable; measure saturation).
- **Dismissing small nodes** in the supraclavicular fossa.
- **Failing to examine both hands, both sides, or all lymph node groups.**
- **Describing nodes without measurement and consistency.**
- **Labelling bilateral leg swelling "heart failure"** without checking JVP, lungs, and alternative causes.
- **Missing facial and odour clues.**
- **Assuming the smell of alcohol explains a patient's confusion.**

---

### 10.15 Key points

- Observe the patient for a full minute before touching: general appearance, breathing, colour, behaviour, surroundings.
- Recognise the unwell-looking patient and act first, examine later.
- Hydration is assessed by combining signs; skin turgor is unreliable in the elderly.
- Pallor, jaundice, cyanosis and clubbing each have defined causes and caveats; confirm with measurements.
- The hands and nails reveal systemic disease: clubbing, koilonychia, splinter haemorrhages, palmar erythema, flapping tremor.
- Classify oedema by distribution (unilateral vs bilateral), character (pitting vs non-pitting) and associated signs.
- Examine lymph nodes systematically, describe them fully, and examine their drainage areas.
- Facies and odours provide quick diagnostic hypotheses.

### 10.16 Self-assessment

1. What are the causes of unilateral leg swelling that you must not miss?
   *Answer: Deep vein thrombosis and cellulitis (and venous obstruction by tumour); also ruptured Baker's cyst and lymphoedema.*

2. Distinguish central from peripheral cyanosis.
   *Answer: Central: tongue and lips are blue, extremities warm; arterial hypoxaemia or abnormal haemoglobin. Peripheral: tongue pink, cold extremities; slow peripheral flow.*

3. Describe Schamroth's window test.
   *Answer: Place the dorsal surfaces of the terminal phalanges of corresponding fingers together; the normal diamond-shaped gap is lost in clubbing.*

4. A woman has jaundice, a palpable non-tender gallbladder and weight loss. What does Courvoisier's law suggest?
   *Answer: A palpable gallbladder with painless jaundice suggests extrahepatic obstruction by something other than gallstones, most notably pancreatic or biliary malignancy.*

5. Name four causes of generalised lymphadenopathy.
   *Answer (any four): infectious mononucleosis, HIV, lymphoma, leukaemia, sarcoidosis, TB, SLE, secondary syphilis, drug reaction.*

6. Why is a normal oxygen saturation by pulse oximetry sometimes falsely reassuring?
   *Answer: Carbon monoxide poisoning produces falsely normal readings; poor perfusion and nail varnish cause errors; it does not detect hypercapnia.*
