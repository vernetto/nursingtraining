## Chapter 35. Bleeding, Bruising and Anemia

### Learning objectives

After studying this chapter you should be able to:

1. Define anaemia, recognise its symptoms and signs, and classify it by red cell size (microcytic, normocytic, macrocytic) and by mechanism (reduced production, increased destruction, blood loss).
2. Use a stepwise approach (full blood count, indices, reticulocyte count, film, ferritin, B12, folate, haemolysis tests) to find the cause of anaemia.
3. Diagnose and manage iron deficiency, B12 and folate deficiency, anaemia of chronic disease, thalassaemia, sickle cell disease, and haemolytic anaemias.
4. Take a structured bleeding history and distinguish primary haemostatic disorders (platelet and vessel) from coagulation disorders.
5. Interpret the platelet count, PT/INR, aPTT, fibrinogen and thrombin time, and know when to order von Willebrand and factor assays.
6. Recognise and manage emergencies in haematology: TTP, HIT, DIC, severe thrombocytopenia, major bleeding on anticoagulants, and acute haemolysis, aplastic anaemia and acute leukaemia.
7. Apply transfusion thresholds, reversal strategies, and principles of safe practice, and recognise non-accidental injury when bruising is unexplained in children.

# Part A: Anaemia

### 35.1 Definition and clinical features

**Anaemia** is a reduction in the haemoglobin (Hb) concentration (and red cell mass) below the reference range for age, sex, pregnancy and altitude. **WHO thresholds:** Hb <130 g/L in adult men, <120 g/L in non-pregnant women, <110 g/L in pregnancy and in children 6–59 months (other age groups differ). Reference ranges differ between laboratories, populations and ethnic groups; interpret in context and trend over time.

Anaemia is a **sign,** and almost always has a cause that needs to be found (Chapter 2: "iron-deficiency anaemia is a sign, not a diagnosis").

#### Symptoms

Depend on **severity, speed of onset, age and cardiopulmonary reserve:**

- **Mild or slowly developing:** often asymptomatic, or non-specific fatigue.
- **Moderate to severe:** fatigue, weakness, exertional breathlessness, palpitations, headache, dizziness, poor concentration, tinnitus, reduced exercise tolerance, cold intolerance, ankle swelling, angina or heart failure in those with cardiac disease, claudication in vascular disease, reduced libido, pica (iron deficiency: craving ice, clay), restless legs.
- **Acute blood loss:** haemodynamic compromise (tachycardia, hypotension, syncope) before the Hb falls.

#### Signs

- **Pallor** (conjunctivae, palmar creases, mucous membranes; unreliable for mild anaemia); tachycardia, flow murmur, bounding pulse, cardiac enlargement or heart failure; postural hypotension.
- **Specific clues:** koilonychia, angular stomatitis, glossitis, brittle hair (iron deficiency); **jaundice and splenomegaly** (haemolysis); lemon-yellow tint, smooth red tongue, neurological signs (B12); bone pain, expansion of facial bones (thalassaemia major); leg ulcers (sickle cell, hereditary spherocytosis); lymphadenopathy, hepatosplenomegaly (leukaemia, lymphoma); bruising and petechiae (marrow failure, leukaemia).

### 35.2 Classification and approach

#### By mechanism

1. **Decreased production** (hypoproliferative): iron deficiency, B12 or folate deficiency, anaemia of chronic disease/inflammation, chronic kidney disease (reduced erythropoietin), endocrine (hypothyroidism, hypopituitarism, hypogonadism), marrow failure (aplastic anaemia, pure red cell aplasia), marrow infiltration (leukaemia, lymphoma, myeloma, metastases, myelofibrosis), myelodysplastic syndromes, drugs and toxins, haemoglobinopathies (thalassaemia: ineffective erythropoiesis).
2. **Increased destruction** (haemolysis): intrinsic (membrane, enzyme, haemoglobin defects) or extrinsic (immune, mechanical, infectious, toxic, hypersplenism).
3. **Blood loss:** acute or chronic (gastrointestinal, uterine, urinary, trauma, surgery, frequent phlebotomy).
4. **Dilutional:** pregnancy, fluid overload, IV fluids, splenomegaly (pooling).

#### By red cell size (MCV)

| MCV | Category | Causes |
|---|---|---|
| **<80 fL** | **Microcytic** | **Iron deficiency** (commonest), **thalassaemia trait,** anaemia of chronic disease (may be microcytic, usually normocytic), sideroblastic anaemia, lead poisoning |
| **80–100 fL** | **Normocytic** | **Anaemia of chronic disease/inflammation, chronic kidney disease,** acute blood loss, haemolysis, marrow failure or infiltration, early or mixed deficiency (iron + B12), hypothyroidism, pregnancy |
| **>100 fL** | **Macrocytic** | **B12 or folate deficiency (megaloblastic),** alcohol, liver disease, hypothyroidism, drugs (methotrexate, hydroxycarbamide, azathioprine, zidovudine, trimethoprim, anticonvulsants), myelodysplastic syndrome, reticulocytosis (brisk haemolysis or bleeding), aplastic anaemia, smoking, haemoglobin variants |

Remember that **mixed pictures** are frequent (iron deficiency with B12 deficiency can give a normal MCV, and a high RDW).

#### Initial investigations

| Test | Purpose |
|---|---|
| **Full blood count with indices** (Hb, MCV, MCH, MCHC, RDW, white cell and platelet counts) | Size, number; pancytopenia suggests marrow failure |
| **Reticulocyte count** (absolute or index) | Marrow response: **low** (production defect), **high** (haemolysis, bleeding, recovery after treatment) |
| **Blood film** | Morphology: hypochromic microcytes, pencil cells (iron deficiency), target cells (thalassaemia, liver disease, post-splenectomy), spherocytes (hereditary spherocytosis, AIHA), schistocytes (microangiopathic haemolysis), sickle cells, macro-ovalocytes and hypersegmented neutrophils (megaloblastic), blasts (leukaemia), rouleaux (myeloma, inflammation), leucoerythroblastic picture (marrow infiltration), Howell–Jolly bodies (hyposplenism), parasites (malaria), Heinz bodies (G6PD deficiency, bite cells) |
| **Ferritin, transferrin saturation, iron, TIBC** | Iron status: **ferritin <30 µg/L** (some use <45) indicates iron deficiency in the absence of inflammation; ferritin is an acute-phase protein: in inflammation, **ferritin up to 100 µg/L** with transferrin saturation <20% may still indicate coexisting iron deficiency |
| **Vitamin B12, folate** (serum or red cell folate); **methylmalonic acid and homocysteine** if borderline | Deficiency |
| **U&E/creatinine, LFTs, TSH, CRP/ESR** | CKD, liver disease, thyroid disease, inflammation |
| **Haemolysis screen:** bilirubin (unconjugated), LDH, haptoglobin, reticulocytes, direct antiglobulin test (DAT/Coombs), film, urinary haemosiderin | Haemolysis |
| **Haemoglobin electrophoresis or HPLC** | Thalassaemia, sickle cell disease, haemoglobin variants |
| **G6PD assay** (after haemolytic episode has resolved) | G6PD deficiency |
| **Coeliac serology, H. pylori testing** | Iron deficiency causes |
| **Faecal immunochemical test, endoscopy** | GI blood loss |
| **Bone marrow aspirate and biopsy** | Unexplained cytopenias, suspected malignancy, aplasia, MDS |
| **Serum protein electrophoresis/free light chains** | Myeloma |
| **Erythropoietin level, renal function** | CKD anaemia |
| **Pregnancy test, menstrual history** | |

### 35.3 Microcytic anaemia

#### Iron deficiency anaemia

**The commonest anaemia worldwide.**

- **Causes:**
  - **Blood loss (most important in developed countries):** GI (peptic ulcer, NSAIDs, gastritis, angiodysplasia, oesophagitis, **colorectal cancer, gastric cancer,** polyps, coeliac disease, inflammatory bowel disease, haemorrhoids), **menstruation (menorrhagia),** haematuria, frequent blood donation, hookworm infestation (tropical regions), schistosomiasis.
  - **Increased demand:** pregnancy, lactation, growth (infants, adolescents).
  - **Reduced intake:** vegetarian or poor diet (alone rarely sufficient in adult men and postmenopausal women).
  - **Malabsorption:** coeliac disease, atrophic gastritis, ***H. pylori,* bariatric surgery,** gastrectomy, PPIs (reduced acid), IBD.
- **Features:** fatigue, pallor, pica, hair loss, brittle nails, **koilonychia,** angular stomatitis, glossitis, dysphagia (Plummer–Vinson syndrome), restless legs; low MCV, MCH, raised RDW, pencil cells; low ferritin, high TIBC, low transferrin saturation.
- **Investigation (principle: find the source):**
  - **Men and postmenopausal women:** **GI evaluation** (upper endoscopy with duodenal biopsy for coeliac disease; colonoscopy) unless there is a clear alternative cause; consider capsule endoscopy if negative; test for coeliac disease.
  - **Premenopausal women:** menstrual history; coeliac serology; consider GI investigation if GI symptoms, family history, age >50 or treatment failure.
  - **Children:** diet, malabsorption, lead exposure, occult blood loss.
  - **Check for urinary blood loss.**
- **Treatment:**
  - **Oral iron** (ferrous sulfate 200 mg once daily or **every other day,** which improves absorption and tolerability; ferrous fumarate, gluconate): expect reticulocytosis in 1 week, **Hb rise of about 10–20 g/L in 2–4 weeks;** continue for **3 months after normalisation** to replenish stores. Side effects: constipation, nausea, black stool. Take with vitamin C (or on an empty stomach) and avoid tea, coffee, calcium, and antacids.
  - **IV iron** (ferric carboxymaltose, ferric derisomaltose, iron sucrose): intolerance or failure of oral iron, malabsorption, ongoing blood loss, CKD, heart failure, inflammatory bowel disease, late pregnancy, preoperative; monitor for hypophosphataemia (ferric carboxymaltose) and rare anaphylactoid reactions.
  - **Treat the cause.**
  - **Blood transfusion** only for severe symptomatic anaemia, haemodynamic instability, or ongoing major bleeding.
  - **Failure to respond:** non-adherence, ongoing loss, malabsorption, wrong diagnosis (thalassaemia trait, anaemia of chronic disease), combined deficiency.

#### Thalassaemia

- Inherited reduced globin chain synthesis: **α-thalassaemia** (deletions of α-globin genes; South-East Asia, Mediterranean, Africa) and **β-thalassaemia** (β-globin mutations; Mediterranean, Middle East, South Asia, Africa, South-East Asia).
- **Trait (carrier) states:** mild microcytic anaemia with **disproportionately low MCV and raised or normal red cell count** and normal or high ferritin; **Mentzer index** (MCV ÷ RBC count) **<13 favours thalassaemia trait; >13 favours iron deficiency.** β-thalassaemia trait: raised **HbA₂** (>3.5%) on electrophoresis. α-thalassaemia trait: normal HbA₂ (needs DNA testing); HbH disease: moderate haemolytic anaemia, splenomegaly.
- **β-thalassaemia major (transfusion-dependent):** severe anaemia from 6–12 months, failure to thrive, hepatosplenomegaly, bone expansion, iron overload from transfusions (heart, liver, endocrine) managed with chelation; curative options: stem-cell transplant, gene therapy.
- **Importance:** **do not treat thalassaemia trait with iron,** and offer **genetic counselling** (and partner testing) in at-risk couples and in pregnancy.

#### Anaemia of chronic disease / inflammation

- Chronic infection, inflammatory disease, malignancy, CKD, heart failure; **hepcidin-driven iron sequestration;** normocytic (or mildly microcytic), low serum iron, **low TIBC,** **normal or raised ferritin,** reduced transferrin saturation, raised CRP. Treat the underlying condition; iron if coexisting deficiency (ferritin <100 µg/L with TSAT <20% in chronic disease); erythropoiesis-stimulating agents in CKD and selected patients.

#### Others

- **Sideroblastic anaemia:** ring sideroblasts in marrow; causes: hereditary, myelodysplasia, alcohol, lead, drugs (isoniazid, linezolid), copper deficiency; pyridoxine trial.
- **Lead poisoning:** basophilic stippling, abdominal pain, neuropathy, children with pica or old paint exposure, adults in battery, ceramics, folk remedies.

### 35.4 Macrocytic anaemia

#### Megaloblastic anaemia (B12 and folate deficiency)

Impaired DNA synthesis causes large, immature red cells (macro-ovalocytes), hypersegmented neutrophils, pancytopenia in severe cases, raised LDH and indirect bilirubin (ineffective erythropoiesis), and a hypercellular marrow with megaloblasts.

**Vitamin B12 deficiency:**

- **Causes:**
  - **Pernicious anaemia** (autoimmune gastritis with intrinsic factor deficiency; anti-intrinsic factor and anti-parietal cell antibodies; associated with other autoimmune disease, northern European descent, increased gastric cancer risk).
  - **Dietary:** strict vegan or vegetarian diets, older adults, malnutrition.
  - **Gastric:** gastrectomy, bariatric surgery, atrophic gastritis, *H. pylori.*
  - **Ileal:** Crohn's disease, ileal resection, *Diphyllobothrium latum* (fish tapeworm), bacterial overgrowth.
  - **Drugs:** metformin (long-term), PPIs and H₂ blockers (reduce release from food), nitrous oxide misuse (functional B12 inactivation).
  - **Pancreatic insufficiency; transcobalamin deficiency; congenital.**
- **Clinical features:** anaemia symptoms, glossitis, mild jaundice, weight loss, **neurological** (symmetrical paraesthesia, loss of vibration and proprioception, **subacute combined degeneration of the cord** (posterior and lateral columns: sensory ataxia, spastic weakness), optic neuropathy, cognitive impairment, depression, psychosis ("megaloblastic madness")). **Neurological damage can occur without anaemia** and can be irreversible if treatment is delayed.
- **Diagnosis:** low serum B12 (<150–200 pg/mL; borderline levels 200–300 may need functional tests: raised methylmalonic acid and homocysteine), **anti-intrinsic factor antibodies,** anti-parietal cell antibodies; endoscopy if pernicious anaemia confirmed or other concerns.
- **Treatment:** **intramuscular hydroxocobalamin** (e.g. 1 mg on alternate days for 2 weeks or until no further improvement, then every 3 months for life in pernicious anaemia; higher frequency if neurological involvement: 1 mg on alternate days until no further improvement, then every 2 months); high-dose oral B12 (1–2 mg daily) is effective in dietary deficiency and many malabsorption states. Monitor potassium early in severe anaemia (hypokalaemia on starting treatment), reticulocyte response in 3–5 days. **Never give folic acid alone to a patient with B12 deficiency** (may correct the anaemia and precipitate or worsen neurological damage). In severe symptomatic anaemia with heart failure, transfuse cautiously.

**Folate deficiency:**

- **Causes:** poor diet (alcohol misuse, elderly, malnutrition), **increased requirement** (pregnancy, haemolysis, exfoliative skin disease, malignancy), **malabsorption** (coeliac disease, tropical sprue, Crohn's), **drugs** (methotrexate, trimethoprim, phenytoin, sulfasalazine, alcohol).
- **Diagnosis:** low serum or red cell folate (red cell folate is more reliable), with B12 normal.
- **Treatment:** folic acid 5 mg daily for 4 months (after confirming B12 is replete or co-administering B12); address cause; **periconceptional folic acid** (400 µg, or 5 mg in high-risk women, e.g. anticonvulsant use, previous neural tube defect, diabetes, obesity) reduces the risk of neural tube defects.

#### Non-megaloblastic macrocytosis

**Alcohol** (very common: MCV raised without anaemia, GGT, AST:ALT ratio), **liver disease, hypothyroidism, myelodysplastic syndrome, reticulocytosis, drugs, smoking, aplastic anaemia, pregnancy, benign familial macrocytosis.** MDS should be considered in an older adult with unexplained macrocytic anaemia (with or without neutropenia/thrombocytopenia); bone marrow and cytogenetics.

### 35.5 Normocytic anaemia

- **Acute blood loss:** haemorrhage from GI tract, trauma, surgery, obstetric causes, aortic rupture, ectopic pregnancy; Hb falls later than volume depletion (Chapter 27).
- **Anaemia of chronic disease / CKD** (above); CKD anaemia is due to erythropoietin deficiency, iron deficiency, uraemic inhibitors; treat with iron and ESAs when Hb <100 g/L with caution (target Hb 100–115 g/L).
- **Haemolytic anaemias** (35.6).
- **Marrow failure and infiltration** (35.7).
- **Endocrine:** hypothyroidism, hypopituitarism, Addison disease.
- **Combined deficiency** (iron + B12/folate; coeliac disease).
- **Pregnancy** (dilutional and deficiency).

### 35.6 Haemolytic anaemias

**Haemolysis** is the premature destruction of red cells (lifespan <120 days), with compensatory marrow hyperplasia.

#### Evidence of haemolysis

- **Increased red cell destruction:** raised unconjugated bilirubin, raised LDH, low or absent haptoglobin, raised urinary urobilinogen, urinary haemosiderin (intravascular haemolysis), haemoglobinuria (dark urine), plasma free haemoglobin.
- **Increased production:** **reticulocytosis,** polychromasia, macrocytosis, nucleated red cells.
- **Morphology:** spherocytes, schistocytes, sickle cells, bite or blister cells, target cells.
- Clinical: **jaundice, pallor, splenomegaly,** pigment gallstones, leg ulcers, folate deficiency, aplastic crises (parvovirus B19).

#### Classification and key conditions

| Type | Examples |
|---|---|
| **Intrinsic: red cell membrane** | **Hereditary spherocytosis** (autosomal dominant; spherocytes, positive family history, negative DAT, raised MCHC, EMA binding test; gallstones, splenectomy for moderate/severe disease; folate; aplastic crisis), hereditary elliptocytosis |
| **Intrinsic: enzyme defects** | **G6PD deficiency** (X-linked; Mediterranean, African, Asian; **oxidative haemolysis triggered by fava beans, infections, drugs (primaquine, dapsone, nitrofurantoin, rasburicase, sulfonamides, methylene blue, high-dose vitamin C)**; bite cells, Heinz bodies; episodic; assay after the acute episode as levels may be falsely normal in reticulocytosis), pyruvate kinase deficiency |
| **Intrinsic: haemoglobinopathies** | **Sickle cell disease,** thalassaemias, unstable haemoglobins |
| **Extrinsic: immune** | **Warm autoimmune haemolytic anaemia** (IgG; DAT positive; idiopathic, SLE, CLL, lymphoma, drugs (methyldopa, penicillins, cephalosporins); treated with prednisolone, rituximab, splenectomy), **cold agglutinin disease** (IgM; acrocyanosis, Raynaud-like symptoms; DAT C3 positive; *Mycoplasma pneumoniae* and EBV, lymphoproliferative disease; avoid cold, rituximab, sutimlimab), **paroxysmal cold haemoglobinuria,** **alloimmune** (transfusion reactions, haemolytic disease of the newborn), **drug-induced** |
| **Extrinsic: microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA)** | **TTP, HUS, DIC, HELLP syndrome, malignant hypertension, scleroderma renal crisis, prosthetic valve** (mechanical haemolysis), vasculitis; **schistocytes** on film, thrombocytopenia, negative DAT (see 35.10) |
| **Infection** | **Malaria,** babesiosis, *Clostridium perfringens* sepsis, Bartonella |
| **Others** | **Paroxysmal nocturnal haemoglobinuria (PNH)** (complement-mediated; haemoglobinuria, thrombosis, cytopenias; flow cytometry; eculizumab, ravulizumab), hypersplenism, burns, snake venom, copper toxicity, Wilson disease crisis |

#### Sickle cell disease (SCD)

- **Genetics:** homozygous HbS (HbSS) or HbS with HbC, β-thalassaemia, etc.; prevalence high in Africa, the Middle East, India, and the Caribbean/African diaspora. **Sickle cell trait** is usually asymptomatic (but exertional rhabdomyolysis, haematuria, splenic infarct at altitude).
- **Pathophysiology:** deoxygenated HbS polymerises, causing sickling, haemolysis, vaso-occlusion and endothelial damage.
- **Acute complications (emergencies):**
  - **Vaso-occlusive (pain) crisis:** severe pain in bones, chest, abdomen; triggers: infection, cold, dehydration, hypoxia, stress. **Treatment:** rapid analgesia (IV opioids titrated within 30–60 minutes of arrival, often in the first hour), hydration, oxygen if hypoxic, treat triggers; avoid under-treatment and delay; individualised care plans.
  - **Acute chest syndrome:** fever, chest pain, cough, hypoxia, new infiltrate on chest X-ray; leading cause of death; oxygen, analgesia, antibiotics, incentive spirometry, **simple or exchange transfusion** in severe cases.
  - **Stroke/TIA** (children and adults): urgent transfusion and neurology; **screening with transcranial Doppler** in children.
  - **Infections:** functional hyposplenia leads to risk of pneumococcal, *Salmonella,* *Haemophilus,* meningococcal sepsis; fever ≥38.5 °C is an emergency (cultures, IV antibiotics); **penicillin prophylaxis** and vaccination.
  - **Splenic sequestration** (children: rapid splenic enlargement and Hb fall; emergency transfusion).
  - **Aplastic crisis** (parvovirus B19: reticulocytopenia, severe anaemia).
  - **Priapism, acute hepatic or renal syndromes, osteonecrosis, retinopathy, leg ulcers, pulmonary hypertension, chronic kidney disease, cholelithiasis.**
- **Disease-modifying therapy:** **hydroxycarbamide (hydroxyurea)** (raises HbF, reduces crises and acute chest syndrome and mortality), **L-glutamine, crizanlizumab, voxelotor** (availability varies), chronic transfusion in selected patients, **haematopoietic stem-cell transplantation and gene therapy** (curative approaches), preventive care (vaccination, folic acid, screening, prompt treatment of fever), pregnancy care.

### 35.7 Pancytopenia, marrow failure and malignancy

**Pancytopenia (anaemia, leucopenia, thrombocytopenia)** is an emergency of diagnosis. Look for **hypoproductive** (aplastic anaemia, drugs, B12/folate deficiency, MDS, marrow infiltration), **peripheral destruction or sequestration** (hypersplenism, autoimmune), or combined mechanisms.

| Condition | Features |
|---|---|
| **Acute leukaemia** | Fatigue, infections, bleeding or bruising, bone pain, lymphadenopathy, hepatosplenomegaly, gum hypertrophy (AML monocytic), raised or low white cells, **blasts on film;** emergency (tumour lysis, DIC in APL, hyperleucocytosis, neutropenic sepsis) |
| **Aplastic anaemia** | Pancytopenia with **hypocellular marrow,** no blasts; causes: idiopathic (immune), drugs (chloramphenicol, carbamazepine, sulphonamides, NSAIDs, gold), viruses (hepatitis, parvovirus, EBV), radiation, toxins, PNH, inherited marrow failure syndromes (Fanconi); treat with immunosuppression (antithymocyte globulin, ciclosporin, eltrombopag) or stem-cell transplantation; supportive care |
| **Myelodysplastic syndromes (MDS)** | Older adults; macrocytic anaemia, cytopenias, dysplasia in marrow, risk of AML; blood film; cytogenetics |
| **Myelofibrosis, myeloproliferative neoplasms** | Splenomegaly, leucoerythroblastic film, teardrop cells, JAK2 mutation |
| **Myeloma** | Anaemia, bone pain, hypercalcaemia, renal impairment, infection, rouleaux; serum protein electrophoresis and free light chains |
| **Marrow infiltration** | Metastatic cancer, lymphoma, granulomatous disease (TB), fibrosis |
| **Hypersplenism** | Splenomegaly (portal hypertension, lymphoma, infection) |
| **HIV, drug toxicity (chemotherapy, linezolid, clozapine, valproate)** | |

**Action for pancytopenia:** urgent haematology involvement; blood film; reticulocytes; B12/folate; liver function, viral serology (HIV, hepatitis, EBV, CMV, parvovirus); bone marrow aspirate and trephine, flow cytometry, cytogenetics; protect against infection (neutropenia: urgent antibiotics for fever); transfuse irradiated, leucodepleted blood products as required.

### 35.8 Transfusion principles

- **Restrictive transfusion strategy** in most haemodynamically stable adults: transfuse when **Hb <70 g/L** (target 70–90 g/L); **<80 g/L** in pre-existing cardiovascular disease, orthopaedic surgery; higher thresholds in acute coronary syndrome (e.g. <80–100 g/L depending on guidelines) and in significant ongoing bleeding. **Treat the patient, not the number:** consider symptoms, haemodynamic stability, ongoing blood loss.
- **Single-unit transfusion** with reassessment.
- **Do not transfuse** stable, asymptomatic patients with iron, B12 or folate deficiency (treat with replacement; transfuse only if decompensated).
- **Informed consent,** correct patient identification (two-person check), monitoring of vital signs (baseline, at 15 minutes, and after), recognition of reactions (fever, rigors, urticaria, dyspnoea: **TRALI, TACO,** haemolytic reaction, anaphylaxis, bacterial contamination).
- **Special requirements:** irradiated products for immunosuppressed patients and some haematology patients; CMV-negative for pregnancy/neonates; Rh(D)-negative blood for women of childbearing age.
- **Jehovah's Witnesses and others who decline blood:** discuss options (cell salvage, iron, erythropoietin, minimising blood draws), advance directives.

# Part B: Bleeding and Bruising

### 35.9 Normal haemostasis and the clinical pattern of bleeding

#### Haemostasis in brief

1. **Primary haemostasis:** vessel constriction and **platelet** adhesion (via von Willebrand factor, VWF, to exposed collagen), activation, and aggregation form a platelet plug.
2. **Secondary haemostasis (coagulation cascade):** clotting factors generate **fibrin,** stabilising the plug.
3. **Fibrinolysis:** plasmin removes clots; anticoagulant systems (antithrombin, proteins C and S) regulate coagulation.

#### Clinical clues: which type of defect?

| Feature | **Primary (platelets, VWF, vessels)** | **Secondary (coagulation factors)** |
|---|---|---|
| **Sites of bleeding** | **Mucocutaneous:** skin (petechiae, purpura, ecchymoses), epistaxis, gum bleeding, menorrhagia, GI bleeding, haematuria | **Deep:** haemarthroses, muscle and soft-tissue haematomas, retroperitoneal, intracranial, post-surgical |
| **Petechiae** | Characteristic | Rare |
| **Bruises** | Small, superficial, multiple | Large, deep, palpable |
| **After minor cuts or dental extraction** | **Immediate** prolonged oozing, stops with pressure | **Delayed** re-bleeding hours to days later |
| **Examples** | ITP, thrombocytopenia, von Willebrand disease, uraemia, antiplatelet drugs, scurvy | Haemophilia A and B, liver disease, warfarin, DOACs, vitamin K deficiency, DIC |

### 35.10 Evaluation

#### History

- **Bleeding history:** age of onset (lifelong vs acquired), type and frequency (nosebleeds >10 minutes or requiring medical attention, easy bruising without trauma, gum bleeding, prolonged bleeding after dental extraction, minor injuries, surgery, childbirth, heavy menstrual bleeding (flooding, clots, iron deficiency), joint bleeds), **structured bleeding assessment tool (ISTH-BAT).**
- **Surgical and dental history:** previous haemostatic challenges (tonsillectomy, circumcision, dental extractions, childbirth) and complications.
- **Family history:** bleeding disorders, consanguinity, males affected through maternal line (X-linked haemophilia).
- **Drugs:** anticoagulants (warfarin, DOACs, heparins), **antiplatelets (aspirin, clopidogrel),** NSAIDs, SSRIs, steroids, antibiotics (affect vitamin K, platelets: beta-lactams), valproate, chemotherapy, herbal supplements (ginkgo, garlic, ginseng, fish oil, vitamin E, turmeric), alcohol.
- **Medical conditions:** liver disease, kidney disease, malignancy (leukaemia, lymphoma, myeloma), autoimmune disease (SLE, antiphospholipid syndrome), infection (sepsis, HIV, hepatitis, viral), connective tissue disorders (Ehlers–Danlos: hypermobility, stretchy skin), malabsorption (vitamin K, vitamin C), poor nutrition, pregnancy.
- **Associated symptoms:** fever, weight loss, bone pain, jaundice, rash, joint pain, abdominal pain, haematuria, neurological symptoms.

#### Examination

- **Skin:** petechiae, purpura (palpable vs flat), ecchymoses, haematomas, telangiectasia (hereditary haemorrhagic telangiectasia: lips, tongue, fingertips, nasal mucosa; epistaxis and GI bleeding), perifollicular haemorrhages with corkscrew hairs (**scurvy**), skin hyperextensibility and scarring (Ehlers–Danlos), amyloid (waxy papules, periorbital purpura after Valsalva).
- **Mucosae:** gum bleeding, haemorrhagic bullae, conjunctival haemorrhages.
- **Joints:** swelling, deformity, limited movement (haemarthroses).
- **Lymph nodes, liver, spleen:** haematological malignancy, liver disease, hypersplenism.
- **Signs of liver disease, jaundice, anaemia.**
- **Fundoscopy:** retinal haemorrhages.
- **Vital signs and signs of shock.**

#### Laboratory tests

| Test | Reflects | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| **Platelet count** (FBC), **blood film** | Number and morphology; clumping (pseudothrombocytopenia), giant platelets, fragments | Thrombocytopenia; film for schistocytes, blasts |
| **Prothrombin time (PT)/INR** | Extrinsic and common pathways (factors VII, X, V, II, fibrinogen); vitamin K-dependent factors | Prolonged in warfarin, vitamin K deficiency, liver disease, DIC, factor VII deficiency, DOACs (variable) |
| **Activated partial thromboplastin time (aPTT)** | Intrinsic and common pathways (factors XII, XI, IX, VIII, X, V, II, fibrinogen) | Prolonged in haemophilia A/B, VWD (sometimes), factor XI deficiency, unfractionated heparin, DOACs, lupus anticoagulant, DIC, liver disease |
| **Thrombin time (TT)** | Fibrinogen to fibrin conversion | Prolonged in heparin, dysfibrinogenaemia, low fibrinogen, direct thrombin inhibitors |
| **Fibrinogen (Clauss)** | Fibrinogen level | Low in DIC, liver disease, massive transfusion, thrombolysis, congenital deficiency |
| **D-dimer** | Fibrin degradation | Raised in DIC, VTE, infection, malignancy, pregnancy |
| **Mixing study** (50:50 mix with normal plasma) | Distinguish **factor deficiency** (corrects) from **inhibitor** (lupus anticoagulant, acquired factor VIII antibody: does not correct) | |
| **Factor assays (VIII, IX, XI, etc.)** | Specific deficiency | |
| **von Willebrand factor antigen, VWF activity (ristocetin cofactor/GPIbM), factor VIII** | VWD | Draw when not acutely unwell or pregnant (VWF is an acute-phase reactant; levels rise with stress, oestrogen, pregnancy, inflammation, which can mask mild VWD) |
| **Platelet function tests** (PFA-100, light transmission aggregometry, flow cytometry) | Platelet disorders (Glanzmann thrombasthenia, Bernard–Soulier, storage pool, drug effect) | |
| **Clot lysis, factor XIII assay, α2-antiplasmin** | Rare causes with normal PT/aPTT | |
| **ADAMTS13 activity and inhibitor** | TTP | |
| **Platelet antibodies** | Limited clinical utility in ITP | |
| **Viscoelastic tests (TEG/ROTEM)** | Point-of-care assessment in major haemorrhage, cardiac surgery, trauma, liver transplant | |

#### Interpreting the screen (PT, aPTT, platelets)

| Pattern | Possible causes |
|---|---|
| **Low platelets, normal PT and aPTT** | ITP, drug-induced, marrow failure, hypersplenism, TTP/HUS (also normal coagulation), HIT, HELLP (variable), viral infection |
| **Normal platelets, prolonged PT only** | Warfarin, vitamin K deficiency, early liver disease, factor VII deficiency |
| **Normal platelets, prolonged aPTT only** | Haemophilia A and B, VWD, factor XI deficiency, unfractionated heparin, lupus anticoagulant (thrombotic rather than bleeding risk), factor XII deficiency (no bleeding), acquired haemophilia |
| **Prolonged PT and aPTT** | DIC, liver disease, severe vitamin K deficiency, DOACs, common pathway factor deficiency (X, V, II), hypofibrinogenaemia, massive transfusion |
| **Low platelets and prolonged PT/aPTT, low fibrinogen, high D-dimer** | **DIC** |
| **Normal screening tests with bleeding** | VWD (aPTT may be normal), platelet function disorders, mild factor deficiencies, factor XIII deficiency, vascular disorders (scurvy, EDS, senile purpura, steroid-induced), hyperfibrinolysis, drug effects (aspirin, SSRIs) |

### 35.11 Thrombocytopenia

**Thrombocytopenia:** platelet count <150 × 10⁹/L. Bleeding risk is related to the count and cause: usually **spontaneous bleeding when <10–20 × 10⁹/L,** surgical risk at <50 × 10⁹/L, and risk with trauma at 20–50. **Bleeding risk is higher at the same platelet count when there is also platelet dysfunction, coagulopathy, uraemia, fever, infection.**

#### First: exclude pseudothrombocytopenia

EDTA-dependent platelet clumping in the sample (check the film; repeat in citrate or heparin tube).

#### Causes

| Mechanism | Examples |
|---|---|
| **Decreased production** | Leukaemia, aplastic anaemia, MDS, marrow infiltration, megaloblastic anaemia, alcohol, chemotherapy, viral infection (HIV, HCV, EBV, parvovirus), drugs, congenital (thrombocytopenia absent radius, Wiskott–Aldrich), liver disease (reduced thrombopoietin) |
| **Increased destruction (immune)** | **ITP (immune thrombocytopenia),** drug-induced immune thrombocytopenia (quinine, vancomycin, carbamazepine, sulphonamides, heparin), SLE, antiphospholipid syndrome, CLL/lymphoma, HIV, hepatitis C, *H. pylori*, post-transfusion purpura, neonatal alloimmune thrombocytopenia |
| **Increased consumption (non-immune)** | **DIC, TTP, HUS, HELLP syndrome,** sepsis, malignancy, vascular lesions (haemangiomas: Kasabach–Merritt), extracorporeal circuits |
| **Sequestration** | Hypersplenism (cirrhosis, portal hypertension, lymphoma), hypothermia |
| **Dilutional** | Massive transfusion |
| **Gestational thrombocytopenia** | Mild (usually >70–100 × 10⁹/L), third trimester, benign; pre-eclampsia/HELLP, ITP, TTP/HUS must be considered |

#### Immune thrombocytopenia (ITP)

- Isolated thrombocytopenia (platelet count <100 × 10⁹/L) due to autoantibody-mediated platelet destruction and impaired production, in the absence of other causes. **Adults:** often chronic, women, associated with SLE, CLL, HIV, hepatitis C, *H. pylori;* **children:** often acute, post-viral, self-limiting.
- **Presentation:** petechiae, bruising, mucosal bleeding; well otherwise; spleen not enlarged; normal film apart from reduced platelets (large platelets).
- **Diagnosis:** exclusion (history, drugs, examination, FBC and film, HIV, HCV, *H. pylori* testing, immunoglobulins, liver/renal tests; marrow if age >60, atypical features or poor response).
- **Management (adults):** **observation** if platelets >30 × 10⁹/L and no bleeding; treat when <30 or significant bleeding or risk factors: **corticosteroids** (prednisolone 1 mg/kg/day or dexamethasone 40 mg daily for 4 days), **IVIG** or anti-D (RhD-positive non-splenectomised) for rapid rise in bleeding or pre-procedure; **thrombopoietin receptor agonists** (eltrombopag, romiplostim, avatrombopag) for persistent or chronic ITP; rituximab, fostamatinib, splenectomy; **platelet transfusion only for life-threatening bleeding.** Treat *H. pylori* if positive. **Avoid** aspirin and NSAIDs, intramuscular injections when the count is low; counsel on bleeding risk.

#### Heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT)

- Immune reaction against platelet factor 4–heparin complexes; **paradoxical thrombosis,** platelet fall of >30–50% **5–10 days after starting heparin** (earlier with recent exposure), more common with unfractionated than low molecular weight heparin, more common in surgical (cardiac, orthopaedic) patients; skin necrosis at injection sites; arterial and venous thromboses.
- **Diagnosis:** **4Ts score** (Thrombocytopenia, Timing, Thrombosis, oTher causes); if intermediate or high probability, **stop all heparin (including flushes and heparin-coated catheters), start a non-heparin anticoagulant** (argatroban, bivalirudin, fondaparinux, DOACs in stable patients) **and send an immunoassay (PF4 antibody)** with a functional assay (serotonin release) to confirm. **Do not give platelet transfusions** unless life-threatening bleeding. **Avoid warfarin** until platelets recover (risk of venous limb gangrene).

#### Thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP)

- **A haematological emergency** (untreated mortality >90%). Severe **ADAMTS13** deficiency (acquired autoantibody or hereditary), causing ultra-large VWF multimers and platelet-rich microthrombi.
- **Classic pentad** (full pentad uncommon): **thrombocytopenia, microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (schistocytes),** fever, **neurological abnormalities** (headache, confusion, stroke, seizures), renal impairment. Clinical diagnosis should be made when **thrombocytopenia + MAHA** is present without another obvious cause (DIC, malignancy, sepsis, malignant hypertension): **raised LDH, low haptoglobin, negative DAT, normal PT/aPTT/fibrinogen;** troponin elevation common.
- **PLASMIC score** estimates pre-test probability of severe ADAMTS13 deficiency.
- **Treatment:** **start urgent plasma exchange and high-dose steroids,** do not wait for ADAMTS13 result (send before treatment); **caplacizumab** (anti-VWF nanobody), **rituximab** for relapse prevention; **avoid platelet transfusions** unless life-threatening bleeding (may worsen thrombosis); folic acid; monitor platelets and LDH daily.
- **Atypical HUS** (complement-mediated; eculizumab) and **STEC-HUS** (Chapter 26) are related thrombotic microangiopathies.

#### Disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC)

- **Systemic activation of coagulation** leading to microvascular thrombosis, consumption of platelets and clotting factors, and bleeding.
- **Causes:** sepsis (most common), trauma (including head injury, burns, heat stroke), obstetric (abruption, amniotic fluid embolism, retained fetus, pre-eclampsia), malignancy (acute promyelocytic leukaemia, mucinous adenocarcinoma), liver disease, snake bite, transfusion reaction, vascular malformations.
- **Features:** bleeding from multiple sites (venepuncture, wounds, mucosa), petechiae, purpura, ischaemic skin lesions (purpura fulminans, acral cyanosis), organ dysfunction; **low platelets, prolonged PT and aPTT, low fibrinogen, raised D-dimer, schistocytes.**
- **ISTH DIC score** (platelets, D-dimer, PT, fibrinogen).
- **Treatment:** **treat the underlying cause** (antibiotics, source control, delivery, chemotherapy); **supportive** transfusion in bleeding or high bleeding risk (platelets <50, fibrinogen <1.5 g/L with cryoprecipitate or fibrinogen concentrate, FFP for prolonged PT/aPTT); heparin in selected thrombotic phenotypes; ATRA in APL (immediately).

### 35.12 Inherited bleeding disorders

#### von Willebrand disease (VWD)

- **The most common inherited bleeding disorder (up to 1% prevalence on laboratory definition, clinically significant in about 1 in 1,000).** Quantitative (type 1: partial deficiency; type 3: virtually absent) or qualitative (type 2) defects of VWF, which mediates platelet adhesion and carries factor VIII.
- **Clinical:** mucocutaneous bleeding, **epistaxis, easy bruising, gum bleeding, heavy menstrual bleeding** (iron deficiency in young women; about 10–20% of women with heavy menstrual bleeding have VWD), **bleeding after dental extractions, surgery and childbirth;** severe types may have joint bleeds.
- **Diagnosis:** VWF antigen, activity (ristocetin cofactor/GPIbM), factor VIII, VWF multimers, ratio activity/antigen; **aPTT may be normal;** blood group O has lower VWF levels; repeat testing if borderline; confirm in haemostasis centre.
- **Treatment:** **desmopressin (DDAVP)** (releases stored VWF; type 1; check response with a trial; hyponatraemia caution), **tranexamic acid** (menstrual and mucosal bleeding), **VWF/FVIII concentrates** (severe types, surgery), hormonal therapy for heavy periods; avoid NSAIDs.

#### Haemophilia A and B

- **X-linked recessive** deficiency of factor VIII (A) or factor IX (B); males affected (females carriers, with variable bleeding tendency); one-third have no family history (de novo mutations).
- **Severity** by factor level: severe (<1%), moderate (1–5%), mild (>5–40%).
- **Features:** **haemarthroses** (knees, elbows, ankles), muscle haematomas, intracranial bleeding, bleeding after circumcision, delayed bleeding after trauma or surgery; **prolonged aPTT** with normal PT and platelets; factor assays confirm.
- **Treatment:** **replacement** with factor concentrates (on-demand or **prophylaxis**), **emicizumab** (bispecific antibody, prophylaxis for haemophilia A), extended half-life products, non-factor agents (fitusiran, concizumab), **gene therapy** (haemophilia A and B approved in some countries); desmopressin for mild haemophilia A; **treat bleeds early,** physiotherapy; inhibitors (alloantibodies) complicate treatment; avoid intramuscular injections and NSAIDs; comprehensive care centres.

#### Other inherited disorders

- **Factor XI deficiency** (Ashkenazi Jews; variable bleeding), **factor VII, X, V, XIII deficiency,** **fibrinogen disorders,** **Glanzmann thrombasthenia, Bernard–Soulier syndrome** (platelet function defects), **hereditary haemorrhagic telangiectasia (Osler–Weber–Rendu)** (recurrent epistaxis, GI bleeding, pulmonary and cerebral arteriovenous malformations, iron deficiency; Curaçao criteria), **Ehlers–Danlos syndromes, osteogenesis imperfecta, Marfan** (vascular fragility).

### 35.13 Acquired coagulopathies and bleeding on drugs

#### Warfarin and vitamin K deficiency

- **Warfarin:** inhibits vitamin K-dependent factors (II, VII, IX, X, proteins C and S). **INR >5 without bleeding:** omit doses, consider low-dose oral vitamin K (1–2.5 mg) if high bleeding risk; **INR >8 without bleeding:** oral vitamin K 1–5 mg; **major bleeding:** **prothrombin complex concentrate (PCC)** plus **IV vitamin K 5–10 mg** (not FFP first-line); intracranial haemorrhage: immediate reversal.
- **Vitamin K deficiency:** malabsorption (cholestasis, coeliac, short bowel), broad-spectrum antibiotics, poor diet, newborns (haemorrhagic disease of the newborn: vitamin K prophylaxis at birth); give IV vitamin K.
- Check INR in bleeding with antibiotics (interaction).

#### Direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs) and heparins

- **Dabigatran** (thrombin inhibitor): reversal with **idarucizumab.** **Factor Xa inhibitors** (rivaroxaban, apixaban, edoxaban): **andexanet alfa** or **PCC** for life-threatening bleeding (availability varies); activated charcoal if recent ingestion; haemodialysis for dabigatran in some cases.
- **Unfractionated heparin:** **protamine** (1 mg per 100 units given in the last 2–3 hours). **LMWH:** partial reversal with protamine.
- **Fondaparinux:** no specific antidote; recombinant factor VIIa in life-threatening bleeding.
- General principles for **major bleeding on anticoagulation:** stop the drug, local haemostasis and compression, tranexamic acid, replace fluids and blood, correct renal function and drug levels, reversal agents for life-threatening or critical-site bleeding, involve haematology; decide on restarting anticoagulation based on thrombotic versus bleeding risk (Chapter 27, 28).

#### Antiplatelet drugs

- **Aspirin, clopidogrel, prasugrel, ticagrelor:** irreversible or reversible platelet inhibition; major bleeding: stop, platelet transfusion (may not work for ticagrelor with circulating drug), DDAVP in selected cases; **dual antiplatelet therapy** after stent has a high risk of thrombosis if stopped early: consult cardiology.

#### Liver disease

- Reduced synthesis of clotting factors (except VIII), vitamin K malabsorption, thrombocytopenia (hypersplenism, reduced thrombopoietin), platelet dysfunction, hyperfibrinolysis, and dysfibrinogenaemia produce **complex coagulopathy: a "rebalanced" haemostasis** that is fragile (bleeding or thrombosis). INR does not predict bleeding in cirrhosis; **do not routinely correct** with FFP; correct for procedures per guidance (viscoelastic tests).

#### Renal failure (uraemic platelet dysfunction)

- Bleeding with normal platelet count; treat with dialysis, desmopressin, conjugated oestrogens, correction of anaemia.

#### Acquired haemophilia

- **Autoantibodies against factor VIII;** older adults, postpartum, autoimmune disease, malignancy, drugs; **sudden severe bleeding** (skin, muscle, retroperitoneal, GI) with **isolated prolonged aPTT that does not correct on mixing;** high mortality; bypassing agents (recombinant FVIIa, FEIBA), emicizumab, porcine FVIII, and immunosuppression (steroids, cyclophosphamide, rituximab).

#### Vascular and connective-tissue causes of bruising

- **Senile (actinic) purpura:** older adults; forearms and hands; flat purple patches that fade slowly; normal tests; reassure.
- **Corticosteroids** (systemic and topical): skin atrophy and easy bruising.
- **Scurvy (vitamin C deficiency):** perifollicular haemorrhages, corkscrew hairs, gum swelling and bleeding, bruising, joint haemorrhage, poor wound healing; diet (older adults, alcohol misuse, restrictive diets, mental illness); treat with vitamin C.
- **Ehlers–Danlos syndrome, Marfan, pseudoxanthoma elasticum, amyloidosis, vasculitis.**
- **Trauma, self-harm, factitious.**

### 35.14 Bruising in children: bleeding disorder or abuse?

- Bruising is common in mobile children over the shins and bony prominences. **Concerning features of non-accidental injury (NAI):** bruises in **non-mobile infants** (any bruise on a baby who is not yet cruising is a red flag), bruises in **protected sites** (torso, ears, neck, frenulum, angle of the jaw, buttocks, genitalia, inner thighs, back), **patterned bruises** (hand, belt, loop-cord marks), multiple bruises of different ages, unexplained or inconsistent history, delay in seeking care (the mnemonic **TEN-4-FACESp**: Torso, Ears, Neck, 4 = child under 4 years, Frenulum, Angle of jaw, Cheeks, Eyelids, Subconjunctivae, plus any bruising in an infant ≤4.99 months).
- **Do not simply assume a bleeding disorder** and do not assume abuse: **perform a coagulation screen and FBC when appropriate** (to exclude leukaemia, ITP, haemophilia, VWD), but a normal screen does not exclude abuse. Many children with NAI have no bleeding disorder, and children with bleeding disorders can also suffer abuse.
- **Action:** follow safeguarding procedures (paediatric assessment, skeletal survey in young children, ophthalmology, neuroimaging when indicated, social services referral); document precisely with body maps and photographs.

### 35.15 Menorrhagia and bleeding in women

- **Heavy menstrual bleeding (HMB):** flooding, clots, changing pads every 1–2 hours, bleeding >7 days, iron deficiency anaemia. Causes: structural (fibroids, polyps, adenomyosis, malignancy), ovulatory dysfunction (PCOS, thyroid), coagulopathy (VWD, platelet function defects, factor deficiencies), iatrogenic (copper IUD, anticoagulants). **Test for bleeding disorders** in HMB since menarche, with a personal or family history of bleeding, or iron deficiency.
- **Investigation:** FBC, ferritin, TSH, pregnancy test, coagulation screen and VWF, pelvic ultrasound, endometrial assessment if age >45 or risk factors.
- **Management:** tranexamic acid, NSAIDs (not in bleeding disorders), levonorgestrel intrauterine system, combined hormonal contraception, progestogens, GnRH analogues, surgery; treat iron deficiency; desmopressin and VWF concentrate if VWD.
- **Postpartum haemorrhage,** **obstetric DIC,** **abnormal uterine bleeding on anticoagulants:** prompt evaluation.

### 35.16 Worked cases

#### Case 1: Iron deficiency in a man

*A 64-year-old man has 3 months of fatigue and breathlessness. Hb 84 g/L, MCV 71, ferritin 6, no GI symptoms, no NSAIDs. FIT is positive. Colonoscopy: caecal adenocarcinoma.*

**Reasoning.** Iron-deficiency anaemia in a man over 60: **GI cancer until excluded.**

**Action.** Colonoscopy and upper endoscopy; staging; IV iron or transfusion for symptomatic anaemia pre-operatively; surgical referral.

#### Case 2: Pernicious anaemia

*A 62-year-old woman with hypothyroidism has 6 months of tiredness, tingling in her feet, and unsteadiness. Hb 92, MCV 114, hypersegmented neutrophils, LDH raised, B12 80 pg/mL, anti-intrinsic factor antibody positive. She has reduced vibration sense and a positive Romberg sign.*

**Reasoning.** **Pernicious anaemia with neurological involvement.**

**Action.** IM hydroxocobalamin 1 mg alternate days until no further improvement, then every 2 months for life (more frequent loading with neurology), monitor potassium and reticulocytes, screen for other autoimmune disease; consider endoscopy; do not give folic acid alone.

#### Case 3: Microcytosis that is not iron deficiency

*A 25-year-old woman of Mediterranean descent has Hb 108 g/L, MCV 62, RBC 5.8 × 10¹²/L, ferritin 95, RDW normal. Mentzer index 10.7. HbA₂ 5.2%.*

**Reasoning.** **β-thalassaemia trait.**

**Action.** Avoid iron, explain; folic acid in pregnancy; test partner; genetic counselling; no treatment otherwise.

#### Case 4: Autoimmune haemolytic anaemia

*A 55-year-old woman has 2 weeks of fatigue, dark urine and jaundice. Hb 68, reticulocytes 15%, bilirubin 55 (indirect), LDH 1,100, haptoglobin undetectable, DAT positive for IgG. Film: spherocytes. CT: splenomegaly and lymphadenopathy.*

**Reasoning.** **Warm autoimmune haemolytic anaemia,** possibly secondary to lymphoma (CLL) or SLE.

**Action.** Prednisolone 1 mg/kg, folic acid, transfusion for severe symptoms (cross-match difficulty; inform blood bank), look for underlying disease, rituximab or splenectomy for refractory disease.

#### Case 5: ITP

*A 28-year-old woman has petechiae, gum bleeding and heavy periods for 10 days. Platelets 12 × 10⁹/L; Hb 118; WBC normal; film: reduced platelets, no schistocytes; no splenomegaly. HIV, hepatitis C and H. pylori negative; pregnancy test negative.*

**Reasoning.** **Primary immune thrombocytopenia.**

**Action.** Prednisolone (or dexamethasone), IVIG if active bleeding or very low platelets, avoid NSAIDs and IM injections, monitor; thrombopoietin receptor agonist if persistent; platelet transfusion only for life-threatening bleeding; contraception counselling and bleeding precautions.

#### Case 6: TTP

*A 36-year-old woman has headache, confusion, fever 38.2 °C and petechiae. Hb 70 g/L, platelets 9 × 10⁹/L, creatinine 130, LDH 2,400, haptoglobin undetectable, film shows many schistocytes, DAT negative, INR and fibrinogen normal. PLASMIC score 7.*

**Reasoning.** **TTP** until proven otherwise.

**Action.** Emergency plasma exchange, methylprednisolone, caplacizumab; send ADAMTS13 before plasma; avoid platelet transfusion; monitor neurological status, troponin, creatinine; rituximab for refractory or relapsing disease.

#### Case 7: von Willebrand disease

*A 17-year-old girl has had heavy periods since menarche, frequent nosebleeds, and prolonged bleeding after a tooth extraction. Ferritin 8, Hb 98, normal platelets, aPTT slightly prolonged, VWF antigen 28%, VWF activity 22%, factor VIII 45%.*

**Reasoning.** **von Willebrand disease type 1.**

**Action.** Tranexamic acid, hormonal therapy (LNG-IUS or combined pill), iron replacement, desmopressin trial and plan for procedures, avoid NSAIDs and aspirin, family testing, haemophilia treatment centre referral.

#### Case 8: Haemophilia A

*A 3-year-old boy has a swollen, warm, painful right knee and refuses to walk, with a history of large bruises after minor falls. His maternal uncle had bleeding problems. aPTT 78 s (normal 25–35), PT normal, platelets normal, factor VIII 1%.*

**Reasoning.** **Severe haemophilia A** with haemarthrosis.

**Action.** Immediate factor VIII replacement (do not wait for imaging), analgesia (avoid NSAIDs), rest, then physiotherapy; start prophylaxis (emicizumab or factor VIII), comprehensive care, genetic counselling, vaccination.

#### Case 9: DIC

*A 62-year-old man with pneumonia and septic shock develops bleeding from venepuncture sites and oozing around lines. Platelets 28, INR 2.4, aPTT 62, fibrinogen 0.8 g/L, D-dimer very high, schistocytes on film.*

**Reasoning.** **Sepsis-induced DIC.**

**Action.** Treat sepsis (antibiotics, source control, fluids, vasopressors), replacement therapy for bleeding: platelets (<50 with bleeding), cryoprecipitate or fibrinogen concentrate (<1.5 g/L), FFP/PCC for prolonged PT/aPTT; monitor labs 6–12 hourly.

#### Case 10: Heparin-induced thrombocytopenia

*A 68-year-old man, 7 days after hip replacement on enoxaparin, has platelets falling from 280 to 85 × 10⁹/L and a new swollen calf with DVT. 4Ts score 6.*

**Reasoning.** **High probability of HIT.**

**Action.** Stop all heparin; start a non-heparin anticoagulant (argatroban, fondaparinux, or a DOAC when stable); send PF4 antibodies; avoid platelet transfusion and warfarin until platelets recover; document as HIT.

#### Case 11: Sickle cell crisis with fever

*A 22-year-old woman with HbSS has 12 hours of severe chest and back pain, fever 39.0 °C, cough, and SpO₂ 90%. Chest X-ray: right lower lobe infiltrate. Hb 70 (baseline 85).*

**Reasoning.** **Acute chest syndrome** complicating a vaso-occlusive crisis (with infection).

**Action.** Oxygen, IV opioid analgesia, antibiotics (cover pneumococcus, atypicals), hydration (avoid overload), incentive spirometry, cross-match, simple or exchange transfusion for deterioration; ICU liaison; haematology review.

#### Case 12: Unexplained bruising in an infant

*A 4-month-old boy presents with a bruise on his cheek and several bruises on the torso. The history is inconsistent. Platelets and coagulation screens are normal.*

**Reasoning.** Bruising in a non-mobile infant on the torso and cheek: **concern for non-accidental injury,** regardless of normal coagulation screen.

**Action.** Safeguarding pathway: senior paediatric assessment, full examination, skeletal survey, head imaging and ophthalmology as indicated, social services involvement, documentation.

### 35.17 Common pitfalls

- **Treating anaemia without finding the cause** (especially iron deficiency in men and postmenopausal women).
- **Giving iron in thalassaemia trait or anaemia of chronic disease** without evidence of deficiency; relying on ferritin alone in inflammation.
- **Giving folic acid alone** in B12 deficiency.
- **Ignoring neurological symptoms** of B12 deficiency or waiting for anaemia.
- **Transfusing to a number** rather than to symptoms and risk; over-transfusing in variceal bleeding.
- **Missing haemolysis** (not checking the film, reticulocytes, LDH, haptoglobin, DAT).
- **Missing TTP** by waiting for ADAMTS13 results; giving platelets in TTP and HIT.
- **Continuing heparin in suspected HIT.**
- **Using aPTT and PT alone** to exclude VWD or platelet disorders; testing VWF during acute illness.
- **Correcting INR in cirrhosis** with FFP without indication.
- **Giving FFP rather than PCC and vitamin K** for warfarin-related major bleeding.
- **Missing acquired haemophilia** in an older patient with extensive bruising and a prolonged aPTT.
- **Attributing bruising in children to a bleeding disorder** or dismissing non-accidental injury.
- **Failing to ask about herbal products and over-the-counter NSAIDs** in bleeding.
- **Not investigating heavy menstrual bleeding** for bleeding disorders and iron deficiency.
- **Missing pancytopenia** as an emergency (leukaemia, aplastic anaemia).

### 35.18 Key points

- Anaemia is a sign: classify by MCV and reticulocytes; use the film; check ferritin, B12, folate, haemolysis markers.
- Iron deficiency in men and postmenopausal women = GI investigation; treat with oral iron (alternate-day dosing) or IV iron; continue after normalisation.
- B12 deficiency needs IM replacement if neurological or malabsorptive; never folate alone.
- Thalassaemia trait is microcytic with high RBC count, normal ferritin, and raised HbA₂ (β); no iron; genetic counselling.
- Haemolysis: raised LDH, bilirubin, reticulocytes, low haptoglobin; DAT distinguishes immune; sickle cell disease needs rapid analgesia, treatment of fever and acute chest syndrome as emergencies.
- Pancytopenia and unexplained cytopenias need urgent haematology evaluation.
- Bleeding pattern (mucocutaneous vs deep) guides testing: platelets, PT, aPTT, fibrinogen; then VWF and factor assays.
- Emergencies: TTP (plasma exchange), HIT (stop heparin), DIC (treat cause and replace), severe thrombocytopenia, major bleeding on anticoagulants (reversal).
- Heavy menstrual bleeding and bruising in children demand attention to bleeding disorders and safeguarding.

### 35.19 Self-assessment

1. List causes of microcytic anaemia and how to distinguish iron deficiency from thalassaemia trait.
   *Answer: Iron deficiency, thalassaemia trait, anaemia of chronic disease, sideroblastic anaemia, lead; iron deficiency has low ferritin, high RDW, and Mentzer index >13; thalassaemia trait has normal ferritin, high RBC count, Mentzer <13, raised HbA₂ in β trait.*

2. Why is folic acid alone dangerous in B12 deficiency?
   *Answer: It may correct the anaemia but allow progression or precipitate neurological damage (subacute combined degeneration).*

3. What laboratory findings confirm haemolysis?
   *Answer: Raised reticulocytes, LDH, and unconjugated bilirubin; low haptoglobin; blood film changes (spherocytes, schistocytes); urinary haemosiderin; DAT for immune causes.*

4. Compare the bleeding patterns in platelet disorders and coagulation factor disorders.
   *Answer: Platelet/VWF/vessel disorders cause mucocutaneous bleeding (petechiae, epistaxis, menorrhagia) with immediate bleeding after injury; coagulation factor disorders cause deep bleeding (haemarthroses, muscle haematomas) with delayed re-bleeding.*

5. What is the initial management of suspected TTP?
   *Answer: Urgent plasma exchange with high-dose corticosteroids (plus caplacizumab), send ADAMTS13 before treatment, avoid platelet transfusion unless life-threatening bleeding.*

6. When should you suspect non-accidental injury in a child with bruises?
   *Answer: Bruising in a non-mobile infant, bruises on the torso, ears, neck, frenulum, angle of jaw, patterned bruises, multiple bruises of different ages, or an inconsistent history.*
