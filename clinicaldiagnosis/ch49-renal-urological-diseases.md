# Chapter 49. Renal and Urological Diseases

## Learning objectives

After studying this chapter you should be able to:

1. Interpret serum creatinine, eGFR, albuminuria, urinalysis and urine microscopy, and recognise the limits of each.
2. Diagnose and stage acute kidney injury (AKI) using KDIGO criteria, separate pre-renal, intrinsic and post-renal causes at the bedside, and start the right first-hour actions.
3. Stage chronic kidney disease (CKD) by cause, GFR (G) and albuminuria (A) categories, estimate risk of progression, and apply the core pillars of CKD management.
4. Recognise nephrotic and nephritic syndromes and rapidly progressive glomerulonephritis, and choose the first investigations.
5. Diagnose and manage urinary tract infection (UTI), including pyelonephritis, catheter-associated UTI and asymptomatic bacteriuria.
6. Assess renal colic and nephrolithiasis, including the red flags that need urgent decompression.
7. Take a structured approach to haematuria, raised PSA, scrotal pain, lower urinary tract symptoms, and common urological cancers.
8. Adjust drug dosing and avoid nephrotoxins in renal impairment.

> **Note on thresholds and doses.** Numbers in this chapter follow widely used guidelines (KDIGO, NICE, EAU, AUA, IDSA) at the time of writing. Local antimicrobial resistance, local protocols and the current edition of your national formulary override any figure printed here. Verify before prescribing.

## 1. Assessing kidney function

### 1.1 Creatinine and eGFR

Serum creatinine is a product of muscle metabolism, filtered at the glomerulus and also secreted by the proximal tubule. Three consequences follow.

- **Creatinine rises late.** A healthy person can lose half of their nephron mass before creatinine leaves the reference range. Creatinine is a delayed marker in acute injury and an insensitive marker of early CKD.
- **Creatinine reflects muscle mass.** A frail 85-year-old woman with creatinine 100 µmol/L (1.13 mg/dL) may have an eGFR of about 45 mL/min/1.73 m². A muscular 25-year-old man with the same creatinine has an eGFR above 80. Amputees, malnourished patients, patients with cirrhosis and sarcopenic older adults all have "falsely reassuring" creatinine values.
- **Some drugs raise creatinine without changing GFR** by blocking tubular secretion: trimethoprim, cimetidine, dolutegravir, cobicistat, and some tyrosine kinase inhibitors. A rise of up to 10–15% is expected.

The **eGFR** equation (CKD-EPI 2009 or the 2021 race-free version, MDRD, or the EKFC equation) estimates GFR from creatinine, age and sex. It assumes steady state; it is **invalid in AKI**, in pregnancy, in extremes of body size, in amputation, in severe malnutrition, and in rapidly changing function. Where the estimate matters (e.g., drug dosing of narrow-therapeutic-index drugs, living kidney donation), consider **cystatin C**-based eGFR or a combined creatinine-cystatin C equation, or measured GFR.

For drug dosing in extremes of body size, the Cockcroft–Gault creatinine clearance is still used in many drug labels; ask your pharmacist which is preferred.

### 1.2 Albuminuria and proteinuria

Albuminuria is the second axis of CKD classification and often the stronger predictor of renal and cardiovascular risk.

| Category | Albumin:creatinine ratio (ACR) | Approximate PCR |
|---|---|---|
| A1: normal to mildly increased | < 3 mg/mmol (< 30 mg/g) | < 15 mg/mmol (< 150 mg/g) |
| A2: moderately increased | 3–30 mg/mmol (30–300 mg/g) | 15–50 mg/mmol |
| A3: severely increased | > 30 mg/mmol (> 300 mg/g) | > 50 mg/mmol |

Practical points:

- Use a **first-morning spot urine ACR**. A 24-hour collection is rarely needed and is often incomplete.
- A positive result should be **confirmed on a repeat sample** (strenuous exercise, fever, menstruation, UTI and heart failure all raise ACR transiently).
- ACR above 70 mg/mmol (about 700 mg/g) is in the range where nephrology referral is usually recommended, and ACR above 300 mg/mmol (about 3 g/g) in the nephrotic range.
- Urine dipstick for protein detects mainly albumin and misses low-grade albuminuria and light chains. **Use ACR for diabetes, hypertension and CKD screening. Use PCR (or serum/urine electrophoresis) when myeloma is a concern.**

### 1.3 Urinalysis and microscopy

A urine dipstick is a bedside test with a lot of information when interpreted together.

- **Blood + protein + red cell casts or dysmorphic red cells**: glomerular disease until proven otherwise.
- **Blood without protein, with normal-shaped red cells**: look for a urological source (stone, tumour, infection, trauma, BPH).
- **Leukocytes + nitrites + symptoms**: UTI. Nitrite-negative does not exclude infection (Gram-positive organisms and organisms with short bladder dwell do not produce nitrite).
- **Muddy brown granular casts**: acute tubular injury (ATN).
- **White cell casts**: pyelonephritis or interstitial nephritis.
- **Eosinophiluria** has poor sensitivity and specificity for interstitial nephritis and should not be used to diagnose it.
- **Oval fat bodies and "Maltese cross" lipid droplets**: heavy proteinuria.
- **Specific gravity fixed around 1.010** (isosthenuria): chronic tubulo-interstitial damage.
- **Urine pH > 5.5 in a patient with stones**: consider renal tubular acidosis or urea-splitting infection (very alkaline, > 7).

Dipstick "blood" reacts with haemoglobin and myoglobin as well as intact red cells. A positive dipstick with no red cells on microscopy suggests haemoglobinuria or myoglobinuria (rhabdomyolysis, haemolysis).

### 1.4 When imaging is needed

- **Renal ultrasound** is the first-line test for AKI of unclear cause, CKD of unclear cause, haematuria with protein, and suspected obstruction. It gives kidney size (small, echogenic kidneys suggest chronicity; normal or large kidneys occur in diabetic nephropathy, amyloid, HIV nephropathy, polycystic kidney disease, acute processes), cortical thickness, hydronephrosis, stones, cysts and masses.
- **Non-contrast low-dose CT (CT KUB)** is the test of choice for renal colic.
- **Contrast CT** is used for masses, haematuria evaluation (CT urogram) and complicated infection. In CKD G4–G5, the risk of contrast-associated AKI is lower than once feared, but the decision should still weigh the benefit; do not withhold a life-saving scan because of eGFR.
- **Gadolinium**: avoid group I agents in eGFR < 30 because of nephrogenic systemic fibrosis. Modern group II macrocyclic agents carry very low risk.

## 2. Acute kidney injury

### 2.1 Definition and staging (KDIGO)

AKI is diagnosed by **any one** of:

- Rise in serum creatinine ≥ 26.5 µmol/L (0.3 mg/dL) within 48 hours.
- Rise in creatinine to ≥ 1.5 × baseline, known or presumed to have occurred within the previous 7 days.
- Urine output < 0.5 mL/kg/h for ≥ 6 hours.

| Stage | Creatinine | Urine output |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 1.5–1.9 × baseline, or rise ≥ 26.5 µmol/L (0.3 mg/dL) | < 0.5 mL/kg/h for 6–12 h |
| 2 | 2.0–2.9 × baseline | < 0.5 mL/kg/h for ≥ 12 h |
| 3 | ≥ 3.0 × baseline, or creatinine ≥ 354 µmol/L (4.0 mg/dL), or initiation of renal replacement therapy | < 0.3 mL/kg/h for ≥ 24 h, or anuria for ≥ 12 h |

AKI is common (up to 20% of hospital admissions), strongly associated with mortality, and a major risk factor for subsequent CKD. A **single creatinine value without a baseline is hard to interpret**: look for previous results in the records, in primary care, or ask the patient.

### 2.2 The three-compartment approach

Always ask: *is the problem before the kidney, in the kidney, or after it?*

**Pre-renal (about 40–60% in hospitalised patients)**

Reduced renal perfusion without structural damage. Causes: volume depletion (vomiting, diarrhoea, haemorrhage, poor intake, diuretics), low cardiac output (heart failure), systemic vasodilatation (sepsis, cirrhosis, anaphylaxis), and renal vasoconstriction (NSAIDs, ACE inhibitors/ARBs in volume-depleted states, calcineurin inhibitors, hepatorenal syndrome, contrast).

Clues: thirst, orthostatic hypotension, dry mucous membranes, low JVP, recent fluid loss, or in cardiorenal syndrome a high JVP and oedema. Bland urine sediment, concentrated urine (osmolality > 500 mOsm/kg), urine sodium < 20 mmol/L, FEurea < 35% (more reliable than FENa on diuretics), urea:creatinine ratio rising out of proportion. Responds to restoring perfusion within 24–72 hours.

**Intrinsic renal**

- *Acute tubular necrosis (ATN)*: the commonest intrinsic cause. Ischaemic (prolonged pre-renal state, sepsis, shock) or toxic (aminoglycosides, vancomycin, amphotericin, cisplatin, contrast, myoglobin, haemoglobin). Muddy brown casts, urine sodium > 40 mmol/L, FENa > 2%, isosthenuria. Recovery over days to weeks.
- *Acute interstitial nephritis (AIN)*: drug hypersensitivity (penicillins, cephalosporins, PPIs, NSAIDs, sulfonamides, rifampicin, checkpoint inhibitors), infection, autoimmune disease (Sjögren, sarcoid, TINU). Fever, rash and eosinophilia in a minority only. Sterile pyuria, white cell casts, mild proteinuria. Stop the offending drug; steroids if no recovery in a few days.
- *Glomerular disease*: nephritic syndrome, RPGN (see section 4). Haematuria, red cell casts, proteinuria, hypertension.
- *Vascular*: renal artery thrombosis/embolism, renal vein thrombosis, thrombotic microangiopathy (HUS, TTP, scleroderma renal crisis, malignant hypertension), atheroembolic disease (livedo, eosinophilia, after arterial catheterisation).
- *Rhabdomyolysis*: CK usually > 5,000 U/L; dark urine, dipstick blood positive with no red cells; hyperkalaemia, hyperphosphataemia, hypocalcaemia early.
- *Tumour lysis syndrome, myeloma cast nephropathy*.

**Post-renal (about 5–10%, but the most reversible)**

Obstruction to flow: prostatic enlargement, urethral stricture, bladder tumour, blood clots, neurogenic bladder, drugs (anticholinergics, opioids), pelvic malignancy compressing ureters, stones in a solitary kidney or bilateral stones, retroperitoneal fibrosis. Obstruction must be **excluded in every case of AKI** with an ultrasound (or at least a bladder scan) unless the cause is clearly otherwise.

### 2.3 Bedside approach

1. **Resuscitate and treat life threats first**: hyperkalaemia with ECG changes, pulmonary oedema, severe acidosis, sepsis, shock. Check a point-of-care potassium and ECG early.
2. **Take a medication history**: ACE inhibitor, ARB, diuretic, NSAID, "triple whammy" (ACE-I/ARB + diuretic + NSAID), metformin, SGLT2 inhibitor (hold during acute illness: "sick day rules"), lithium, aminoglycosides, vancomycin, trimethoprim, contrast, PPIs, herbal remedies.
3. **Assess volume status**: weight, orthostatic blood pressure, JVP, capillary refill, mucous membranes, skin turgor, oedema, lung bases, fluid balance chart. Bedside ultrasound (IVC, lung B-lines) can help.
4. **Look for obstruction**: palpable bladder, prostate examination, bladder scan, renal ultrasound.
5. **Urinalysis and microscopy** on fresh urine, ideally examined by someone who looks at the sediment themselves.
6. **Investigations**: urea and electrolytes, bicarbonate/venous gas, calcium, phosphate, CK if indicated, full blood count and film (haemolysis, schistocytes), CRP, LFTs, glucose, urine ACR/PCR, urine sodium/urea, blood cultures if septic. If a glomerular or vasculitic cause is suspected: ANA, dsDNA, C3/C4, ANCA, anti-GBM, hepatitis B/C and HIV serology, serum free light chains and protein electrophoresis, cryoglobulins.
7. **Imaging**: ultrasound within 24 hours (immediately if sepsis plus suspected obstruction).

### 2.4 Management principles

- **Treat the cause.** Stop nephrotoxins and, temporarily, drugs that reduce glomerular perfusion pressure (ACE-I/ARB, NSAIDs, diuretics in volume depletion, SGLT2i, metformin when eGFR is low or the patient is unwell).
- **Fluids**: give isotonic crystalloid (balanced solutions such as Ringer's lactate/Plasma-Lyte are at least as good as 0.9% saline and avoid hyperchloraemic acidosis) in boluses of 250–500 mL with reassessment, in hypovolaemic patients. Avoid fluid overload; do not give fluids to a patient who is already oedematous and hypervolaemic. Avoid starch solutions.
- **Relieve obstruction**: urethral catheter for lower tract obstruction (expect post-obstructive diuresis; monitor sodium, potassium and fluid balance); nephrostomy or retrograde stent for upper tract obstruction with infection, a solitary kidney, or AKI.
- **Hyperkalaemia**: calcium gluconate 10% 10–30 mL IV for ECG changes (repeat if needed), insulin–dextrose (10 units soluble insulin with 25 g glucose; monitor glucose for several hours), nebulised salbutamol 10–20 mg as an adjunct, treat acidosis, and remove potassium (loop diuretic if the patient is making urine, sodium zirconium cyclosilicate or patiromer, dialysis in refractory cases).
- **Diuretics** do not improve outcomes in AKI but are useful to treat volume overload.
- **Dose adjust** all renally cleared drugs and review daily.
- **Monitor**: urine output (catheter if needed), daily weights, electrolytes at least daily.
- **Nephrology referral**: stage 3 AKI, AKI of unclear cause, suspected glomerulonephritis/vasculitis/TMA/myeloma, no response to treatment, need for dialysis, AKI on CKD G4–G5, or transplant kidney.

### 2.5 Indications for renal replacement therapy: "AEIOU"

- **A**cidosis, refractory (e.g., pH < 7.15 despite treatment).
- **E**lectrolytes: refractory hyperkalaemia.
- **I**ntoxication: dialysable toxins (lithium, methanol, ethylene glycol, salicylates, metformin-associated lactic acidosis).
- **O**verload: pulmonary oedema unresponsive to diuretics.
- **U**raemic complications: pericarditis, encephalopathy, bleeding, neuropathy.

Do not start dialysis solely because of a number. Timing studies show that waiting for a clear indication is safe in most patients.

### 2.6 After AKI

A third of patients are left with new or worsening CKD. Check creatinine and ACR 3 months after an episode; record AKI in the problem list and discharge summary; give clear "sick day" advice (temporarily stop ACE-I/ARB, diuretics, SGLT2 inhibitors, metformin and NSAIDs during vomiting, diarrhoea or fever; restart after 24–48 hours of normal eating and drinking).

## 3. Chronic kidney disease

### 3.1 Definition and classification

CKD is defined as abnormalities of kidney structure or function, present for **more than 3 months**, with implications for health. Either of the following is enough:

- eGFR < 60 mL/min/1.73 m².
- Markers of kidney damage: albuminuria (ACR ≥ 3 mg/mmol), urine sediment abnormalities, electrolyte or other abnormalities due to tubular disorders, histological abnormalities, structural abnormalities on imaging, or a history of kidney transplantation.

Classification is by **Cause, GFR category (G1–G5) and Albuminuria category (A1–A3)**.

| GFR category | eGFR (mL/min/1.73 m²) | Description |
|---|---|---|
| G1 | ≥ 90 | Normal or high |
| G2 | 60–89 | Mildly decreased |
| G3a | 45–59 | Mildly to moderately decreased |
| G3b | 30–44 | Moderately to severely decreased |
| G4 | 15–29 | Severely decreased |
| G5 | < 15 | Kidney failure |

The "heat map" combining G and A categories predicts risk of ESKD, AKI, cardiovascular events and death. CKD G3a A1 is low risk; G3b A3 or G4 is very high risk.

### 3.2 Causes

Globally, the commonest causes are **diabetes** and **hypertension/vascular disease**, followed by glomerulonephritis, polycystic kidney disease, obstructive uropathy, reflux nephropathy and tubulo-interstitial disease. In specific regions: CKD of uncertain aetiology in agricultural workers (Central America, Sri Lanka, India), sickle cell nephropathy, HIV-associated nephropathy and APOL1-associated disease (West African ancestry), schistosomiasis, herbal nephropathy (aristolochic acid), lead and other toxins, and analgesic use.

In practice a cause is not found in many patients, and diabetic and hypertensive CKD are often presumptive diagnoses. **Atypical features that should prompt a search for another cause**: rapid fall in eGFR (> 5 mL/min/year, or > 25% in a short time), heavy haematuria, active sediment, nephrotic-range proteinuria in a patient without diabetes, short diabetes duration or absence of retinopathy in a patient with presumed diabetic nephropathy, onset below age 30 or above 70 with a new syndrome, family history, or systemic features.

### 3.3 Detection and monitoring

Screen people at risk: diabetes, hypertension, cardiovascular disease, family history of kidney failure, acute kidney injury history, urological disease, nephrotoxic drugs, multisystem disease (lupus), obesity. Use eGFR **and** ACR at least annually, more often at higher risk categories.

**Kidney Failure Risk Equation (KFRE)**: a validated 4-variable tool (age, sex, eGFR, ACR) that gives 2-year and 5-year risk of needing dialysis or transplantation. A 5-year risk above about 5% suggests nephrology referral; above 10–20% prompts planning of kidney replacement therapy or conservative care.

### 3.4 Management pillars

1. **Blood pressure control**: target systolic < 120 mmHg (standardised measurement) per KDIGO 2021 if tolerated, with individual judgement in frail patients and those with limiting orthostatic hypotension; many guidelines use < 130/80 mmHg.
2. **Renin–angiotensin system blockade** (ACE inhibitor or ARB) for patients with albuminuria (A2–A3), titrated to maximum tolerated dose. Accept a creatinine rise up to 30% after starting; stop and investigate if larger (consider renal artery stenosis, volume depletion). Check potassium and creatinine 1–2 weeks after starting or increasing the dose. Do not combine ACE-I and ARB.
3. **SGLT2 inhibitors** (dapagliflozin, empagliflozin, canagliflozin): reduce progression and cardiovascular events in CKD with or without diabetes (eGFR ≥ 20 for initiation in most guidelines). Expect an initial small dip in eGFR. Hold during acute illness; counsel on genital mycotic infections; risk of euglycaemic ketoacidosis in diabetes.
4. **Non-steroidal mineralocorticoid receptor antagonist (finerenone)** in type 2 diabetes with CKD and albuminuria despite maximum RAS blockade and normal potassium (≤ 4.8 mmol/L at initiation).
5. **GLP-1 receptor agonists** for patients with type 2 diabetes and CKD (cardiovascular and kidney benefit), and increasingly for obesity.
6. **Glycaemic control**: individualised HbA1c (often 53–64 mmol/mol, 7.0–8.0%); adjust drugs for eGFR (metformin reduce dose at eGFR < 45, stop < 30; avoid sulfonylureas such as glibenclamide; insulin requirements fall as GFR falls).
7. **Lipid lowering**: statin (or statin/ezetimibe) in adults ≥ 50 with CKD; no need to monitor lipids routinely once on treatment.
8. **Lifestyle**: stop smoking, weight loss, exercise, dietary salt < 5 g/day, avoid high protein loads in advanced disease (0.8 g/kg/day). Avoid dietary potassium restriction unless hyperkalaemic.
9. **Avoid nephrotoxins**: NSAIDs, aminoglycosides, repeated contrast, PPIs (long-term, review need), lithium, herbal remedies.
10. **Vaccination**: influenza, pneumococcal, COVID-19, hepatitis B (for those likely to need dialysis).

### 3.5 Complications

- **Anaemia of CKD** (usually when eGFR < 45 and worsening): check ferritin, transferrin saturation, B12 and folate first. Correct iron deficiency (oral or IV iron; IV preferred in haemodialysis). Erythropoiesis-stimulating agents (ESA) or HIF-prolyl hydroxylase inhibitors where available, targeting haemoglobin about 100–120 g/L; avoid targeting above 130 g/L (stroke, thrombosis, mortality). Always exclude other causes of anaemia (occult GI bleeding, haemolysis, myeloma).
- **CKD mineral and bone disorder (CKD-MBD)**: high phosphate, low calcium, low active vitamin D, high PTH. Monitor calcium, phosphate, PTH and 25-OH vitamin D from G3. Correct vitamin D deficiency; dietary phosphate control; phosphate binders in persistent hyperphosphataemia; calcimimetics or parathyroidectomy for severe secondary hyperparathyroidism. Vascular calcification is a major driver of cardiovascular risk.
- **Metabolic acidosis**: oral sodium bicarbonate when serum bicarbonate persistently < 22 mmol/L (aim to keep ≥ 22), watching sodium and fluid load.
- **Hyperkalaemia**: dietary review, stop offending drugs, potassium binders to allow continuation of RAS blockade.
- **Fluid overload and hypertension**: salt restriction and loop diuretics (thiazides add effect, chlorthalidone has shown benefit even in G4).
- **Cardiovascular disease**: a patient with CKD is more likely to die of cardiovascular disease than to reach dialysis. Treat accordingly.
- **Uraemic symptoms** (at eGFR usually < 10, earlier in some): anorexia, nausea, itching, restless legs, fatigue, taste change, pericarditis, encephalopathy, bleeding.

### 3.6 Planning for kidney failure

Discuss options early, at the latest when 5-year KFRE is above 10–20% or eGFR approaches 20:

- **Pre-emptive kidney transplantation** (living donor best): superior survival and quality of life. Refer for assessment before dialysis is needed.
- **Haemodialysis**: in-centre or home. Needs vascular access (arteriovenous fistula preferred, created months before need).
- **Peritoneal dialysis**: home-based, preserves residual function; contraindicated in extensive abdominal surgery or severe hernias.
- **Conservative (non-dialytic) kidney care**: a legitimate, planned pathway for frail patients, those with high comorbidity, or those who prefer it; focuses on symptom control, anticipatory planning and avoiding burdensome hospitalisation. Outcomes for very frail older adults with multimorbidity are not clearly better on dialysis.

Make the choice a shared decision. Use the diagnostic skills of this manual: the question is not just "what is the eGFR" but "what does this person value, and what trajectory are they on".

## 4. Glomerular diseases

### 4.1 Clinical syndromes

| | Nephrotic syndrome | Nephritic syndrome |
|---|---|---|
| Hallmarks | Proteinuria > 3.5 g/day (PCR > 300 mg/mmol), albumin < 30 g/L, oedema, hyperlipidaemia | Haematuria (red cell casts, dysmorphic RBC), hypertension, oedema, reduced GFR, mild-moderate proteinuria |
| Onset | Insidious | Often abrupt |
| Urine | Frothy, heavy protein, oval fat bodies | "Cola" or smoky, red cell casts |
| Typical causes | Minimal change, FSGS, membranous, diabetic nephropathy, amyloid | IgA nephropathy, post-infectious GN, lupus nephritis, ANCA vasculitis, anti-GBM disease, MPGN |

**Rapidly progressive glomerulonephritis (RPGN)**: nephritic picture with loss of GFR over days to weeks, crescents on biopsy. It is a **nephrological emergency**: delay in diagnosis costs kidneys. Causes: ANCA-associated vasculitis (most common, often with pulmonary haemorrhage), anti-GBM disease (Goodpasture), immune-complex GN (lupus, IgA, cryoglobulinaemia, post-infectious).

### 4.2 Complications of nephrotic syndrome

- **Oedema** (including periorbital, ascites, effusions) from sodium retention.
- **Venous thrombosis** (renal vein, deep vein, pulmonary embolism) due to urinary loss of antithrombin and other factors; risk is highest in membranous nephropathy with albumin < 25 g/L. Consider prophylactic anticoagulation in selected patients.
- **Infection** (loss of immunoglobulins; cellulitis, peritonitis, pneumococcal sepsis).
- **Hyperlipidaemia and accelerated atherosclerosis**.
- **AKI** from hypovolaemia or tubular injury.

### 4.3 Investigations in suspected glomerular disease

- Urinalysis with microscopy, ACR/PCR, creatinine, eGFR, albumin, lipids.
- Complement C3/C4 (low in post-infectious GN, lupus, MPGN, C3 glomerulopathy), ANA, anti-dsDNA, ANCA (PR3/MPO), anti-GBM, anti-PLA2R antibody, hepatitis B/C, HIV, syphilis, cryoglobulins, serum free light chains and electrophoresis (amyloid, myeloma), HbA1c.
- Renal ultrasound.
- **Renal biopsy** is the definitive investigation in most adults with nephrotic syndrome (except children with typical minimal change, and diabetic nephropathy with the classic picture), unexplained nephritic syndrome, rapidly falling GFR, or unexplained proteinuria > 1 g/day. Contraindications: uncontrolled hypertension, bleeding diathesis, solitary kidney (relative), small kidneys (relative), uncooperative patient.

### 4.4 Key glomerular diseases

**Minimal change disease (MCD).** Leading cause of nephrotic syndrome in children (70–90%) and a cause in about 10–15% of adults. Abrupt oedema, selective proteinuria, normal BP and eGFR, bland sediment. Triggers: NSAIDs, lymphoma (Hodgkin), atopy, infection. In children, a trial of prednisolone (60 mg/m²/day) for 4–6 weeks without biopsy is standard; >90% remit. Relapses are common; steroid-sparing agents (cyclophosphamide, tacrolimus, rituximab) for frequent relapsers.

**Focal segmental glomerulosclerosis (FSGS).** Primary (circulating factor, steroid-responsive in some), secondary (obesity, reflux nephropathy, HIV, heroin, bisphosphonates, reduced nephron mass), and genetic (APOL1, NPHS2, etc.). Heavy proteinuria, hypertension, often declining GFR. Treatment of primary FSGS is steroids; secondary FSGS is treated by addressing the cause plus RAS blockade and SGLT2 inhibition.

**Membranous nephropathy.** Common cause of nephrotic syndrome in non-diabetic adults, especially men over 50. About 70–80% of primary cases carry **anti-PLA2R antibodies** (a serological diagnosis is possible in some without biopsy). Secondary causes: hepatitis B, lupus, malignancy (lung, GI, prostate; screen age-appropriately), NSAIDs, gold/penicillamine. Roughly a third remit spontaneously, a third remain stable, a third progress. Supportive therapy first (RAS blockade, SGLT2i, diuretics, statins, anticoagulation if low albumin), immunosuppression (rituximab, cyclophosphamide with steroids, calcineurin inhibitors) in those at high risk of progression.

**IgA nephropathy.** The commonest primary glomerulonephritis worldwide. Typical presentation: macroscopic haematuria **during or 1–2 days after** an upper respiratory tract infection (synpharyngitic), in a young adult; or persistent microscopic haematuria with proteinuria. Biopsy shows mesangial IgA deposits. Prognosis: roughly 20–40% develop kidney failure in 20 years; risk is higher with proteinuria > 1 g/day, hypertension, reduced GFR and crescents. Supportive care (RAS blockade, SGLT2i, BP control) is the foundation; steroids, budesonide targeted-release formulations and newer agents for persistent proteinuria in selected patients. Henoch–Schönlein purpura (IgA vasculitis) shares the pathology and involves skin, joints, gut and kidney.

**Post-infectious glomerulonephritis.** Classically after Group A streptococcal pharyngitis (1–3 weeks) or skin infection (3–6 weeks), more often in children. Cola-coloured urine, oedema, hypertension, low C3 that returns to normal in 6–8 weeks. Supportive care (salt restriction, diuretics, antihypertensives). Most children recover fully; adults and the elderly are at greater risk of persistent disease. In adults consider infection-related GN in the context of staphylococcal endocarditis, abscess or shunt infection.

**Lupus nephritis.** Occurs in about 30–60% of adults with SLE. Any proteinuria, haematuria, or rising creatinine in a patient with lupus warrants evaluation, and biopsy classifies the lesion (class III/IV proliferative and class V membranous need immunosuppression: mycophenolate or cyclophosphamide with steroids, plus belimumab or voclosporin in selected patients; hydroxychloroquine for all).

**ANCA-associated vasculitis.** Granulomatosis with polyangiitis (PR3-ANCA), microscopic polyangiitis (MPO-ANCA), eosinophilic granulomatosis with polyangiitis. Systemic features: fever, weight loss, sinus disease, haemoptysis, purpura, mononeuritis multiplex. Renal biopsy: pauci-immune necrotising crescentic GN. Treatment: high-dose steroids plus rituximab or cyclophosphamide; plasma exchange in selected cases (severe renal failure, alveolar haemorrhage). Start treatment promptly once the diagnosis is likely.

**Anti-GBM disease.** Rare, severe; haemoptysis plus rapidly progressive GN in young men and older women. Anti-GBM antibodies and linear IgG staining. Urgent plasma exchange, steroids, and cyclophosphamide; dialysis-dependent patients with 100% crescents rarely recover function.

**Diabetic kidney disease.** Typically after 10+ years of type 1 diabetes (earlier in type 2 as it may be present at diagnosis), with progression from hyperfiltration through microalbuminuria to overt proteinuria and then falling GFR, usually accompanied by retinopathy. Management: glycaemic and blood pressure control, ACE-I/ARB, SGLT2i, finerenone, GLP-1 RA, statin, smoking cessation.

**Amyloidosis and myeloma kidney.** Consider in any older adult with nephrotic syndrome without diabetes, or with unexplained AKI and anaemia, hypercalcaemia, or bone pain. Check serum free light chains and protein electrophoresis.

## 5. Tubulo-interstitial, cystic and other parenchymal diseases

### 5.1 Chronic tubulo-interstitial disease

Slowly progressive loss of function with little proteinuria, small amounts of albuminuria, tubular defects (renal tubular acidosis, polyuria, salt wasting), and bland sediment. Causes: analgesic and NSAID use, lithium, calcineurin inhibitors, reflux nephropathy, obstruction, sarcoidosis, Sjögren syndrome, heavy metals, aristolochic acid, chronic hypokalaemia, hypercalcaemia, urate.

### 5.2 Autosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease (ADPKD)

Prevalence about 1 in 1,000. Mutations in *PKD1* (more severe) or *PKD2*. Presents with hypertension, flank pain, haematuria (cyst haemorrhage), UTI or cyst infection, kidney stones, palpable kidneys, and an enlarging liver (polycystic liver). Extrarenal: intracranial aneurysms (about 5–10%, higher with family history of aneurysm or subarachnoid haemorrhage), mitral valve prolapse, diverticular disease, hernias.

Diagnosis: ultrasound with age-specific criteria (e.g., for a person at 50% risk aged 15–39: ≥ 3 cysts in total in both kidneys; 40–59: ≥ 2 cysts in each kidney; ≥ 60: ≥ 4 cysts in each kidney). MRI total kidney volume and Mayo imaging classification (class 1C–1E: rapid progressors) predict outcomes. Genetic testing for atypical cases and living-donor screening.

Management: strict BP control (ACE-I/ARB), high water intake (unless advanced CKD or hyponatraemia), moderate salt restriction, treat UTI with lipophilic antibiotics that penetrate cysts (fluoroquinolones, trimethoprim–sulfamethoxazole) for cyst infection, and **tolvaptan** in selected rapidly progressing adults (liver monitoring required). Screening for intracranial aneurysm in those with a family history or high-risk occupation (pilots).

### 5.3 Other conditions worth recognising

- **Renal artery stenosis / renovascular hypertension**: resistant hypertension, abrupt creatinine rise on ACE-I/ARB, flash pulmonary oedema, asymmetric kidneys, abdominal bruit. In atherosclerotic disease, medical therapy is as good as stenting for most; fibromuscular dysplasia (young woman) responds to angioplasty.
- **Hepatorenal syndrome**: AKI in cirrhosis with ascites after exclusion of other causes; treated with albumin plus terlipressin (or noradrenaline) and consideration of liver transplantation.
- **Cardiorenal syndrome**: reciprocal dysfunction of heart and kidney; treat congestion carefully (a small rise in creatinine during effective decongestion is acceptable).
- **Sickle cell nephropathy**, **HIV nephropathy**, **Fabry disease**, **Alport syndrome** (haematuria, sensorineural deafness, ocular lesions, X-linked in most), **thin basement membrane nephropathy** (benign familial haematuria) should be considered when the pattern fits.

## 6. Urinary tract infection

### 6.1 Classification

- **Uncomplicated cystitis**: non-pregnant, premenopausal adult woman without structural or functional abnormality.
- **Complicated UTI**: men, pregnancy, children, catheters, obstruction, stones, diabetes (poor control), immunosuppression, recent instrumentation, health-care associated, or anatomical/functional abnormality.
- **Pyelonephritis**: upper tract infection with systemic features.
- **Recurrent UTI**: ≥ 2 episodes in 6 months or ≥ 3 in 12 months.
- **Asymptomatic bacteriuria**: positive culture without symptoms. **Do not screen or treat** except in pregnancy and before urological procedures that breach the mucosa.

### 6.2 Clinical diagnosis

**Cystitis**: dysuria, frequency, urgency, suprapubic pain, with or without haematuria. In women with typical symptoms and no vaginal discharge or irritation, the probability of UTI is about 80–90%. The combination of dysuria and frequency without vaginal discharge is highly predictive. Dipstick positive for nitrite or leukocyte esterase supports the diagnosis; a negative dipstick in a woman with strong symptoms lowers the probability to about 20%, and urine culture should be considered if symptoms persist.

**Pyelonephritis**: fever, rigors, flank pain, costovertebral angle tenderness, nausea, vomiting, with or without lower urinary tract symptoms. Check for sepsis, obstruction, and in pregnancy, which has a higher risk of preterm labour and sepsis.

**In men**: UTI is by definition complicated; consider prostatitis (perineal pain, urinary symptoms, fever, tender boggy prostate: avoid vigorous massage in acute infection), epididymitis, and urethritis from sexually transmitted infection.

**In older adults and the cognitively impaired**: bacteriuria is present in up to 10–50% of residents of long-term care. A positive dipstick in an elderly patient with non-specific features such as confusion, falls or reduced intake is very often asymptomatic bacteriuria. **Look for another cause** (see chapter 61), and treat as UTI only when there are localising urinary symptoms, fever with no other source, or systemic signs of sepsis.

### 6.3 Investigations

- **Urine culture** before antibiotics in: suspected pyelonephritis, men, pregnancy, treatment failure, recurrent infection, catheterised patients with symptoms, and haematuria or risk factors for resistance.
- **Blood cultures** in pyelonephritis with systemic illness.
- **Imaging**: ultrasound (or CT) in pyelonephritis that fails to improve within 48–72 hours, in men, sepsis, history of stones, diabetes with severe illness, or suspicion of obstruction/abscess/emphysematous pyelonephritis.
- Pregnancy test in women of reproductive age.

### 6.4 Treatment (examples; follow local antibiogram)

| Condition | Typical first-line options | Duration |
|---|---|---|
| Uncomplicated cystitis | Nitrofurantoin 100 mg modified-release twice daily (avoid if eGFR < 45 in many guidelines; check local rules), or fosfomycin 3 g single dose, or pivmecillinam, or trimethoprim if susceptibility is known or local resistance < 20% | 3–5 days (single dose for fosfomycin) |
| Cystitis in pregnancy | Cefalexin, amoxicillin–clavulanate, or nitrofurantoin in 2nd trimester (avoid trimethoprim in the first trimester and nitrofurantoin near term); guided by culture | 7 days |
| Pyelonephritis, outpatient | Oral ciprofloxacin or levofloxacin (if local resistance low; fluoroquinolone safety warnings apply), cefpodoxime, or amoxicillin–clavulanate, per susceptibility | 7 days for fluoroquinolone, 10–14 days for beta-lactams |
| Pyelonephritis, inpatient or sepsis | IV ceftriaxone or cefotaxime, or piperacillin–tazobactam, adding an aminoglycoside in severe sepsis; carbapenem where ESBL organisms are likely (previous ESBL, recent travel to endemic regions, recent broad-spectrum use) | 7–14 days total; IV-to-oral switch when afebrile and improving |
| Male UTI (without prostatitis) | Per culture; trimethoprim or fluoroquinolone commonly | 7–14 days |
| Acute bacterial prostatitis | Fluoroquinolone or trimethoprim–sulfamethoxazole (good prostatic penetration) | 2–4 weeks (up to 6 for chronic) |

Symptom relief: fluids, paracetamol, NSAIDs in short courses if renal function is normal. Delayed prescribing with "back-up" antibiotics is acceptable for mild cystitis in selected women; ibuprofen alone resolves symptoms in a proportion of young women, though with a higher risk of progression to pyelonephritis.

### 6.5 Recurrent UTI

Look for modifiable factors: incomplete emptying, post-menopausal atrophy (vaginal oestrogen reduces recurrence), diabetes, stones, spermicide or diaphragm use, constipation, catheter. Prevention: hydration, post-coital voiding, vaginal oestrogen, methenamine hippurate (equivalent to low-dose antibiotic prophylaxis in a randomised trial), cranberry products (modest effect), D-mannose (limited evidence), and low-dose antibiotic prophylaxis when other measures fail. Cystoscopy and imaging are warranted for recurrent infection with haematuria, men, suspicion of stones or structural abnormality, or an unusual organism (Proteus suggests urease-producing stone).

### 6.6 Catheter-associated UTI

Do not send urine cultures from asymptomatic catheterised patients. Symptoms are fever, suprapubic or flank tenderness, delirium, new haematuria. Change the catheter (if in place > 2 weeks) before obtaining culture and starting antibiotics, treat for 7 days (14 days if delayed response, 5 days in selected women who improve quickly), and remove the catheter as soon as it is no longer necessary.

## 7. Urinary stones and renal colic

### 7.1 Presentation

Sudden, severe, colicky flank pain radiating to the groin or testis/labium, writhing and unable to find a comfortable position (in contrast to peritonitis, where the patient lies still), nausea, vomiting, haematuria (microscopic haematuria is present in about 85%; absent in a minority, especially with complete obstruction).

The differential includes: ruptured abdominal aortic aneurysm (older patients, hypotension, pulsatile mass: **always consider in a first presentation of "renal colic" after age 60**), appendicitis, diverticulitis, testicular torsion, ovarian torsion, ectopic pregnancy, pyelonephritis, biliary colic, bowel obstruction, musculoskeletal pain, herpes zoster before the rash.

### 7.2 Investigations

- Urinalysis, pregnancy test, creatinine, electrolytes, calcium, urate, FBC, CRP.
- **Non-contrast low-dose CT** is the investigation of choice (sensitivity and specificity > 95%). Ultrasound is first-line in pregnancy and children, and is increasingly used first in young adults with point-of-care ultrasound (hydronephrosis, ureteric jets); a combination with plain film (KUB) helps follow radio-opaque stones. Uric acid stones are radiolucent on X-ray but visible on CT.
- Stone analysis after passage or removal, plus a metabolic evaluation (24-hour urine, serum calcium, PTH, urate) for recurrent or high-risk stone formers.

### 7.3 Management

- **Analgesia**: NSAIDs (diclofenac 75 mg IM/IV or oral; ketorolac; ibuprofen) are first-line if renal function is adequate and no contraindication. Add paracetamol and, if needed, opioids. Antiemetics.
- **Medical expulsive therapy**: alpha-blockers (tamsulosin 0.4 mg daily) for distal ureteric stones > 5 mm; benefit is modest and trial results vary, so shared decision-making is appropriate. Most stones < 5 mm pass spontaneously (about 90%), 5–10 mm about 50%, and > 10 mm rarely.
- **Hydration**: encourage normal fluid intake; aggressive hydration does not help passage.
- **Urgent decompression (stent or percutaneous nephrostomy) and admission** for: **obstruction plus infection** (fever, raised WCC/CRP, pyuria), solitary or transplant kidney, bilateral obstruction, AKI, intractable pain or vomiting, or sepsis. An infected obstructed system is a urological emergency: untreated, it leads to septic shock; do not rely on antibiotics alone.
- **Definitive treatment** for stones unlikely to pass, large stones, or persistent symptoms: shock wave lithotripsy (SWL), ureteroscopy with laser lithotripsy, percutaneous nephrolithotomy (large renal stones).
- **Follow-up** after any episode: repeat imaging to confirm passage or follow residual fragments, and counsel on prevention.

### 7.4 Prevention

- Fluid intake to produce at least 2.0–2.5 L of urine daily.
- Normal dietary calcium (restriction increases risk), reduce sodium (< 5 g salt), moderate animal protein, avoid excess oxalate-rich foods in hyperoxaluria, avoid high-dose vitamin C supplements.
- Thiazide diuretics (e.g., chlortalidone, indapamide) reduce calcium excretion in recurrent calcium stone formers; potassium citrate for hypocitraturia, uric acid stones (target urine pH 6.0–6.5) and RTA; allopurinol for hyperuricosuria with calcium stones.
- Treat underlying causes: primary hyperparathyroidism, inflammatory bowel disease, bariatric surgery (enteric hyperoxaluria), RTA, cystinuria, infection stones (struvite from urease-producing organisms: complete stone removal is required).

## 8. Urological problems and cancers

### 8.1 Haematuria

**Visible (macroscopic) haematuria** carries a significant risk of malignancy, particularly in people over 40 and smokers (bladder cancer is found in about 10–20%, kidney cancer in up to 5%). **Non-visible haematuria** has a lower yield (about 2–5% malignancy) and is evaluated according to age, smoking and occupational exposure (aromatic amines, dyes, rubber, leather), prior pelvic radiotherapy, cyclophosphamide exposure, and chronic schistosomiasis in endemic areas.

Approach:

1. Confirm: dipstick plus microscopy; exclude menstruation and urinary infection (repeat after treatment), vigorous exercise, myoglobinuria, and dietary or drug discolouration (beetroot, rifampicin, nitrofurantoin, phenazopyridine).
2. Is it **glomerular** (proteinuria, casts, dysmorphic red cells, elevated creatinine, hypertension)? Send to renal investigation.
3. Is it **urological** (painless, clots, normal shaped RBCs)? Investigate: CT urogram or ultrasound plus cystoscopy, per local pathway; most guidelines recommend cystoscopy for all visible haematuria and for non-visible haematuria in patients over 40–60 or with risk factors.
4. Anticoagulants and antiplatelets do not explain haematuria: investigate anyway.

### 8.2 Lower urinary tract symptoms and benign prostatic hyperplasia

LUTS in men are classified as storage (frequency, urgency, nocturia, urgency incontinence), voiding (hesitancy, weak stream, intermittency, straining, terminal dribble), and post-micturition symptoms. They are caused by BPH, overactive bladder, urethral stricture, neurogenic bladder, polyuria (diabetes, diuretics, nocturnal polyuria), infection, bladder stones/tumour, prostate cancer (rarely).

Assessment: validated symptom score (IPSS), frequency–volume chart for 3 days (distinguishes nocturnal polyuria), urinalysis, creatinine, PSA after counselling, digital rectal examination (size, consistency, nodules), post-void residual volume (bladder scan), medication review (diuretics, anticholinergics, decongestants, opioids, antidepressants).

Treatment of BPH: conservative measures (reduce evening fluids and caffeine, timed voiding), alpha-blockers (tamsulosin, alfuzosin: fast onset; orthostatic hypotension and floppy iris syndrome in cataract surgery), 5-alpha reductase inhibitors (finasteride, dutasteride) for prostates > 30–40 mL (reduce volume by about 20–25% and risk of retention and surgery; lower PSA by about 50%), combination therapy for larger prostates, antimuscarinics or beta-3 agonists for storage symptoms if residual volume is low, desmopressin for nocturnal polyuria (monitor sodium), and surgery (TURP, laser enucleation) for refractory symptoms or complications (recurrent retention, recurrent infection, bladder stones, renal impairment, recurrent haematuria).

**Acute urinary retention**: painful, inability to void. Catheterise; record the residual volume (> 800 mL or high pressure retention may produce post-obstructive diuresis); look for precipitants (constipation, drugs, infection, spinal cord compression, cauda equina syndrome). **Painless chronic retention** with overflow incontinence and renal impairment is easy to miss; palpate or scan the bladder in every older man with AKI or CKD.

### 8.3 Prostate cancer and PSA

Prostate cancer is the commonest cancer in men in many countries. Early disease is asymptomatic; advanced disease presents with LUTS, bone pain, spinal cord compression, haematuria, anaemia, weight loss.

**PSA** is organ-specific, not cancer-specific. Raised by BPH, prostatitis, urinary infection, instrumentation, ejaculation (hold 48 hours), cycling (controversial), and age. Approximate age-specific upper limits: 3.0 ng/mL for ages 50–59, 3.5 for 60–69, 4.5 for 70–79 (varies by guideline). Informed decision-making about screening is advised: PSA testing reduces prostate cancer mortality modestly but carries a risk of over-diagnosis and over-treatment.

The modern pathway for raised PSA or abnormal DRE: **multiparametric/biparametric MRI before biopsy**, followed by targeted (and often systematic) biopsy for PI-RADS 3–5 lesions. This reduces unnecessary biopsies and detection of insignificant disease. Risk stratification: ISUP grade group, PSA, clinical stage. Options include active surveillance (low risk), radical prostatectomy or radiotherapy (intermediate and high risk), androgen deprivation therapy (advanced or metastatic disease) with newer agents (abiraterone, enzalutamide, apalutamide, darolutamide, docetaxel).

Biopsy infection (including sepsis) is a risk, especially with transrectal approaches; transperineal biopsy lowers the risk.

### 8.4 Renal cell carcinoma

The classic triad (flank pain, haematuria, palpable mass) is present in fewer than 10%; most tumours are now found incidentally on imaging. Paraneoplastic features: polycythaemia, hypercalcaemia, hypertension, fever, anaemia, Stauffer syndrome (hepatic dysfunction), and a left varicocele that does not empty when supine (tumour thrombus in renal vein). Risk factors: smoking, obesity, hypertension, dialysis, von Hippel–Lindau, family history. Imaging with contrast CT or MRI; treatment depends on stage (partial nephrectomy, ablation, active surveillance for small masses, radical nephrectomy; immunotherapy and targeted therapy for metastatic disease).

### 8.5 Bladder cancer

Painless visible haematuria is the presenting symptom in most. Smoking is the dominant risk factor, then occupational exposures and schistosomiasis (squamous cell carcinoma). Diagnosis by cystoscopy and transurethral resection; 70–75% are non-muscle invasive (high recurrence rate; intravesical BCG or chemotherapy), and muscle-invasive disease needs radical cystectomy or chemoradiation with systemic therapy.

### 8.6 Testicular and scrotal conditions

**Testicular torsion** is a surgical emergency: sudden severe unilateral scrotal pain, nausea and vomiting, high-riding transverse lying testis, absent cremasteric reflex. Peak age is 12–18 years but it occurs at any age, including neonates. Salvage rates are > 90% within 6 hours and fall rapidly after 12 hours. **Do not delay surgical exploration for imaging if the diagnosis is clinically likely.** Doppler ultrasound can help in equivocal cases but a normal Doppler does not exclude torsion (intermittent torsion).

**Epididymo-orchitis**: gradual onset, fever, tender epididymis, often reduced pain on elevation (Prehn sign is unreliable). In sexually active men under 35 consider *Chlamydia trachomatis* and *Neisseria gonorrhoeae*; in older men, enteric organisms and urinary tract pathology. In children, consider mumps (viral orchitis).

**Testicular cancer**: the most common solid tumour in men aged 15–40. A painless hard lump or swelling in one testis; any solid intratesticular mass is a cancer until proven otherwise. Do not biopsy through the scrotum. Check tumour markers (AFP, β-hCG, LDH) before orchiectomy and ultrasound. Highly curable, including metastatic disease.

**Other**: hydrocele (transilluminates), varicocele ("bag of worms", more commonly left-sided; a sudden-onset right varicocele or one that does not decompress supine needs imaging), inguinal hernia, spermatocele, Fournier gangrene (necrotising fasciitis of the perineum: diabetes, SGLT2 inhibitor, severe pain out of proportion, crepitus; urgent surgical debridement and broad-spectrum antibiotics).

### 8.7 Erectile dysfunction

Erectile dysfunction is both a symptom and a **marker of cardiovascular disease**: it precedes coronary events by an average of 2–5 years. Assess cardiovascular risk, blood pressure, glucose, lipids, testosterone (morning, repeated if low), prolactin when indicated, medications (antidepressants, antihypertensives, 5-ARIs, opioids), alcohol and drug use, psychological factors, and relationship issues. Phosphodiesterase-5 inhibitors (sildenafil, tadalafil, vardenafil) are first-line; **absolute contraindication with nitrates** (and caution with alpha-blockers). Address risk factors; counsel on unrealistic expectations.

## 9. Drug dosing and nephrotoxins in renal impairment

### 9.1 General rules

1. Know the patient's GFR (use the equation your formulary recommends) and whether it is stable. **In AKI, eGFR is unreliable: assume lower function than the number suggests when creatinine is rising.**
2. Identify drugs that are **renally cleared with a narrow therapeutic index** (digoxin, lithium, aminoglycosides, vancomycin, enoxaparin, DOACs with renal clearance, methotrexate, gabapentin and pregabalin, some antiepileptics): reduce dose or extend interval and consider drug level monitoring.
3. Identify drugs that are **nephrotoxic**: NSAIDs, aminoglycosides, amphotericin, vancomycin (with piperacillin–tazobactam), colistin, cisplatin, calcineurin inhibitors, lithium (chronic), contrast, tenofovir disoproxil, zoledronic acid, aciclovir (crystal nephropathy if rapid IV bolus), sulfonamides, methotrexate, high-dose PPIs.
4. Avoid **combinations** that reduce GFR: ACE-I/ARB + diuretic + NSAID ("triple whammy").
5. For metformin: full dose if eGFR ≥ 45; review dose at 30–44; stop if < 30 or during acute illness with hypovolaemia, sepsis, or contrast in high-risk patients.
6. For DOACs, apixaban, rivaroxaban, edoxaban and dabigatran have different renal handling; dabigatran is the most dependent on the kidney (80%). Use the label's criteria (age, weight, creatinine) rather than eGFR alone.
7. Opioids: morphine and codeine metabolites accumulate in CKD; prefer fentanyl, oxycodone (with caution) or buprenorphine.
8. For antibiotics, give the first dose at **full** dose (loading dose is not reduced), then adjust.
9. Do not stop an essential drug only because eGFR is low: weigh risk and benefit. Do not continue a harmful drug (such as NSAIDs) "because the patient always takes it".

### 9.2 Sick day rules

Patients with CKD or on RAS blockers/diuretics/SGLT2i/metformin/NSAIDs should be taught: if acutely unwell with vomiting, diarrhoea or fever and not drinking, **temporarily stop** these medicines and restart 24–48 hours after returning to normal eating and drinking. If symptoms persist beyond 48 hours seek medical advice.

## Worked cases

### Case 1: The "triple whammy" in an older woman

**Presentation.** Mrs R., 78, is brought by her daughter after 3 days of vomiting and diarrhoea. She has hypertension and osteoarthritis. Medications: ramipril 10 mg, bendroflumethiazide 2.5 mg, ibuprofen 400 mg three times daily for knee pain. BP 92/54 lying, 78/48 standing, pulse 108, JVP not visible, dry mucous membranes, no oedema, chest clear.

**Data.** Creatinine 238 µmol/L (2.7 mg/dL); baseline 82 µmol/L three months ago. Urea 24 mmol/L (BUN about 67 mg/dL), potassium 5.9 mmol/L, bicarbonate 16 mmol/L. Urinalysis: trace protein, no blood. Urine sodium 12 mmol/L. Bladder scan: 60 mL. Ultrasound: normal-sized kidneys, no hydronephrosis. ECG: peaked T waves.

**Reasoning.** Creatinine rose about 2.9-fold: **KDIGO stage 2 AKI**. Pre-renal picture (volume depletion with low urine sodium, bland sediment, normal ultrasound), compounded by medicines that remove autoregulation of glomerular pressure (ACE-I removes efferent constriction, NSAID removes afferent dilation, diuretic depletes volume). Hyperkalaemia with ECG changes is the immediate threat.

**Action.** IV calcium gluconate 10 mL of 10% over 5–10 minutes (repeat if ECG changes persist), insulin–dextrose, nebulised salbutamol. Isotonic balanced crystalloid in 250–500 mL boluses with reassessment, aiming at a euvolaemic state and urine output > 0.5 mL/kg/h. Stop ramipril, bendroflumethiazide and ibuprofen. Catheter for hourly urine output while unstable. Repeat electrolytes within 2–4 hours. On day 4 creatinine 104 µmol/L. Before discharge: explain "sick day rules", recommend paracetamol and topical NSAID for the knee, restart ramipril once stable (check electrolytes at 1–2 weeks), do not restart the thiazide unless clearly needed.

**Lessons.** Medication review is the quickest diagnostic step in AKI. The three drugs together did the damage that none would have done alone.

### Case 2: The man who "passes small amounts"

**Presentation.** Mr T., 72, is seen by his GP for fatigue, nausea and nocturia ×4 for months. He dribbles urine at night. Creatinine 412 µmol/L (4.7 mg/dL), eGFR 12, potassium 5.4, haemoglobin 98 g/L. BP 168/96. Palpable, dull suprapubic mass reaching the umbilicus. Rectal examination: smooth, enlarged prostate.

**Reasoning.** Chronic high-pressure retention from BPH with obstructive uropathy and AKI on CKD. The bladder is palpable and the history of poor stream, nocturia and overflow suggests the diagnosis. A bladder scan shows 1,400 mL. Renal ultrasound: bilateral hydronephrosis and thinned cortex (some chronicity).

**Action.** Admit. Urethral catheter and record hourly urine output. **Watch for post-obstructive diuresis** (> 200 mL/h for 2 hours or > 3 L/day): replace urine losses with IV fluids (about 50–75% of urine output volume) while monitoring sodium, potassium and magnesium, balancing the risk of dehydration against the risk of prolonging the diuresis. Creatinine falls over 5 days to 168 µmol/L (new baseline CKD G4). Nephrology and urology follow-up: alpha-blocker, 5-ARI, later TURP or laser enucleation. BP controlled with amlodipine first, ACE-I introduced later with monitoring. Anaemia: check iron stores, replace as needed.

**Lessons.** Always examine for a palpable bladder and the prostate in unexplained renal failure in an older man; an ultrasound within 24 hours is mandatory. Post-obstructive diuresis is common after relieving chronic obstruction.

### Case 3: Cola-coloured urine after a sore throat

**Presentation.** Daniel, 22, has dark red urine two days after a sore throat began. No dysuria, no fever. BP 148/92. Mild periorbital oedema. Creatinine 118 µmol/L (previously 78). Urine dipstick: blood +++, protein ++. Microscopy: dysmorphic red cells and red cell casts. ACR 120 mg/mmol (about 1,060 mg/g). C3 normal. Antistreptolysin O titre normal.

**Reasoning.** The nephritic picture appeared **during** the infection (1–2 days), not after a 1–3 week latency. Normal complement argues against post-streptococcal GN. The synpharyngitic pattern suggests **IgA nephropathy**. Because creatinine is rising, the proteinuria is substantial (> 1 g/day) and the patient is young, a biopsy is indicated.

**Action.** Renal biopsy: mesangial IgA deposits, mesangial proliferation and a minority of cellular crescents (Oxford MEST-C score E0 M1 S0 T0 C1). Start ramipril titrated to the maximum tolerated dose, BP target < 125/75, SGLT2 inhibitor, salt restriction. At 3 months, proteinuria still > 1 g/day on optimised supportive care: consider a 6-month corticosteroid regimen or targeted-release budesonide, after discussing risks and benefits. Monitor eGFR, ACR and BP every 3 months; counsel on fertility and pregnancy planning.

**Lessons.** Timing of haematuria relative to infection helps separate IgA nephropathy (days) from post-infectious GN (weeks). In IgA nephropathy, supportive care and risk-stratification matter more than any single drug.

### Case 4: A flank pain that is not a stone

**Presentation.** Mr K., 67, hypertensive and a smoker, is sent to the emergency department with "renal colic": sudden left flank pain radiating to the groin, vomiting, microscopic haematuria on dipstick. He is pale and sweating, writhing, BP 98/60, pulse 112. A pulsatile, tender mass in the epigastrium.

**Reasoning.** First presentation of "renal colic" in a man over 60 with hypotension is a **ruptured or leaking abdominal aortic aneurysm** until proven otherwise. Urine dipstick haematuria occurs with AAA (local inflammation) and is not specific for stones.

**Action.** Activate the vascular/major haemorrhage pathway. Two large-bore IV lines, crossmatch, **permissive hypotension** (systolic about 70–90 mmHg, avoiding excess fluid), immediate bedside ultrasound (aorta > 3 cm) or CT angiography if stable enough, and immediate vascular surgical or endovascular repair. Avoid IV NSAIDs (kidney and bleeding risk).

**Lessons.** "Renal colic" is a diagnosis of exclusion in older patients and in those with haemodynamic instability. Bedside ultrasound of the aorta in older patients with flank pain saves lives.

### Case 5: Obstruction plus infection

**Presentation.** Ms L., 34, with left renal colic for 24 hours, now has temperature 39.4 °C, rigors, heart rate 122, BP 94/58, lactate 3.8 mmol/L. Dipstick: leukocytes +++, nitrite positive. CT KUB: 9 mm obstructing left proximal ureteric stone with hydronephrosis and perinephric stranding.

**Reasoning.** An infected obstructed system with sepsis. Antibiotics cannot sterilise a closed, infected space.

**Action.** Sepsis bundle: blood and urine cultures, IV fluids, IV broad-spectrum antibiotics within the hour (ceftriaxone, or piperacillin–tazobactam or a carbapenem per local resistance and history), analgesia, and **urgent decompression** by retrograde ureteric stent or percutaneous nephrostomy (within hours). Definitive stone treatment is deferred until the infection has been treated (usually 1–2 weeks of antibiotics). Metabolic stone workup follows.

**Lessons.** Fever plus an obstructing stone is an emergency. Decompression is the key, not more antibiotics.

### Case 6: Heavy proteinuria in a middle-aged man

**Presentation.** Mr S., 58, notices swelling of both legs and frothy urine over 4 weeks. BP 134/82, pitting oedema to the thighs, ascites on ultrasound. Albumin 18 g/L, ACR 520 mg/mmol (about 4,600 mg/g), PCR 680 mg/mmol. Creatinine 96 µmol/L, eGFR 80. Cholesterol 9.8 mmol/L. HbA1c 36 mmol/mol (5.4%). No diabetes. D-dimer high, left leg swollen more than the right.

**Reasoning.** Nephrotic syndrome in a non-diabetic older man: membranous nephropathy is high on the list, together with minimal change, FSGS, amyloid and secondary causes (hepatitis B/C, HIV, lupus, malignancy, drugs). Unilateral leg swelling with high D-dimer in the nephrotic state suggests DVT.

**Action.** Leg Doppler: left femoral DVT. Start anticoagulation (low molecular weight heparin, adjusting for renal function and low albumin, then warfarin or a DOAC). Serology: anti-PLA2R antibody positive at high titre, hepatitis B/C and HIV negative, ANA negative, free light chains normal. Age-appropriate cancer screening (chest X-ray, colonoscopy as indicated, PSA after discussion). Given high anti-PLA2R titre and a classic presentation, biopsy may be omitted in some centres, or performed to assess chronicity (in this case, done: stage II membranous). Supportive therapy: ACE-I, SGLT2 inhibitor, loop diuretic with salt restriction, statin. Because the risk of progression is high (heavy proteinuria > 8 g/day, albumin < 25 g/L, high titre), rituximab is started after discussion with nephrology.

**Lessons.** A nephrotic patient is a thrombotic patient. Always search for secondary causes of membranous nephropathy, particularly malignancy in older patients.

### Case 7: "Recurrent UTI" that was not

**Presentation.** Mrs A., 81, in long-term care, has had five antibiotic courses in a year for "urinary tract infection", each prompted by a positive dipstick in the context of confusion or poor appetite. She has no dysuria, fever, or suprapubic tenderness. Three of the cultures grew *E. coli*, one *Klebsiella*, one mixed growth. She now has *C. difficile* diarrhoea.

**Reasoning.** This is likely **asymptomatic bacteriuria** with unnecessary treatment: dipsticks are positive in most older women in care, and the confusion and anorexia had other causes (constipation, dehydration, medication, early dementia progression). The harms of overtreatment are real: *C. difficile*, resistant organisms, drug interactions, and diversion from the real cause.

**Action.** Treat the *C. difficile* per local guideline. Reframe urinary testing: culture only when there are localising symptoms (dysuria, new frequency or urgency, suprapubic pain, new incontinence), fever > 37.8 °C with no other source, or signs of sepsis. When delirium or reduced intake occurs, perform a systematic search (hydration, constipation, medications, pain, urinary retention, hypoxia, infection elsewhere) and observe. If true recurrent symptomatic infections occur, consider vaginal oestrogen, methenamine hippurate, and review of post-void residual.

**Lessons.** Treating a result is not treating a patient. Stewardship starts with not sending the urine specimen.

## Common pitfalls

1. **Trusting a normal creatinine** in a frail, sarcopenic or malnourished patient. Estimate GFR with the awareness of muscle mass; use cystatin C when it matters.
2. **Using eGFR in AKI**, which is not at steady state. Creatinine rising means the true GFR is lower than reported.
3. **Missing obstruction.** Failing to scan the bladder and kidneys in AKI; overlooking painless retention in older men.
4. **Ignoring the medication list**: ACE-I, ARB, NSAID, diuretic, metformin, SGLT2i, trimethoprim, lithium, contrast.
5. **Over-aggressive fluids** in patients with cardiac or renal failure and established oedema; pre-renal AKI needs volume only if the patient is hypovolaemic.
6. **Stopping an ACE inhibitor too soon** after a small creatinine rise (up to 30%) in CKD; conversely continuing it in volume depletion.
7. **Treating asymptomatic bacteriuria** or "dipstick positive confusion".
8. **Missing infected obstruction** and treating with antibiotics alone.
9. **Calling a first "renal colic" in an older patient a stone** without excluding AAA.
10. **Delaying scrotal exploration for imaging** when torsion is likely.
11. **Dismissing haematuria on anticoagulants**; investigating it is mandatory.
12. **Using urine dipstick protein** to screen for CKD in diabetes, instead of ACR.
13. **Missing myeloma** in an older patient with AKI, anaemia and a bland urinalysis (dipstick protein is negative with light chains).
14. **Starting dialysis too late for planning purposes**, or dialysing a frail patient without discussing conservative care.
15. **Giving full dose of renally cleared drugs** (gabapentin, enoxaparin, antibiotics, digoxin) in impaired GFR.

## Key points

- Diagnose AKI by creatinine and urine output criteria; stage it; look for the cause in three compartments; scan for obstruction; stop nephrotoxins; treat hyperkalaemia early.
- CKD is classified by cause, GFR and albuminuria. ACR is as important as eGFR. Management rests on blood pressure control, RAS blockade, SGLT2 inhibitors, statins, avoiding nephrotoxins and addressing complications; plan kidney replacement or conservative care well ahead.
- Nephrotic and nephritic syndromes call for urine microscopy, serology and often biopsy. RPGN is an emergency.
- Treat symptomatic UTI with short, targeted courses; do not treat asymptomatic bacteriuria (except pregnancy and before urological procedures); remember that confusion alone is rarely UTI.
- Obstruction plus infection needs decompression, not antibiotics alone.
- Visible haematuria needs a urological work-up; PSA should be interpreted with MRI-guided pathways and informed discussion.
- Testicular torsion is a clinical diagnosis: operate early.
- Renal dosing and sick-day rules prevent preventable harm.

## Self-assessment

**Questions**

1. A 29-year-old man with a creatinine rise from 80 to 95 µmol/L two weeks after starting trimethoprim has no other abnormality. What is the most likely explanation and what would you do?
2. A 70-year-old woman presents with creatinine 180 µmol/L (baseline 90 µmol/L one week ago), urine output 20 mL/h for 8 hours, weight 60 kg. Which KDIGO stage?
3. List three features that, in a patient with presumed diabetic kidney disease, should prompt a search for another cause.
4. A 6-year-old has periorbital oedema, albumin 18 g/L, PCR 400 mg/mmol, normal BP and normal creatinine. What is the likely diagnosis and initial treatment?
5. What bedside feature separates renal colic from peritonitis, and why does it matter?
6. A 75-year-old nursing home resident with a positive dipstick, new confusion for 6 hours and no other symptoms: do you start antibiotics? What do you do?
7. Name three indications for urgent urological decompression in a patient with an obstructing stone.
8. A 62-year-old smoker has painless visible haematuria on one occasion, now resolved. Is investigation necessary?
9. A 55-year-old man on lisinopril has creatinine rise from 110 to 170 µmol/L (+55%) two weeks after the dose was doubled. What are the possible explanations and next steps?
10. Which of the following does NOT raise PSA: BPH, urinary infection, ejaculation 24 hours ago, finasteride therapy?

**Answers**

1. Trimethoprim blocks tubular secretion of creatinine, raising it by roughly 10–15% without a change in true GFR. If the rise is within about 15–20% and the patient is well, no action beyond awareness is needed; consider cystatin C if confirmation is necessary and recheck after stopping if there is any doubt.
2. Creatinine ratio 2.0 (stage 2 by creatinine). Urine output: 20 mL/h ÷ 60 kg = 0.33 mL/kg/h (< 0.5 mL/kg/h) for 8 hours = stage 1 by output (needs ≥ 12 hours for stage 2). The highest stage by any criterion applies, so **stage 2** by creatinine.
3. Short duration of diabetes (especially type 1 < 10 years), absence of diabetic retinopathy, rapid fall in eGFR or sudden onset of nephrotic syndrome, active urinary sediment with red cell casts or heavy haematuria, systemic features suggesting vasculitis or lupus, abnormal complement or serology, onset of heavy proteinuria without prior microalbuminuria, family history of renal disease. (Any three.)
4. Minimal change disease. Prednisolone 60 mg/m²/day (maximum 60 mg) for 4–6 weeks followed by tapering, without biopsy; add salt restriction and monitor for relapse and infection.
5. A patient with renal colic writhes and cannot lie still; a patient with peritonitis lies still because movement worsens pain, with guarding and rebound. This separates a ureteric/visceral from a peritoneal process and flags the need for surgical review.
6. No: this is most probably asymptomatic bacteriuria. Search for other causes of delirium (hydration, constipation, retention, medication, hypoxia, infection elsewhere, metabolic abnormalities), take vital signs, examine for sepsis and urinary retention. Treat only if localising urinary symptoms, fever with no other source, or signs of sepsis develop.
7. Obstruction with infection/sepsis; a solitary or transplanted kidney (or bilateral obstruction); AKI. Also intractable pain or vomiting.
8. Yes. Painless visible haematuria in a smoker over 40 has a high malignancy risk, regardless of resolution: cystoscopy and imaging (CT urogram or equivalent).
9. Creatinine rise > 30% after ACE-I escalation suggests renal artery stenosis (bilateral or in a solitary kidney), volume depletion, concurrent NSAID use, or intercurrent illness. Stop or reduce the ACE-I, check potassium and volume status, review medications, repeat creatinine, and image the renal arteries (duplex or CT/MR angiography) if it recurs or if there are suggestive features.
10. Finasteride therapy lowers PSA (by about 50% after 6 months). BPH, infection and recent ejaculation can raise it.
