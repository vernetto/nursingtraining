## Chapter 31. Edema, Swelling and Lumps

### Learning objectives

After studying this chapter you should be able to:

1. Explain the mechanisms of oedema (Starling forces, venous and lymphatic obstruction, capillary permeability, sodium retention) and use them to classify causes.
2. Approach oedema by distribution (generalised vs localised; unilateral vs bilateral), character (pitting vs non-pitting) and tempo (acute vs chronic).
3. Differentiate the major causes of generalised oedema (heart, liver, kidney, albumin loss, drugs) and unilateral leg swelling (DVT, cellulitis, lymphatic and venous obstruction).
4. Recognise angioedema and superior vena cava obstruction as airway-threatening emergencies.
5. Describe a lump systematically (site, size, shape, surface, consistency, tenderness, mobility, fixity, pulsatility, transillumination, reducibility) and use these features to narrow the differential.
6. Evaluate common lumps: neck, thyroid, salivary, groin, scrotal, limb and soft-tissue lumps, and know which warrant urgent assessment for malignancy.
7. Choose investigations (ultrasound, MRI, aspiration, biopsy) in the correct order and avoid pitfalls such as unplanned excision of a possible sarcoma.

# Part A: Oedema

### 31.1 What oedema is

**Oedema** is a palpable increase in interstitial fluid. It becomes clinically evident when interstitial fluid expands by about 2–3 litres (about 10% of the extracellular volume of an adult). It is a **sign, not a diagnosis.** It can be local or generalised, pitting or non-pitting, and its cause may be benign (prolonged standing) or life-threatening (heart failure, pulmonary embolism, nephrotic syndrome).

#### Pathophysiology

Fluid moves between plasma and interstitium according to the **Starling forces:**

- **Capillary hydrostatic pressure** (pushes fluid out): raised by venous obstruction, right heart failure, fluid overload, and arteriolar dilatation (calcium channel blockers).
- **Plasma oncotic pressure** (holds fluid in): reduced by hypoalbuminaemia (nephrotic syndrome, cirrhosis, malnutrition, protein-losing enteropathy).
- **Capillary permeability:** increased in inflammation, sepsis, burns, allergy and angioedema, allowing protein and fluid to leak.
- **Lymphatic drainage:** the lymphatics return excess interstitial fluid and protein; obstruction or damage causes **lymphoedema** (protein-rich oedema, non-pitting in later stages).
- **Renal sodium and water retention** (via the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system, ADH): sustains and amplifies oedema in heart failure, cirrhosis and nephrotic syndrome. This explains why **salt restriction and diuretics** are effective.

### 31.2 Approach to the patient with oedema

#### Five questions

1. **Is it generalised or localised?** Localised oedema suggests a local cause (venous, lymphatic, inflammatory); generalised (anasarca) suggests a systemic cause.
2. **Is it unilateral or bilateral?**
3. **Does it pit?** (Chapter 10: press firmly for 5 seconds; non-pitting suggests lymphoedema, myxoedema, lipoedema, or established chronic venous disease with fibrosis.)
4. **How fast did it develop?** Acute (hours to days): DVT, cellulitis, acute heart failure, angioedema, nephritic syndrome, drugs; chronic (months): venous insufficiency, lymphoedema, chronic heart failure, CKD, cirrhosis, hypothyroidism.
5. **Is there evidence of heart, liver, kidney or thyroid disease, or are drugs implicated?**

#### History

- **Distribution and timing:** ankles at the end of the day (gravitational), morning periorbital puffiness (renal, allergic), sacral oedema in bed-bound patients, abdominal distension (ascites), breathlessness (heart failure, pleural effusions).
- **Associated symptoms:** orthopnoea, PND, exertional dyspnoea (heart failure); jaundice, alcohol history, abdominal swelling (liver); frothy urine, haematuria, reduced urine output (kidney); fatigue, cold intolerance, constipation (hypothyroidism); pain, redness, warmth, fever (cellulitis, DVT); history of cancer, surgery, radiotherapy (lymphoedema); pregnancy (physiological, pre-eclampsia); menstrual pattern (cyclical oedema); prolonged immobility, long travel (DVT).
- **Medications:** **calcium channel blockers (amlodipine, nifedipine), NSAIDs, corticosteroids, thiazolidinediones (pioglitazone), gabapentin and pregabalin, minoxidil, hydralazine, oestrogens, fludrocortisone, MAO inhibitors, antidepressants (mirtazapine), some antineoplastics (docetaxel, imatinib), and IL-2.**
- **Diet and salt intake, alcohol, weight change.**
- **Past history:** heart, liver, kidney, thyroid disease, varicose veins, DVT, filariasis (travel), skin infections.

#### Examination

- **Vital signs, weight, JVP** (key to separating cardiac from non-cardiac causes), **blood pressure.**
- **Distribution and pitting** (Chapter 10); skin changes (venous eczema, haemosiderin, lipodermatosclerosis, ulcers, hyperkeratosis, papillomatosis in lymphoedema), temperature, tenderness, colour.
- **Cardiac:** apex, murmurs, S3, signs of right heart failure (hepatomegaly, ascites).
- **Chest:** crackles, effusions.
- **Abdomen:** ascites (shifting dullness), hepatomegaly, splenomegaly, stigmata of chronic liver disease, masses (pelvic mass causing venous or lymphatic obstruction).
- **Thyroid, face** (periorbital oedema, myxoedema), **lymph nodes.**
- **Calf circumference** (measure 10 cm below the tibial tuberosity); **Stemmer sign** (inability to pinch the skin at the base of the second toe: lymphoedema).
- **Urine dipstick** at the bedside.

### 31.3 Generalised (bilateral) oedema

| Cause | Mechanism | Clues |
|---|---|---|
| **Heart failure** (right or biventricular) | Raised venous pressure, renal sodium and water retention | Raised JVP, hepatojugular reflux, S3, displaced apex, basal crackles, pleural effusions, hepatomegaly, orthopnoea, weight gain, oedema worst at ankles and sacrum |
| **Constrictive pericarditis / restrictive cardiomyopathy** | Impaired ventricular filling | Raised JVP with Kussmaul sign, steep y descent, ascites and oedema disproportionate to dyspnoea, pericardial knock |
| **Cor pulmonale and pulmonary hypertension** | Right heart failure | COPD, sleep apnoea, loud P₂, RV heave |
| **Cirrhosis with portal hypertension** | Hypoalbuminaemia, portal hypertension, splanchnic vasodilatation, sodium retention | Ascites, stigmata of liver disease, jaundice, spider naevi, splenomegaly, low albumin, prolonged INR; oedema often follows ascites |
| **Nephrotic syndrome** | Heavy proteinuria (>3.5 g/day), hypoalbuminaemia (<30 g/L), sodium retention, hyperlipidaemia | Periorbital and generalised oedema, frothy urine, hypercoagulability (renal vein thrombosis), infection risk; causes: minimal change disease (children), membranous nephropathy, FSGS, diabetic nephropathy, amyloidosis, SLE, drugs (NSAIDs) |
| **Nephritic syndrome / acute glomerulonephritis** | Sodium and water retention with reduced GFR | Haematuria (cola-coloured), hypertension, oliguria, periorbital oedema, post-streptococcal in children |
| **Acute or chronic kidney disease** | Reduced sodium excretion | Hypertension, raised creatinine, uraemic features, anaemia |
| **Protein-losing enteropathy, malabsorption** | Hypoalbuminaemia | Diarrhoea, weight loss, coeliac disease, IBD, intestinal lymphangiectasia |
| **Malnutrition (kwashiorkor, severe hypoproteinaemia)** | Low albumin | Children in food insecurity; hair and skin changes; also older adults, alcohol, eating disorders |
| **Severe anaemia** | High-output state | Pallor |
| **Hypothyroidism (myxoedema)** | Accumulation of glycosaminoglycans | **Non-pitting,** periorbital puffiness, dry skin, bradycardia, delayed reflexes, hoarseness |
| **Hyperthyroidism** (pretibial myxoedema in Graves) | Local | Non-pitting pretibial plaques |
| **Drug-induced** | Arteriolar dilatation (CCB), sodium retention (NSAIDs, steroids, glitazones, fludrocortisone), increased permeability (minoxidil) | Temporal relation to the drug; dose-related; bilateral ankle oedema without raised JVP or other signs |
| **Pregnancy** | Physiological sodium retention, venous compression by the uterus | Mild ankle oedema; **sudden or facial oedema with hypertension, proteinuria, headache, visual disturbance suggests pre-eclampsia** |
| **Idiopathic (cyclic) oedema** | Uncertain | Women of reproductive age, fluctuating weight, diurnal variation; diagnosis of exclusion (avoid diuretic overuse) |
| **Capillary leak** | Increased permeability | Sepsis, burns, SIRS, pancreatitis, IL-2 therapy, capillary leak syndrome |
| **Chronic venous insufficiency** | Venous hypertension (often bilateral but asymmetric) | Varicose veins, haemosiderin pigmentation, venous eczema, lipodermatosclerosis, ulceration at the medial malleolus |
| **Dependent oedema of immobility** | Gravity, muscle pump failure | Prolonged sitting, long flights, paralysis, obesity |
| **Lipoedema** | Abnormal subcutaneous fat | Women, symmetric fat deposition in legs, spares feet (cuff), tender, easy bruising |

**Heart, liver, kidney:** The first screen for generalised oedema is JVP and heart examination (cardiac), stigmata and ascites (liver), and urine dipstick (kidney), followed by albumin, creatinine, liver tests, BNP, thyroid function, and echocardiography.

#### Investigations for generalised oedema

| Test | Purpose |
|---|---|
| **Urine dipstick, urine protein:creatinine ratio (uPCR) or albumin:creatinine ratio** | Proteinuria (nephrotic range uPCR >300–350 mg/mmol (≥3 g/day)); haematuria |
| **Serum albumin, creatinine/eGFR, electrolytes** | Hypoalbuminaemia, renal function |
| **Liver function tests, INR** | Cirrhosis, hepatic synthetic function |
| **BNP/NT-proBNP, ECG, chest X-ray, echocardiography** | Heart failure, pericardial disease, valve disease, pulmonary hypertension |
| **TSH** | Hypothyroidism |
| **FBC** | Anaemia |
| **Lipid profile, HbA1c, urine electrophoresis, complement, ANA, hepatitis B/C, HIV** | Nephrotic syndrome work-up; renal biopsy often needed in adults |
| **Abdominal ultrasound, Doppler of hepatic and portal veins** | Cirrhosis, portal hypertension, renal vein thrombosis, ascites |
| **Diagnostic paracentesis** (ascites) | SAAG (serum-ascites albumin gradient) ≥1.1 g/dL indicates portal hypertension; neutrophil count for SBP; cytology |
| **Stool α1-antitrypsin, faecal calprotectin, small bowel imaging** | Protein-losing enteropathy |

#### Management principles

- **Treat the cause:** optimise heart failure therapy; treat nephrotic syndrome and its cause; abstain from alcohol in liver disease; replace thyroid hormone; stop or substitute the offending drug.
- **Dietary sodium restriction** (about 2 g/day sodium) and fluid restriction when hyponatraemia is present.
- **Diuretics:** loop diuretics (furosemide, bumetanide, torsemide) first-line; add thiazide-like diuretic (metolazone) for resistance; **spironolactone** (with or without a loop diuretic) for cirrhotic ascites; aim for gradual weight loss (0.5–1 kg/day, up to 0.5 kg in patients without peripheral oedema with ascites) to avoid hypovolaemia and renal impairment; monitor electrolytes and creatinine.
- **Albumin infusion** in selected patients (large-volume paracentesis, SBP, hepatorenal syndrome).
- **Compression therapy and leg elevation** for venous insufficiency and mild lymphoedema.
- **Skin care** to prevent cellulitis; treat tinea pedis.

#### Drug-induced oedema and prescribing cascades

Amlodipine-related ankle oedema is typically dose-dependent, is not responsive to diuretics, and improves with reducing the dose, switching to another agent (or ACE inhibitor/ARB combination, which reduces it), or stopping. A frequent error: treating it with a loop diuretic, causing hypovolaemia, hypokalaemia, urinary symptoms, and falls (**prescribing cascade**). Always review the medication history first (Chapter 7).

### 31.4 Unilateral leg swelling

Unilateral swelling nearly always has a **local** cause. The two key emergencies are **DVT** and **cellulitis/necrotising infection.**

| Cause | Features |
|---|---|
| **Deep vein thrombosis** | Calf or thigh swelling, pain, tenderness along the deep veins, warmth, pitting oedema, dilated superficial veins; risk factors: immobility, surgery, cancer, pregnancy, hormones, previous VTE, thrombophilia, long travel, catheter |
| **Cellulitis** | Spreading erythema, warmth, tenderness, fever, lymphangitis, skin break (tinea pedis, ulcer, trauma), well-demarcated edge in erysipelas |
| **Ruptured Baker cyst** | Knee joint disease (OA, RA), sudden calf pain and swelling, "pseudo-thrombophlebitis," bruising around the ankle (crescent sign) |
| **Venous obstruction** | Pelvic mass (tumour, fibroids, pregnancy), iliac vein compression (May–Thurner), IVC thrombosis, post-thrombotic syndrome |
| **Lymphatic obstruction** | Lymphoedema (primary or secondary), malignancy in pelvic or inguinal nodes, post-surgical, post-radiotherapy, filariasis, recurrent cellulitis |
| **Trauma, muscle tear, haematoma, fracture** | History, bruising |
| **Compartment syndrome** | Severe pain out of proportion, tense swelling, pain on passive stretch; emergency |
| **Arterial disease with reperfusion** | Post-embolectomy swelling |
| **Chronic venous insufficiency with asymmetry** | Varicose veins, skin changes |
| **Complex regional pain syndrome** | Pain, swelling, colour change after injury |
| **Superficial thrombophlebitis** | Tender red cord along a superficial vein |
| **Gout, septic arthritis, cellulitis of the foot** | Joint involvement |

#### Evaluating suspected DVT

1. **Pre-test probability** with the **Wells score for DVT** (Chapter 70): active cancer (+1), paralysis or recent immobilisation of the leg (+1), recent bedridden ≥3 days or major surgery within 12 weeks (+1), localised tenderness along deep veins (+1), entire leg swollen (+1), calf swelling >3 cm compared with the other leg (+1), pitting oedema confined to the symptomatic leg (+1), collateral superficial veins (+1), previous documented DVT (+1), alternative diagnosis at least as likely (−2). Score ≤1: DVT unlikely; ≥2: likely.
2. **DVT unlikely:** D-dimer (negative excludes; positive → ultrasound).
3. **DVT likely:** **compression ultrasound** (proximal; whole-leg if needed); if delay, start anticoagulation while awaiting.
4. If ultrasound is negative but suspicion remains, repeat in a week or use D-dimer.
5. **Unprovoked DVT:** consider cancer screening limited to history, examination, basic bloods, and age-appropriate screening; thrombophilia testing only in selected patients.
6. **Treatment:** anticoagulation (DOAC, LMWH), compression stockings for symptom relief (not for prevention of post-thrombotic syndrome), mobilisation.
7. **Extensive iliofemoral DVT with limb-threatening (phlegmasia cerulea dolens)** requires urgent vascular intervention.

#### Distinguishing cellulitis from DVT and venous eczema

Cellulitis: acute onset, fever, well-defined spreading erythema with tenderness; DVT: swelling and deep tenderness with less erythema; stasis dermatitis: bilateral, chronic, itchy, scaly, with pigmentation, no fever. Cellulitis and DVT may coexist, and bilateral cellulitis is rare (suggesting alternative causes such as venous eczema or heart failure).

### 31.5 Lymphoedema and lipoedema

- **Lymphoedema:** chronic swelling due to lymph transport failure. **Primary** (congenital: Milroy disease; praecox: onset at puberty; tarda: after 35) and **secondary** (most common worldwide: **filariasis**; in developed countries: **cancer treatment** (axillary node dissection, radiotherapy, inguinal node dissection), malignant obstruction, recurrent cellulitis, trauma, obesity, immobility, venous disease).
- **Features:** initially soft and pitting, then firm and non-pitting; starts distally, involves the dorsum of the foot with "squared-off" toes, positive Stemmer sign, skin thickening (peau d'orange), papillomatosis, hyperkeratosis, recurrent infections; extremely rare angiosarcoma (Stewart–Treves).
- **Diagnosis:** clinical; lymphoscintigraphy, MR lymphangiography, or ultrasound in selected cases; exclude malignant obstruction with CT or MRI (new unilateral lymphoedema in a patient without a clear cause).
- **Management:** **complex decongestive therapy** (skin care, manual lymphatic drainage, compression bandaging and garments, exercise), weight reduction, treat infection promptly (antibiotics), prophylactic antibiotics for recurrent cellulitis, surgery in selected cases; patient education (avoid skin injury; avoid blood pressure or venepuncture in the at-risk arm is a traditional but weakly supported rule).

### 31.6 Angioedema and facial/neck swelling

#### Angioedema

Localised, non-pitting swelling of the deep dermis, subcutaneous tissue or mucosa, often affecting the lips, tongue, eyelids, face, larynx, hands and feet, or gut wall (abdominal pain). It is potentially **life-threatening if the tongue, larynx or pharynx is involved** (stridor, voice change, difficulty swallowing, drooling).

| Type | Mechanism | Features | Treatment |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Histaminergic (allergic)** | IgE-mediated or mast cell activation (foods, drugs, stings, latex) | Associated urticaria, itch, wheeze, hypotension (anaphylaxis); onset within minutes to hours | **Intramuscular adrenaline (0.5 mg of 1:1,000 in adults)** for anaphylaxis or airway involvement, antihistamine, steroids, oxygen, fluids |
| **ACE-inhibitor-induced** | Bradykinin accumulation | Face, lips, tongue, larynx; no urticaria; can appear weeks to years after starting; more frequent in people of African descent; **antihistamines and steroids are not effective**; stop the drug permanently; airway protection; adrenaline is often tried; icatibant or C1-inhibitor in refractory cases |
| **Hereditary angioedema (C1-esterase inhibitor deficiency)** | Bradykinin-mediated | Recurrent swelling of skin, GI tract (severe abdominal pain, vomiting) and airway; no urticaria; family history; attacks last 2–5 days; triggered by trauma, dental procedures, stress, oestrogens; low C4 (screening test), low C1-inhibitor level or function | **C1-inhibitor concentrate, icatibant, ecallantide** for attacks; long-term prophylaxis (lanadelumab, berotralstat, C1-INH); avoid ACE inhibitors and oestrogens; adrenaline, antihistamines and steroids are ineffective |
| **Acquired C1-inhibitor deficiency** | Lymphoproliferative disease, autoimmune | Late-onset angioedema, low C1q | Treat the cause |
| **Idiopathic** | | Recurrent, no cause identified | Antihistamines, omalizumab |

#### Superior vena cava (SVC) obstruction

- **Features:** facial and neck swelling (worse in the morning and on bending or lying), arm swelling, dilated neck and chest wall veins, plethora, headache, dyspnoea, cough, hoarseness, stridor (laryngeal oedema; emergency), **non-pulsatile raised JVP,** Pemberton sign (facial plethora and venous distension when raising both arms).
- **Causes:** malignancy (lung cancer, lymphoma, thymoma, metastases) accounts for most, central venous catheters and pacemaker leads (thrombosis), mediastinal fibrosis, goitre, aortic aneurysm.
- **Management:** urgent CT chest with contrast, treat according to the cause (radiotherapy, chemotherapy, stent, anticoagulation), elevate the head, steroids in lymphoma, avoid IV access in the arms if possible. Establish a tissue diagnosis before steroids and radiotherapy when feasible and the patient is stable; if there is stridor or cerebral oedema, treat urgently.

#### Other causes of facial or periorbital swelling

Nephrotic and nephritic syndromes; allergic reactions; hypothyroidism; thyroid eye disease (Graves ophthalmopathy: proptosis, lid retraction, diplopia); cellulitis (orbital cellulitis is an emergency: pain with eye movements, proptosis, fever, reduced vision); dental abscess (Ludwig angina: bilateral submandibular swelling, floor-of-mouth elevation, airway risk); parotitis; trichinosis; dermatomyositis (heliotrope rash); Cushing syndrome; superior vena cava obstruction.

# Part B: Lumps

### 31.7 Describing and approaching a lump

#### History

- **Duration and speed of growth:** recent rapid growth suggests infection, haematoma or aggressive malignancy; slow growth suggests benign lesions or low-grade tumours (but persistence over weeks to months should prompt evaluation).
- **Pain or tenderness:** inflammation (abscess, lymphadenitis), but many cancers are painless; haemorrhage into a lesion may cause pain.
- **Changes in size** (e.g. intermittent swelling: hernia, salivary gland stone, ganglion, cyst), relation to meals (salivary), posture, cough, exertion.
- **Associated symptoms:** fever, night sweats, weight loss (lymphoma, TB, malignancy); symptoms of the relevant organ (dysphagia, hoarseness: thyroid, hypopharynx; urinary symptoms: groin/scrotum); skin change, discharge.
- **Previous lumps, trauma, surgery, cancer history,** radiation exposure, smoking and alcohol (head and neck cancer), sun exposure (skin cancer), immunosuppression, HIV, family history (thyroid cancer, breast cancer, sarcoma syndromes, neurofibromatosis).
- **Travel and exposures** (TB, cat-scratch disease, tularaemia, toxoplasmosis, brucellosis).

#### Examination: the "Ten S's" (or the standard description)

| Feature | What to note |
|---|---|
| **Site** | Anatomical location, relation to structures |
| **Size** | In cm, measured with a ruler (record for follow-up) |
| **Shape** | Round, oval, irregular |
| **Surface** | Smooth, lobulated, nodular, irregular |
| **Edge** | Well-defined or ill-defined |
| **Consistency** | Soft (lipoma), cystic/fluctuant (cyst, abscess), firm/rubbery (lymphoma node), hard (carcinoma, calcified), stony |
| **Tenderness and temperature** | Inflammation, abscess |
| **Mobility** | Mobile in all planes (lipoma, benign node); **fixed** to skin or deeper tissues (invasion by carcinoma); moves with swallowing (thyroid), tongue protrusion (thyroglossal cyst), or muscle contraction |
| **Skin and surroundings** | Tethering, ulceration, erythema, venous distension, punctum (epidermoid cyst) |
| **Special tests** | **Transillumination** (hydrocele, cystic hygroma, spermatocele), **pulsatility** (expansile: aneurysm; transmitted: mass overlying an artery), **reducibility and cough impulse** (hernia), **auscultation** (bruit: vascular lesions, thyroid in Graves), **regional lymph nodes**, relation to adjacent organs (examine the whole region and the drainage area) |

**Consistency and mobility are most important for judging malignancy risk.** A **hard, irregular, fixed lump** is cancer until proved otherwise. Size >5 cm, rapid growth, deep location (beneath the deep fascia), and pain (in soft-tissue masses) increase the risk of sarcoma.

### 31.8 Lumps by region

#### Skin and subcutaneous lumps

| Lesion | Features |
|---|---|
| **Epidermoid (sebaceous) cyst** | Smooth, round, firm, mobile, **central punctum,** attached to skin, in face, scalp, back; can become infected (red, painful) or rupture |
| **Lipoma** | Soft, doughy, lobulated, mobile (slips under the finger), painless, subcutaneous; multiple in familial lipomatosis; deep or large (>5 cm), rapidly growing or painful lipomatous masses need imaging to exclude liposarcoma |
| **Dermatofibroma** | Firm brown papule, dimples on lateral pinching ("dimple sign"), legs |
| **Neurofibroma** | Soft, skin-coloured; "buttonhole" invagination; multiple: neurofibromatosis type 1 (café-au-lait macules, axillary freckling, Lisch nodules) |
| **Abscess** | Hot, tender, fluctuant, erythematous; incision and drainage |
| **Ganglion** | Smooth, firm cystic swelling near a joint or tendon sheath (dorsal wrist, volar wrist, flexor sheath); transilluminates; may fluctuate in size |
| **Wart, molluscum, skin tags** | Typical morphology |
| **Basal cell carcinoma** | Pearly translucent nodule with telangiectasia, rolled edge, central ulcer; face, slowly growing |
| **Squamous cell carcinoma, keratoacanthoma** | Rapidly growing hyperkeratotic nodule, central crater, sun-exposed skin; SCC may metastasise |
| **Melanoma** | Pigmented lesion with ABCDE features (Chapter 18); nodular melanoma can be amelanotic; urgent excision biopsy |
| **Metastatic skin nodules** | Firm, multiple, painless |

#### Neck lumps

| Position | Differential |
|---|---|
| **Midline** | **Thyroglossal cyst** (moves upward on tongue protrusion and swallowing; children and young adults), **thyroid** (moves on swallowing), dermoid cyst, submental lymph node, ranula, plunging ranula |
| **Anterior triangle** (lateral) | **Lymph nodes** (reactive: infection; TB: matted, "cold abscess"; lymphoma: rubbery; metastatic: hard), **branchial cyst** (anterior to the sternocleidomastoid, upper third; young adults; fluctuant), **carotid body tumour** (paraganglioma: pulsatile, mobile side-to-side but not vertically), carotid aneurysm, submandibular gland (stone: pain on eating; tumour), parotid tail |
| **Posterior triangle** | Lymph nodes (EBV, toxoplasmosis, rubella, lymphoma), **cystic hygroma** (children; transilluminates), cervical rib, lipoma, pharyngeal pouch (rare) |
| **Supraclavicular** | **Virchow node** (left: abdominal malignancy), apical lung tumour, lymphoma |

**Red flags for head and neck malignancy:** persistent lump >3 weeks in an adult, especially firm, painless, and in a smoker/drinker; unexplained hoarseness >3 weeks; unilateral ear pain or hearing loss; non-healing mouth ulcer >3 weeks; dysphagia; persistent sore throat; **HPV-related oropharyngeal cancer** may present with a cystic neck node in a non-smoker (a cystic lateral neck mass in an adult over 40 should be considered malignant until proven otherwise). Investigations: ultrasound with FNA or core biopsy, CT/MRI, panendoscopy, PET.

#### Thyroid swellings

- **Diffuse goitre:** Graves disease, Hashimoto thyroiditis, iodine deficiency, subacute thyroiditis (tender), physiological (puberty, pregnancy).
- **Nodular goitre:** multinodular goitre (common; may cause tracheal compression, retrosternal extension, Pemberton sign), toxic nodule.
- **Solitary nodule:** most are benign (colloid nodules, cysts, adenomas); about 5–10% malignant. **Features raising suspicion:** age <20 or >60, male, history of head and neck radiation, family history of thyroid cancer or MEN2, rapid growth, **hard, fixed nodule,** hoarseness or dysphagia, **cervical lymphadenopathy.**
- **Investigations:** **TSH** (if low: radionuclide scan), **neck ultrasound** (risk stratification with TI-RADS: microcalcifications, hypoechogenicity, irregular margins, taller-than-wide shape), **fine-needle aspiration cytology (Bethesda system)** for suspicious nodules (size thresholds depend on features), calcitonin if medullary carcinoma is suspected.
- Thyroid cancers: papillary (most common, good prognosis), follicular, medullary, anaplastic (rapid growth, dysphagia, airway compromise; emergency).

#### Salivary gland swellings

- **Parotid:** mumps (bilateral, painful, children, unvaccinated), acute bacterial parotitis (older, dehydrated, post-operative), sialolithiasis (rare in the parotid), HIV-associated cysts, **Sjögren syndrome,** sarcoidosis (Heerfordt), **pleomorphic adenoma** (slow-growing, painless, mobile, most common benign tumour), Warthin tumour (older men, smokers), **malignant tumours** (facial nerve palsy, fixity, rapid growth, pain, skin involvement, lymph nodes).
- **Submandibular:** **stone** (swelling and pain on eating that subsides afterwards; stone palpable in the floor of the mouth), sialadenitis, tumour (higher proportion malignant than parotid).
- **Investigation:** ultrasound, FNA, MRI; surgical referral. Facial nerve palsy with a parotid mass = malignancy until proven otherwise.

#### Groin lumps

| Lump | Features |
|---|---|
| **Inguinal hernia** | Above and medial to the pubic tubercle; cough impulse; reducible; direct or indirect (Chapter 14) |
| **Femoral hernia** | Below and lateral to the pubic tubercle; women; high risk of strangulation |
| **Lymph nodes** | Below the inguinal ligament; infection of the leg/genitalia, lymphoma, melanoma, STI, squamous carcinoma |
| **Saphena varix** | Soft, bluish, disappears on lying down, thrill on coughing, fluid thrill from the distal saphenous vein; medial to the femoral pulse, below the inguinal ligament |
| **Femoral artery aneurysm** | Expansile pulsation |
| **Psoas abscess or bursitis** | Deep, tender, fever, hip flexion pain |
| **Undescended or ectopic testis** | Empty scrotum |
| **Hydrocele of the cord, lipoma of the cord** | |

#### Scrotal lumps

| Lump | Features |
|---|---|
| **Testicular tumour** | **Painless, hard, irregular lump in the testis** (cannot get above it, may feel heavy); young men (15–35); sometimes secondary hydrocele; gynaecomastia, back pain (para-aortic nodes); urgent scrotal ultrasound, serum AFP, β-hCG, LDH, urgent urology referral for orchidectomy via the inguinal approach (never scrotal biopsy) |
| **Epididymo-orchitis** | Painful, tender, swollen epididymis, fever, urethral discharge or urinary symptoms (STI in young; coliforms in older); pain relieved by lifting the testis (Prehn sign, unreliable) |
| **Testicular torsion** | **Sudden severe pain, high-riding testis with horizontal lie, absent cremasteric reflex, nausea and vomiting;** adolescents; **emergency surgery within 6 hours** |
| **Hydrocele** | Fluid around the testis; **transilluminates; cannot be separated from the testis; can get above the swelling** (differs from inguinal hernia); causes: idiopathic, infection, tumour, trauma |
| **Epididymal cyst/spermatocele** | Smooth cystic swelling above and behind the testis, transilluminates |
| **Varicocele** | "Bag of worms," more prominent on standing, left side more often (right-sided or non-reducing: consider retroperitoneal mass or renal cell carcinoma); associated with infertility |
| **Inguinoscrotal hernia** | Cannot get above; cough impulse; bowel sounds |
| **Haematocele, haematoma** | Trauma |

#### Abdominal wall lumps

Umbilical, paraumbilical, epigastric and incisional hernias; rectus sheath haematoma (anticoagulated patients, sudden pain, Fothergill sign); lipoma; desmoid tumour; endometrioma in a caesarean scar; Sister Mary Joseph nodule (periumbilical metastasis).

#### Lumps in the limbs and soft tissues

- **Ganglion, bursitis** (prepatellar, olecranon), **Baker cyst** (popliteal), **Dupuytren nodule,** **lipoma,** **epidermoid cyst,** **haematoma.**
- **Soft-tissue sarcoma:** can arise anywhere, most often in the thigh, buttock, retroperitoneum; **painless, enlarging mass, deep to the fascia, >5 cm,** firm; any soft-tissue lump with these features requires **urgent imaging** (ultrasound then MRI) and referral to a sarcoma centre **before** biopsy or excision. **Unplanned ("whoops") excision** of a sarcoma compromises the outcome.
- **Bone tumours:** painful swelling, night pain, pathological fracture; X-ray (periosteal reaction, lytic or sclerotic lesion), MRI; young patients (osteosarcoma, Ewing sarcoma).
- **Myositis ossificans, abscess, haemangioma, lymphangioma, desmoid.**

#### Chest wall and breast lumps

See Chapter 19 for breast lumps. Chest wall lumps: costochondritis (Tietze syndrome), rib tumour, lipoma, sebaceous cyst, lymph node (axillary).

#### Lumps in children

- **Reactive cervical lymph nodes** (most common), **congenital** (thyroglossal duct cyst, branchial cleft cyst, dermoid cyst, cystic hygroma, haemangioma), **torticollis** (sternocleidomastoid tumour of infancy), **inguinal hernia and hydrocele** (infants), **undescended testis,** **pilomatricoma,** **umbilical hernia** (often resolves). **Malignancies** (lymphoma, neuroblastoma, rhabdomyosarcoma, leukaemia) must be considered with persistent, hard, rapidly growing, or supraclavicular nodes, systemic symptoms or hepatosplenomegaly.

### 31.9 Investigating lumps

| Modality | Use |
|---|---|
| **Ultrasound** | **First-line for most superficial lumps** (neck, thyroid, salivary, breast, scrotum, soft tissue, groin, lymph nodes): cystic vs solid, vascularity, relation to structures, guide for aspiration or biopsy |
| **Fine-needle aspiration cytology (FNAC)** | Thyroid nodules, lymph nodes, salivary glands, breast, cysts |
| **Core needle biopsy (image-guided)** | Preferred for soft-tissue masses, lymph nodes (lymphoma needs architecture), breast |
| **Excision biopsy** | Skin lesions, nodes when core biopsy is non-diagnostic; **not** for suspected sarcoma before imaging |
| **MRI** | Soft-tissue masses, sarcoma, salivary glands, head and neck, spine |
| **CT** | Neck, chest, abdomen/pelvis for staging, deep masses |
| **PET-CT** | Staging, unknown primary, lymphoma |
| **Blood tests** | FBC, ESR, CRP, LDH; tumour markers for testicular tumours (AFP, β-hCG, LDH); TFTs and calcitonin; HIV, TB tests; infectious serology (EBV, CMV, toxoplasma, Bartonella) |
| **Endoscopy** | Head and neck, upper GI, bronchoscopy for primary tumour in nodal presentations |

**Triple assessment** (clinical, imaging, tissue) applies to lumps suspicious of cancer, notably the breast and neck.

#### Referral criteria (examples; follow local urgent cancer pathways)

- Unexplained neck lump in an adult (>3 weeks)
- Hard, fixed, enlarging lymph node, supraclavicular node, generalised lymphadenopathy with systemic symptoms
- Soft-tissue lump >5 cm, deep, enlarging, or painful
- Thyroid nodule with suspicious features
- Painless testicular lump or swelling
- Any lump with skin ulceration or fixation
- Any parotid mass with facial weakness

### 31.10 Worked cases

#### Case 1: DVT

*A 62-year-old woman, 10 days after a total knee replacement, has a swollen, tender left calf (4 cm larger than the right), with pitting oedema and warmth; afebrile. Wells score 4 (major surgery within 12 weeks +1, calf swelling >3 cm +1, pitting oedema +1, localised tenderness +1).*

**Reasoning.** **DVT likely.**

**Action.** Proximal compression ultrasound urgently; start therapeutic anticoagulation if there will be a delay; if confirmed, DOAC for at least 3 months (provoked), compression stockings for symptom relief, mobilise; check for PE symptoms.

#### Case 2: Nephrotic syndrome

*A 24-year-old man develops puffy eyes, then leg swelling over 2 weeks, with frothy urine. BP 118/76. Urine protein 4+, no blood. Albumin 18 g/L, uPCR 650 mg/mmol, cholesterol 9.8 mmol/L, creatinine normal.*

**Reasoning.** **Nephrotic syndrome** (minimal change disease, FSGS, membranous). Risks: infection, thromboembolism, AKI.

**Action.** Nephrology referral, renal biopsy (in adults), secondary cause screen (HIV, hepatitis B/C, SLE, drugs, diabetes), salt restriction, loop diuretic, ACE inhibitor/ARB for proteinuria, statin, consider anticoagulation if albumin very low, immunosuppression (corticosteroids for minimal change disease).

#### Case 3: Ascites in cirrhosis

*A 56-year-old man with alcohol-related cirrhosis has abdominal distension and ankle oedema. Shifting dullness present, spider naevi, palmar erythema. Ascitic fluid: SAAG 1.5 g/dL, protein 12 g/L, neutrophils 60/µL.*

**Reasoning.** **Portal hypertensive ascites** (SAAG ≥1.1), no spontaneous bacterial peritonitis (neutrophils <250/µL).

**Action.** Alcohol abstinence, sodium restriction, spironolactone ± furosemide, monitor weight and creatinine, large-volume paracentesis with albumin if tense, consider transplant assessment; prophylactic antibiotics if protein <15 g/L and other risk factors.

#### Case 4: A drug causing oedema, and a cascade

*A 74-year-old woman on amlodipine 10 mg has bilateral ankle oedema; she was started on furosemide, then developed urinary frequency, dizziness on standing and a fall, with hypokalaemia.*

**Reasoning.** **Amlodipine-induced oedema** treated as heart failure: a prescribing cascade. JVP normal, no crackles, BNP normal.

**Action.** Stop furosemide, reduce or switch amlodipine (consider ACE inhibitor/ARB, lower dose, or other agent), correct potassium, elevate legs, review falls risk.

#### Case 5: Lymphoedema after cancer treatment

*A 58-year-old woman has progressive swelling of the right arm 18 months after a mastectomy with axillary node dissection and radiotherapy. The arm is firm, non-pitting, with a positive Stemmer sign; no pain or fever. Doppler shows no DVT.*

**Reasoning.** **Secondary lymphoedema** after axillary treatment; exclude recurrence (new or rapid swelling) and venous thrombosis.

**Action.** Referral to lymphoedema service, compression garments, manual lymphatic drainage, exercise, skin care and prompt antibiotics for cellulitis; imaging if progression suggests recurrent cancer.

#### Case 6: ACE-inhibitor angioedema

*A 66-year-old man on lisinopril for 3 years wakes with swelling of his lips and tongue and a muffled voice. No rash or itching. Stridor is beginning.*

**Reasoning.** **ACE-inhibitor angioedema** with airway involvement: bradykinin-mediated, antihistamine- and steroid-resistant.

**Action.** Emergency airway assessment (senior anaesthetic/ENT), oxygen, IM adrenaline may be tried, consider icatibant or C1-inhibitor concentrate; early intubation or surgical airway if progressive; stop ACE inhibitors permanently (and consider ARB caution); document an allergy/intolerance alert.

#### Case 7: Thyroid nodule

*A 41-year-old woman has a firm 2.5-cm nodule in the right thyroid lobe and a hard 1-cm node in the right level III neck. TSH normal. Ultrasound: hypoechoic nodule with microcalcifications and irregular margins. FNA: papillary carcinoma.*

**Reasoning.** **Papillary thyroid carcinoma** with nodal involvement.

**Action.** Surgical referral for total thyroidectomy and neck dissection, staging, radioiodine as indicated, TSH suppression and long-term follow-up with thyroglobulin and ultrasound.

#### Case 8: Soft-tissue sarcoma

*A 54-year-old man notices a painless, deep, firm lump in the anterior thigh, 8 cm and growing over 4 months. He plans to see a surgeon for removal.*

**Reasoning.** Deep, >5 cm, enlarging, firm: **sarcoma until proven otherwise.**

**Action.** **MRI before any biopsy or excision;** refer to a sarcoma centre; image-guided core biopsy planned by the sarcoma team; avoid unplanned excision.

#### Case 9: Testicular lump

*A 27-year-old man has a painless, hard, 2-cm lump in the right testis, noticed in the shower, with a feeling of heaviness. Ultrasound: hypoechoic intratesticular mass. AFP and β-hCG raised; LDH raised.*

**Reasoning.** **Testicular germ-cell tumour.**

**Action.** Urgent urology referral, radical inguinal orchidectomy, staging CT, sperm banking before treatment, MDT discussion; high cure rates.

#### Case 10: Groin lump in an older woman

*An 80-year-old woman with 24 hours of colicky abdominal pain, vomiting and absolute constipation has a tender, firm, irreducible lump below and lateral to the pubic tubercle.*

**Reasoning.** **Strangulated femoral hernia** causing small bowel obstruction.

**Action.** Resuscitate, nasogastric tube, analgesia, antibiotics, **emergency surgery.**

#### Case 11: Neck lump in a non-smoker

*A 47-year-old non-smoker has a painless, firm, cystic 3-cm level II neck mass for 2 months. No sore throat or fever. FNA shows squamous cells; p16 positive.*

**Reasoning.** **HPV-related oropharyngeal carcinoma** with a cystic nodal metastasis (often mistaken for a branchial cyst).

**Action.** ENT/head and neck MDT, panendoscopy with tonsillar and base-of-tongue biopsies, MRI/CT/PET, treatment with chemoradiotherapy or surgery.

#### Case 12: Hydrocele

*A 65-year-old man has a gradual painless scrotal swelling over a year. The swelling is smooth, fluctuant, transilluminates brightly, the testis cannot be felt separately, and one can get above the swelling.*

**Reasoning.** **Hydrocele** (primary or secondary). Ultrasound to exclude underlying testicular pathology (tumour, infection, hernia), then conservative management or surgical repair if symptomatic.

### 31.11 Common pitfalls

- **Treating oedema with diuretics before finding the cause,** and creating prescribing cascades.
- **Failing to examine the JVP** and abdomen in oedema.
- **Missing nephrotic syndrome** (no urine dipstick).
- **Assuming unilateral swelling is "just a pulled muscle,"** missing DVT.
- **Missing a pelvic mass** as the cause of unilateral venous or lymphatic obstruction.
- **Diagnosing cellulitis in bilateral stasis dermatitis** or heart failure.
- **Not recognising the airway risk of angioedema** and giving only antihistamines for ACE-inhibitor swelling.
- **Overlooking SVC obstruction** in facial swelling with distended neck veins.
- **Not measuring and documenting the size** of lumps.
- **Excising a "lipoma" or deep mass without imaging.**
- **Aspirating a testicular mass** or doing a scrotal incision (use inguinal orchidectomy).
- **Missing a cystic neck node in an adult as a branchial cyst.**
- **Ignoring facial nerve weakness** with a parotid mass.
- **Treating a thyroid nodule without ultrasound and FNA.**
- **Reassuring too early** about a persistent lump.

---

### 31.12 Key points

- Oedema is a sign: classify by distribution (generalised/localised), laterality, pitting, tempo; check JVP, albumin, urine protein, liver, thyroid and drugs.
- Generalised oedema: heart failure, cirrhosis, nephrotic syndrome and other hypoalbuminaemic states, CKD, drugs, hypothyroidism (non-pitting).
- Unilateral leg swelling: DVT (Wells, ultrasound, D-dimer), cellulitis, Baker cyst, pelvic mass obstruction, lymphoedema, compartment syndrome.
- Review drug causes of oedema before adding diuretics.
- Angioedema with airway involvement and SVC obstruction are emergencies; ACE-inhibitor and hereditary angioedema do not respond to antihistamines or steroids.
- Describe lumps systematically; hard, fixed, irregular, enlarging, deep and >5 cm lumps need urgent investigation.
- Use ultrasound first, then FNA/core biopsy; MRI before biopsy for suspected sarcoma; avoid whoops excisions.
- Do not miss testicular torsion, strangulated hernia, testicular tumour, thyroid and head and neck cancers.

### 31.13 Self-assessment

1. List the Starling forces and give one cause of oedema for each disturbance.
   *Answer: Raised capillary hydrostatic pressure (heart failure, venous obstruction), reduced oncotic pressure (nephrotic syndrome, cirrhosis), increased permeability (sepsis, angioedema), lymphatic obstruction (lymphoedema), sodium retention (renal disease, drugs).*

2. How does the SAAG help in ascites?
   *Answer: SAAG ≥1.1 g/dL indicates portal hypertension (cirrhosis, heart failure); <1.1 suggests peritoneal causes (malignancy, TB, pancreatic).*

3. Why are antihistamines and steroids ineffective in ACE-inhibitor angioedema?
   *Answer: The swelling is bradykinin-mediated, not histamine-mediated; treatment is airway support, stopping the ACE inhibitor, and bradykinin-targeted drugs (icatibant, C1-inhibitor) in severe cases.*

4. Differentiate a hydrocele from an inguinoscrotal hernia on examination.
   *Answer: A hydrocele transilluminates, the examiner can get above it, and it has no cough impulse; a hernia cannot be 'got above,' has a cough impulse and may contain bowel.*

5. Which features of a soft-tissue lump should prompt urgent imaging for sarcoma?
   *Answer: Size >5 cm, deep to fascia, enlarging, painful, firm; avoid biopsy or excision before MRI.*

6. Name four features of a thyroid nodule that increase the risk of malignancy.
   *Answer (any four): age <20 or >60, male sex, prior neck radiation, family history, hard fixed nodule, rapid growth, hoarseness, cervical nodes, suspicious ultrasound features (microcalcifications, irregular margins, hypoechogenicity).*
