# Chapter 56. Psychiatry

## Learning objectives

After studying this chapter you should be able to:

1. Take a psychiatric history, apply the mental state examination (chapter 20) to reach a working diagnosis, and exclude medical causes of psychiatric presentations.
2. Diagnose and grade depression, distinguish unipolar from bipolar illness, and plan first-line treatment.
3. Recognise and manage anxiety disorders, panic attacks, obsessive–compulsive disorder and post-traumatic stress disorder.
4. Recognise a first episode of psychosis, differentiate primary from secondary causes, and know the principles of antipsychotic use and monitoring.
5. Diagnose delirium early, find its cause, and distinguish it from dementia and depression.
6. Assess substance use disorders, alcohol withdrawal, and overdose, and apply brief intervention.
7. Perform a structured suicide and violence risk assessment, and manage a patient in acute behavioural crisis safely.
8. Recognise eating disorders, somatic symptom presentations, and psychiatric emergencies including neuroleptic malignant syndrome, serotonin syndrome and catatonia.
9. Use psychotropic drugs with attention to interactions, adverse effects and discontinuation, and understand the legal framework for involuntary treatment.

> **Note on thresholds and doses.** Doses and regimens follow commonly used guidelines (NICE, APA, CANMAT, WFSBP, DSM-5-TR/ICD-11 criteria) at the time of writing. Check your national formulary, local mental health law and local protocols before acting.

## 1. Principles of psychiatric diagnosis in general medicine

### 1.1 Why this matters

Roughly one in four adults has a mental disorder in a given year, and 30–50% of general medical patients have psychiatric symptoms. Mental illness shortens life expectancy by 10–20 years, largely through cardiovascular, metabolic and respiratory disease and suicide. Psychiatric and medical illness are intertwined: depression doubles the risk of poor outcome after myocardial infarction; chronic pain and diabetes raise the risk of depression; delirium is the commonest psychiatric syndrome in hospital.

### 1.2 The diagnostic approach

1. **Establish the syndrome first** (a cluster of symptoms and signs: depressive, manic, psychotic, anxious, cognitive, behavioural), then look for the disorder and its cause.
2. **Exclude organic causes before accepting a primary psychiatric diagnosis**, especially when onset is acute, late (after age 40–50), with fluctuating consciousness, visual hallucinations, disorientation, focal neurological signs, abnormal vital signs, or a rapidly changing picture.
3. **Take a full history including collateral**: onset, course, precipitants, previous episodes, substance and medication use, family history, social context, risk.
4. **Use the mental state examination** (appearance and behaviour, speech, mood and affect, thought form and content, perceptions, cognition, insight and judgement) and a risk assessment on every patient.
5. **Use validated tools to quantify**: PHQ-9, GAD-7, AUDIT-C, CAGE, MDQ, Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale, CAM, MoCA/MMSE, C-SSRS. Tools support but never replace clinical judgement.
6. **Diagnose using the classification in your system** (DSM-5-TR or ICD-11). Diagnoses require duration, severity, and functional impairment, and exclusion of substance or medical causes.

### 1.3 Medical conditions presenting as psychiatric illness

| Presentation | Consider |
|---|---|
| Depression | Hypothyroidism, anaemia, vitamin B12/folate deficiency, Cushing, Addison, hypercalcaemia, diabetes, obstructive sleep apnoea, Parkinson disease, stroke, dementia, cancer (pancreatic), HIV, medications (steroids, beta-blockers, interferon, isotretinoin, levetiracetam, hormonal contraceptives) |
| Anxiety / panic | Hyperthyroidism, phaeochromocytoma, arrhythmia, hypoglycaemia, asthma/COPD, caffeine or stimulant use, alcohol or benzodiazepine withdrawal, pulmonary embolism, medications (salbutamol, theophylline, thyroxine, SSRIs on initiation) |
| Mania | Hyperthyroidism, steroids, stimulants, multiple sclerosis, frontal lesions, HIV, neurosyphilis |
| Psychosis | Delirium, drugs (cannabis, amphetamines, LSD, steroids, dopamine agonists), temporal lobe epilepsy, autoimmune encephalitis (anti-NMDA receptor), SLE, Wilson disease, B12 deficiency, HIV, neurosyphilis, brain tumour, Huntington disease |
| Cognitive impairment | Delirium, depression ("pseudodementia"), hypothyroidism, B12 deficiency, normal pressure hydrocephalus, subdural haematoma, drugs, alcohol |
| Catatonia | Encephalitis, metabolic derangement, neuroleptics, mood disorders, schizophrenia |
| Sudden personality change | Frontal lobe tumour, frontotemporal dementia, stroke, head injury |

**Basic work-up in a first presentation:** vital signs and glucose, full examination including neurological, FBC, urea and electrolytes, calcium, LFTs, TSH, B12 and folate, glucose/HbA1c, urine drug screen, urinalysis, HIV and syphilis serology by risk, ECG (before QT-prolonging drugs), pregnancy test in women, and neuroimaging (CT or MRI) for first-episode psychosis, late-onset symptoms, focal signs, head injury or atypical course. EEG, lumbar puncture and autoimmune panels are directed by features (seizures, catatonia, rapid onset, dyskinesias, autonomic instability).

## 2. Depression

### 2.1 Diagnosis

Major depressive episode (DSM-5-TR): at least **five** of nine symptoms present most of the day, nearly every day, for **at least two weeks**, including at least one of (1) depressed mood or (2) loss of interest/pleasure:

- Depressed mood (in children and adolescents: irritability).
- Markedly diminished interest or pleasure (anhedonia).
- Significant weight or appetite change.
- Insomnia or hypersomnia.
- Psychomotor agitation or retardation.
- Fatigue or loss of energy.
- Worthlessness or excessive guilt.
- Diminished concentration or indecisiveness.
- Recurrent thoughts of death or suicidal ideation.

Symptoms cause distress or impairment and are not due to a substance or medical condition. **Always ask about previous manic or hypomanic episodes before diagnosing unipolar depression** (see section 3).

**Screening.** PHQ-2 (depressed mood, anhedonia over the past two weeks): a score ≥ 3 prompts PHQ-9. PHQ-9: 5–9 mild, 10–14 moderate, 15–19 moderately severe, ≥ 20 severe. Item 9 (thoughts of being better off dead or self-harm) requires a risk assessment whatever the total.

**Severity and special forms.**

- **Mild**: few symptoms, little impairment. **Moderate**: marked impairment. **Severe**: most symptoms, major impairment; **with psychotic features** (mood-congruent delusions: guilt, nihilistic, persecutory); **with melancholic features** (early waking, diurnal variation worse in the morning, marked psychomotor change, pervasive anhedonia); **atypical** (mood reactivity, hypersomnia, weight gain, leaden paralysis, rejection sensitivity); **with catatonia**; **with anxious distress**; **peripartum onset**; **seasonal pattern**.
- **Persistent depressive disorder (dysthymia)**: depressed mood most days for ≥ 2 years (1 year in children).
- **Adjustment disorder**: emotional or behavioural symptoms within 3 months of an identifiable stressor, not meeting criteria for another disorder.
- **Bereavement**: grief is normal; distinguish from depression by waves of longing with preserved self-esteem. Prolonged grief disorder (≥ 12 months in adults) is a separate diagnosis.

**Elderly patients and men** may present with somatic complaints, irritability, apathy, cognitive complaints, alcohol use or reckless behaviour rather than sadness.

### 2.2 Differential diagnosis and work-up

Exclude medical causes and medication effects (table above), bipolar disorder, grief, dementia, delirium (hypoactive), substance use. Check TSH, FBC, B12, folate, glucose, calcium, vitamin D (selectively), and consider sleep apnoea, chronic pain and medication review.

### 2.3 Management

**Mild depression.** Active monitoring, psychoeducation, sleep hygiene, exercise (structured aerobic activity is effective), behavioural activation, guided self-help, internet CBT. Antidepressants are not first-line in mild depression unless it is persistent or there is a past history.

**Moderate to severe depression.** Combination of psychological therapy (CBT, interpersonal therapy, behavioural activation, problem-solving therapy) **and** antidepressant is more effective than either alone in severe depression.

**Antidepressant choice and use.**

- **First line: SSRI** (sertraline, escitalopram, fluoxetine, citalopram, paroxetine less preferred for side effects and discontinuation) or SNRI (venlafaxine, duloxetine), mirtazapine, bupropion, vortioxetine.
- Start low and titrate (e.g., sertraline 50 mg, escitalopram 10 mg, fluoxetine 20 mg); **onset 2–4 weeks**, full effect 6–8 weeks. Review at 1–2 weeks (side effects, suicidal ideation in young adults), then at 4 weeks and 6–8 weeks; if less than 20% improvement at 4 weeks, optimise dose; if no response at 6–8 weeks at an adequate dose, switch class or augment.
- **Continue for at least 6–12 months after remission** (first episode), 2 years or longer if recurrent or severe; taper gradually over weeks to months to avoid discontinuation syndrome (dizziness, "brain zaps", anxiety, nausea, insomnia, flu-like symptoms; most with paroxetine and venlafaxine).
- **Adverse effects**: nausea, headache, anxiety early on, sexual dysfunction, weight change, sweating, insomnia or somnolence, hyponatraemia (older adults), bleeding risk (with NSAIDs, anticoagulants, antiplatelets: add a PPI), QT prolongation (citalopram/escitalopram dose limits, especially > 40 mg/20 mg, > 60 years), serotonin syndrome with combinations (MAOIs, linezolid, tramadol, triptans, lithium, St John's wort, methylene blue), and activation/suicidality in under-25s.
- **Cautions**: mirtazapine (sedation, weight gain), venlafaxine (hypertension, higher overdose toxicity), TCAs (cardiotoxic and lethal in overdose; useful in neuropathic pain at low dose), bupropion (seizure risk, avoid in eating disorders), MAOIs (dietary restrictions and drug interactions; specialist use).
- **Pregnancy and breastfeeding**: weigh risks; sertraline is often preferred in breastfeeding; avoid paroxetine in early pregnancy; untreated depression carries risks too.

**Treatment-resistant depression** (failure of two adequate trials): confirm diagnosis and adherence, exclude bipolarity and comorbid substance use or medical illness, then augment (lithium, quetiapine, aripiprazole, brexpiprazole, T3), combine (mirtazapine with SSRI/venlafaxine), switch, add psychotherapy, consider **esketamine nasal spray** (specialist), **transcranial magnetic stimulation**, and **electroconvulsive therapy (ECT)**.

**ECT** is the most effective treatment for severe, psychotic or treatment-resistant depression, catatonia, and when rapid response is needed (food/fluid refusal, high suicide risk, pregnancy). Response rates 60–80%; cognitive adverse effects (transient memory impairment); requires anaesthesia and consent (or legal safeguards).

**Safety.** Every patient with depression needs a risk assessment (section 9), a safety plan, and involvement of family or carers as appropriate.

## 3. Bipolar disorder

### 3.1 Recognition

Bipolar I: at least one **manic** episode (≥ 1 week, or any duration if hospitalisation is needed): abnormally elevated, expansive or irritable mood, with increased energy plus at least three of: grandiosity, decreased need for sleep, pressured speech, flight of ideas, distractibility, increased goal-directed activity or psychomotor agitation, risky behaviour (spending, sexual indiscretion). Marked impairment, psychosis, or need for hospitalisation. Bipolar II: hypomania (≥ 4 days, no marked impairment or psychosis) plus major depression. Cyclothymia is milder.

Features suggesting bipolar rather than unipolar depression: onset before 25, family history of bipolar disorder, recurrent episodes, rapid onset/offset, psychotic or atypical features (hypersomnia, psychomotor retardation), antidepressant-induced switching or agitation, postpartum onset, seasonal pattern. Ask directly: "Have you ever had a period when you needed far less sleep than usual yet had lots of energy?" Collateral from family is crucial. Use the Mood Disorder Questionnaire as a screen.

### 3.2 Management

- **Acute mania**: stop antidepressants and stimulants, ensure safety, minimise stimulation, treat sleep. First-line: antipsychotic (olanzapine, quetiapine, risperidone, aripiprazole, haloperidol) and/or mood stabiliser (lithium, valproate). Benzodiazepines for acute agitation/insomnia short term. Admit when risk to self or others, severe psychosis, or poor self-care; use mental health legislation if necessary.
- **Bipolar depression**: quetiapine, lurasidone, lumateperone, cariprazine, lamotrigine (maintenance), olanzapine–fluoxetine; avoid antidepressant monotherapy.
- **Maintenance**: **lithium** is the gold standard (reduces relapse and suicide); valproate (avoid in women of childbearing potential unless no alternative: teratogenic), lamotrigine (slow titration for rash; SJS risk; valproate doubles lamotrigine levels), quetiapine, olanzapine, aripiprazole.
- **Lithium monitoring**: baseline U&E, eGFR, calcium, TSH, ECG, weight; levels 0.6–0.8 mmol/L (0.8–1.0 acutely) 12 hours post-dose, checked 5–7 days after dose changes, then every 3–6 months; renal, thyroid and calcium every 6 months. **Toxicity** (coarse tremor, vomiting, diarrhoea, ataxia, confusion, dysarthria, seizures) is precipitated by dehydration, AKI, NSAIDs, ACE inhibitors/ARBs, thiazides. Narrow therapeutic index; long-term adverse effects: hypothyroidism, nephrogenic diabetes insipidus, hyperparathyroidism, CKD, weight gain, tremor.

## 4. Anxiety, trauma and obsessive–compulsive disorders

### 4.1 Generalised anxiety disorder (GAD)

Excessive worry about multiple domains, most days for ≥ 6 months, with at least three symptoms (restlessness, fatigue, poor concentration, irritability, muscle tension, sleep disturbance) and impairment. Frequent in primary care; often presents with somatic symptoms and comorbid depression. GAD-7: 5, 10, 15 = mild, moderate, severe.

Management: education, self-help, CBT, applied relaxation; SSRIs or SNRIs (sertraline, escitalopram, duloxetine, venlafaxine); pregabalin (risk of misuse); buspirone; hydroxyzine. **Avoid benzodiazepines beyond 2–4 weeks** (tolerance, dependence, falls, cognitive impairment, respiratory depression with opioids). Avoid prescribing for the long term. Exercise, caffeine and alcohol reduction.

### 4.2 Panic disorder

Recurrent unexpected panic attacks (abrupt peak of fear within minutes with palpitations, sweating, tremor, dyspnoea, chest pain, nausea, dizziness, derealisation, fear of dying or losing control) followed by persistent worry about further attacks or avoidance. Differentiate from cardiac (arrhythmia, ischaemia), respiratory, thyroid, phaeochromocytoma, hypoglycaemia, substance causes: ECG, TSH, glucose, as indicated. Treatment: CBT with interoceptive exposure, SSRI/SNRI (start low to avoid initial jitteriness). Explain the physiology of hyperventilation.

### 4.3 Phobias and social anxiety disorder

Marked fear of specific objects/situations or of social scrutiny, avoided or endured with distress. Exposure-based CBT is first-line; SSRIs for social anxiety; propranolol for performance anxiety only (situational).

### 4.4 Obsessive–compulsive disorder (OCD)

Obsessions (recurrent intrusive thoughts, images or urges causing distress: contamination, harm, symmetry, taboo themes) and compulsions (repetitive behaviours or mental acts to reduce distress), time-consuming (> 1 h/day) or impairing. Insight is usually preserved. Often hidden and delayed for years: ask about handwashing, checking, intrusive thoughts. Treat with **exposure and response prevention** (ERP) and/or high-dose SSRI (e.g., fluoxetine 40–60 mg, sertraline up to 200 mg) for 8–12 weeks at maximum tolerated dose; clomipramine; augmentation with low-dose antipsychotic. Distinguish from psychosis and from tic disorders. Children: PANDAS/PANS is controversial.

### 4.5 Post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) and acute stress

Exposure to actual or threatened death, serious injury, or sexual violence, followed for > 1 month by intrusive re-experiencing (flashbacks, nightmares), avoidance, negative alterations in cognition and mood, and hyperarousal. **Complex PTSD** (ICD-11) follows prolonged or repeated trauma, adding difficulties in affect regulation, self-concept and relationships.

First-line therapy: trauma-focused CBT, **EMDR**, prolonged exposure. Medications (sertraline, paroxetine, venlafaxine; prazosin for nightmares in some guidelines) are second-line. **Do not offer routine single-session psychological debriefing** after trauma (may worsen outcomes); offer practical help, support, and monitoring. Screen for substance use, depression and suicidality; trauma-informed care.

### 4.6 Somatic symptom, functional and health anxiety disorders

Persistent distressing physical symptoms with excessive thoughts, feelings or behaviours about them (somatic symptom disorder), preoccupation with having or acquiring a serious illness (illness anxiety disorder), or neurological symptoms incompatible with disease (functional neurological disorder, FND: positive signs such as Hoover sign, tremor entrainment, give-way weakness, non-epileptic attacks with eyes closed). Key principles:

- **Take symptoms seriously and examine thoroughly**; investigate sensibly but avoid repeated, redundant testing.
- **Make a positive diagnosis** (FND rests on positive clinical signs, not exclusion alone); explain it as a genuine, common, reversible disorder of nervous-system functioning, not "imagined".
- **Regular scheduled appointments** (not symptom-contingent), a single clinician coordinating care, graded activity, CBT, physiotherapy for FND, treatment of comorbid depression and anxiety. Antidepressants (SSRI/TCA) may help with pain and comorbidity.
- Remember that unexplained symptoms and organic disease can coexist; reassess when new features appear.

## 5. Psychosis and schizophrenia

### 5.1 Recognition

Psychosis: loss of contact with reality, with hallucinations (usually auditory in primary psychosis; visual, tactile, olfactory suggest organic causes), delusions (persecutory, referential, grandiose, somatic, nihilistic), disorganised thought and speech (loosening of associations, tangentiality, word salad, neologisms), disorganised or catatonic behaviour, and negative symptoms (blunted affect, alogia, avolition, anhedonia, social withdrawal).

**Schizophrenia** (DSM-5-TR): two or more of delusions, hallucinations, disorganised speech, grossly disorganised/catatonic behaviour, negative symptoms (at least one of the first three), for ≥ 1 month, with signs persisting ≥ 6 months and functional decline. Typical onset late teens to mid-30s (earlier in men). Lifetime prevalence 0.5–1%. Risk factors: family history, cannabis (especially high-potency and early use), urbanicity, migration, childhood adversity, obstetric complications.

Other categories: **brief psychotic disorder** (< 1 month), **schizophreniform** (1–6 months), **schizoaffective disorder** (mood episodes with psychosis persisting when mood is normal), **delusional disorder**, **substance-induced psychosis** (often resolves within days to weeks after cessation; may precede schizophrenia), **psychosis due to a medical condition**, **postpartum psychosis** (emergency; see chapter 57), **mood disorders with psychotic features**.

### 5.2 Assessment of a first-episode psychosis

- Mental state examination, including insight and risk (to self, others, from others, vulnerability, command hallucinations).
- Collateral history from family, friends, school/work.
- Physical examination and neurological examination; vital signs; weight, BMI, blood pressure, waist circumference.
- Investigations: FBC, U&E, LFTs, TSH, glucose/HbA1c, lipids, B12/folate, calcium, HIV, syphilis, urine drug screen, pregnancy test, ECG, **neuroimaging (MRI preferred)**. Consider EEG, lumbar puncture, autoimmune antibodies (anti-NMDA receptor encephalitis: young woman, psychosis, seizures, dyskinesias, autonomic instability, decreased consciousness), copper/caeruloplasmin (young patient with movement signs), and genetic tests selectively.
- Early referral to a specialist **early intervention in psychosis** service: reduces duration of untreated psychosis, improves outcomes.

### 5.3 Treatment

- **Antipsychotic medication** (start low, titrate; single antipsychotic): second-generation (risperidone, olanzapine, aripiprazole, amisulpride, quetiapine, paliperidone, lurasidone, cariprazine, brexpiprazole) or first-generation (haloperidol). Choose by side-effect profile and patient preference. Review at 4–6 weeks; if no response to an adequate trial, switch; **clozapine** for treatment-resistant illness (failure of two antipsychotics), persistent suicidality, or aggression: needs registered monitoring for neutropenia (ANC), myocarditis (first 4–6 weeks: troponin, CRP), constipation (potentially fatal ileus), sialorrhoea, seizures, metabolic effects.
- **Maintenance**: continue for at least 1–2 years after a first episode (relapse risk is high on discontinuation), often longer; long-acting injectables improve adherence.
- **Psychosocial**: CBT for psychosis, family intervention, supported employment, cognitive remediation, peer support, treatment of substance use.
- **Physical health**: antipsychotics cause weight gain, diabetes, dyslipidaemia, prolactin elevation, QT prolongation, extrapyramidal side effects. Monitor weight, waist, BP, fasting glucose/HbA1c, lipids at baseline, 3 months, then at least annually; prolactin if symptomatic; ECG when indicated; smoking cessation (note that stopping smoking raises clozapine and olanzapine levels by induction removal), diet and exercise programmes. Cardiovascular disease is the leading cause of early death in people with schizophrenia.

### 5.4 Antipsychotic adverse effects

| Effect | Features | Management |
|---|---|---|
| Acute dystonia | Hours to days after starting; torticollis, oculogyric crisis, laryngeal spasm | IM/IV procyclidine or benzatropine; lorazepam; reduce dose |
| Akathisia | Restlessness, inability to sit still; may increase suicide risk | Lower dose, propranolol, mirtazapine, benzodiazepine; change drug |
| Parkinsonism | Tremor, rigidity, bradykinesia | Reduce dose or switch; anticholinergic cautiously |
| Tardive dyskinesia | Months to years: orofacial and limb choreoathetoid movements | Switch to clozapine or lower-risk drug; VMAT2 inhibitors (valbenazine, deutetrabenazine) |
| Hyperprolactinaemia | Galactorrhoea, amenorrhoea, sexual dysfunction, bone loss | Switch to prolactin-sparing drug (aripiprazole), dose reduce |
| Metabolic syndrome | Weight gain, diabetes, dyslipidaemia | Monitoring, lifestyle, metformin, switch |
| QT prolongation | Torsades de pointes | ECG, correct K/Mg, avoid combination of QT drugs |
| **Neuroleptic malignant syndrome (NMS)** | Hyperthermia, "lead-pipe" rigidity, altered mental status, autonomic instability, high CK, leucocytosis; days after start or dose increase | Stop antipsychotic, ICU, cooling, fluids, benzodiazepines, dantrolene or bromocriptine in severe cases |

## 6. Delirium

### 6.1 Definition and importance

Delirium is an acute, fluctuating disturbance of attention and awareness with additional cognitive disturbance (memory, orientation, language, perception) developing over hours to days, caused by a medical condition, substance, or medication. It occurs in 15–25% of general hospital inpatients, 50–80% in ICU, and is associated with higher mortality, longer stay, institutionalisation and accelerated cognitive decline. **It is missed in more than half of cases**, especially the hypoactive subtype.

Subtypes: **hyperactive** (agitation, hallucinations, restlessness), **hypoactive** (drowsy, withdrawn, apathetic; the most common and most overlooked), **mixed**.

### 6.2 Detection

- **Core features** (CAM): (1) acute onset and fluctuating course, (2) inattention, and either (3) disorganised thinking or (4) altered level of consciousness. Test attention: months of the year backwards, serial sevens, digit span; a "4AT" screen (alertness, AMT4, attention, acute change) takes under two minutes.
- Ask relatives: "Is this their usual self?"
- **Differentiating from dementia**: delirium has acute onset, fluctuation, inattention, altered consciousness; dementia is insidious, stable, with preserved alertness until late stages. The two coexist: dementia is the strongest risk factor for delirium.
- **From depression**: depression has preserved attention and consciousness, a consistent mood.
- **From acute psychosis**: orientation and attention preserved in primary psychosis.

### 6.3 Causes: "I WATCH DEATH" and "DELIRIUMS"

Search systematically: **I**nfection (UTI, pneumonia, sepsis, cellulitis), **W**ithdrawal (alcohol, benzodiazepines, opioids), **A**cute metabolic (hyponatraemia, hypercalcaemia, uraemia, hepatic encephalopathy, hypo-/hyperglycaemia, hypoxia, hypercapnia, acidosis), **T**rauma (head injury, fracture, burns, surgery), **C**NS pathology (stroke, seizure/post-ictal, meningitis/encephalitis, tumour, subdural), **H**ypoxia, **D**eficiencies (thiamine, B12), **E**ndocrine (thyroid, adrenal), **A**cute vascular (MI, heart failure, shock, hypertensive encephalopathy), **T**oxins/drugs, **H**eavy metals.

**Medications are the most frequent reversible cause**: anticholinergics (oxybutynin, tricyclics, antihistamines, antipsychotics), benzodiazepines, opioids, steroids, H2 blockers, antiparkinsonian drugs, anticonvulsants, digoxin, fluoroquinolones, lithium. Also check for urinary retention, constipation/faecal impaction, pain, dehydration, sleep deprivation, sensory deprivation (glasses, hearing aids), unfamiliar environment, catheters, restraints.

**Investigations**: vital signs and glucose, oxygen saturation, hydration, medication review, examination for infection, retention, impaction, neurological signs; FBC, U&E, calcium, LFTs, CRP, glucose, TSH, B12/folate as indicated, blood and urine cultures, ECG, chest X-ray, ABG, ammonia, drug levels, urine drug screen; CT head if head injury, focal signs or no other cause found; EEG (non-convulsive status epilepticus, encephalitis), lumbar puncture if meningitis/encephalitis suspected.

### 6.4 Management

1. **Identify and treat the cause** (the only definitive treatment).
2. **Non-pharmacological care for all**: reorientation, regular communication, clocks and calendars, glasses and hearing aids, sleep protection (lights, noise, night-time observations), early mobilisation, hydration, nutrition, pain control, bowel and bladder care, avoid catheters and restraints, familiar faces and objects, family presence, a quiet, well-lit room.
3. **Medication review**: stop or reduce deliriogenic drugs; avoid benzodiazepines except for alcohol/benzodiazepine withdrawal or seizure.
4. **Pharmacological treatment only for severe distress or risk to self or others when non-drug measures fail**: low-dose haloperidol (0.5–1 mg oral/IM, maximum low total daily doses in older adults) or an atypical (olanzapine, quetiapine, risperidone) for the shortest time; check QT; avoid in Parkinson disease and Lewy body dementia (use quetiapine or clozapine at low doses; lorazepam if essential). Dexmedetomidine in ICU.
5. **Prevention** (multicomponent programmes, e.g., HELP) reduces incidence by 30–40%.
6. **Follow-up**: delirium can last weeks to months; inform patient and relatives, document in the discharge letter, and reassess cognition after recovery (3 months) because dementia may be unmasked.

## 7. Substance use disorders

### 7.1 Screening and assessment

Ask everybody: "In the past year, how many times have you had [5/4] or more drinks in a day?" (single-question screen), AUDIT-C (frequency, quantity, binge frequency), DAST-10 or single-item drug screen. Non-judgemental, open questions. Record quantity, pattern, route, dependence features (craving, tolerance, withdrawal, loss of control, neglect of activities, continued use despite harm), previous withdrawal seizures or delirium, treatment attempts, injection risk (HIV, hepatitis B/C testing), psychiatric comorbidity (very common), and social consequences.

The **DSM-5-TR substance use disorder** criteria (11 items; ≥ 2 = disorder: mild 2–3, moderate 4–5, severe ≥ 6).

### 7.2 Alcohol

Low-risk limits vary by country; one drink is roughly 10–14 g of alcohol; many guidelines advise no more than 14 units (UK) or lower per week, with several alcohol-free days. Risk of cancer rises from the first drink.

**Alcohol use disorder.** Features: morning drinking, tolerance, withdrawal symptoms, hepatic and gastric disease, neuropathy, cognitive deficits. Laboratory markers: raised MCV, GGT, AST>ALT, CDT, triglycerides, uric acid.

**Alcohol withdrawal.** 6–24 h after last drink: tremor, sweating, anxiety, nausea, tachycardia, hypertension, insomnia; 12–48 h: **seizures**; 48–96 h: **delirium tremens** (confusion, hallucinations, agitation, severe autonomic instability, fever; mortality up to 5% untreated). Assess with CIWA-Ar. Indicators of severe withdrawal: previous withdrawal seizures or DTs, daily heavy drinking, concurrent medical illness, abnormal LFTs, low platelets, age.

*Management.*
- **Thiamine before glucose**: parenteral thiamine (e.g., 200–500 mg IV/IM once to three times daily for 3–5 days, then oral) for all at risk of Wernicke encephalopathy (confusion, ophthalmoplegia/nystagmus, ataxia), which is under-diagnosed; also magnesium, folate, multivitamins, correct hypoglycaemia and electrolytes.
- **Benzodiazepines** are first-line: chlordiazepoxide or diazepam in a symptom-triggered or fixed reducing regimen over 5–7 days (lorazepam or oxazepam in severe liver disease, older adults). Carbamazepine as an alternative in mild-to-moderate withdrawal. Antipsychotics only as adjuncts for hallucinations.
- Ambulatory detox for mild-to-moderate withdrawal with safe home environment; admit for severe withdrawal, previous seizures/DTs, comorbidity, pregnancy, or lack of support.
- **Relapse prevention**: psychosocial interventions, mutual-help groups (AA), **naltrexone**, acamprosate, disulfiram (supervised), baclofen (specialist; evidence mixed), nalmefene, topiramate (off-label); treat comorbidity.
- **Wernicke–Korsakoff**: ophthalmoplegia, ataxia, confusion; anterograde amnesia and confabulation in Korsakoff syndrome.
- **Intoxication**: ensure airway protection, glucose, thiamine, treat trauma (always look for head injury), co-ingestion.

### 7.3 Opioids

Opioid use disorder: pupils pinpoint in intoxication, respiratory depression (treat with **naloxone**: 0.4–2 mg IV/IM/intranasal, repeated, as it has a short half-life), constipation, itching. **Withdrawal** (not life-threatening): yawning, lacrimation, rhinorrhoea, mydriasis, piloerection, myalgia, abdominal cramps, diarrhoea, vomiting, insomnia, anxiety; begins 6–12 h after short-acting, 30+ h after methadone; Clinical Opiate Withdrawal Scale (COWS).

Treatment: **opioid agonist therapy** (methadone, buprenorphine ± naloxone, slow-release morphine) reduces mortality by 50% and is the standard of care; buprenorphine induction after mild-to-moderate withdrawal has begun (avoid precipitated withdrawal), including low-dose "micro-induction"; long-acting injectables. Provide take-home naloxone, harm-reduction services (needle exchange), HIV/HCV/HBV screening and treatment, vaccinations. Check for overdose risk when tolerance falls (release from prison, after detox). Prescription-opioid-induced harm in chronic pain: taper slowly with support; do not abruptly cut.

### 7.4 Stimulants, cannabis, benzodiazepines, others

- **Cocaine/amphetamines/methamphetamine**: sympathomimetic toxidrome (tachycardia, hypertension, hyperthermia, mydriasis, agitation, psychosis, chest pain, arrhythmia, stroke, rhabdomyolysis). Treat with benzodiazepines, cooling, fluids, GTN or calcium-channel blockers for chest pain; avoid beta-blockers in acute cocaine toxicity (debated). No approved pharmacotherapy for stimulant use disorder; contingency management and CBT are effective.
- **Cannabis**: intoxication (anxiety, paranoia, tachycardia), cannabis hyperemesis syndrome (cyclical vomiting relieved by hot showers), cannabis use disorder, and a dose-related increased risk of psychosis. Withdrawal: irritability, insomnia, anorexia.
- **Benzodiazepines/Z-drugs**: withdrawal can cause seizures and delirium; taper slowly over weeks to months (10% reduction every 1–2 weeks, convert to diazepam).
- **GHB/GBL**: sudden coma, severe withdrawal (delirium) after regular use.
- **Hallucinogens, MDMA (hyponatraemia, hyperthermia, serotonin syndrome), synthetic cannabinoids and cathinones (unpredictable toxicity), ketamine (cystitis), inhalants.**
- **Tobacco**: advice and treatment at every opportunity: varenicline, nicotine replacement (combination), bupropion, behavioural support.

### 7.5 Brief intervention and motivational interviewing

The "5 As": Ask, Advise, Assess readiness, Assist, Arrange follow-up. Motivational interviewing uses open questions, affirmation, reflective listening and summaries (OARS), explores ambivalence, supports autonomy and builds confidence. FRAMES: Feedback, Responsibility, Advice, Menu of options, Empathy, Self-efficacy. Stages of change: precontemplation, contemplation, preparation, action, maintenance, relapse.

## 8. Eating disorders, sleep, personality and neurodevelopmental conditions

### 8.1 Eating disorders

- **Anorexia nervosa**: restriction leading to significantly low body weight, intense fear of weight gain, disturbed body image. Highest mortality of any psychiatric disorder (medical complications and suicide). Medical assessment: BMI (or percent of median BMI in adolescents), vital signs (bradycardia, hypotension, orthostatic changes, hypothermia), ECG (QTc), electrolytes (low K, phosphate, Mg), glucose, LFTs, FBC, amenorrhoea, DEXA. **Refeeding syndrome** (hypophosphataemia, hypokalaemia, hypomagnesaemia, thiamine deficiency, cardiac failure) when nutrition is restarted: start slow, monitor electrolytes daily, give thiamine. Admission criteria include BMI < 15 (adults), rapid weight loss, heart rate < 40, systolic BP < 90, K < 3.0, syncope, electrolyte disturbance, acute food refusal, and risk. Treatment: family-based treatment (adolescents), CBT-E, MANTRA, SSCM; no effective drug.
- **Bulimia nervosa**: binge eating with compensatory behaviours (purging, laxatives, fasting, excess exercise) at normal weight. Signs: parotid swelling, dental erosion, Russell sign, hypokalaemia, metabolic alkalosis. CBT-E; fluoxetine 60 mg.
- **Binge-eating disorder**: recurrent binges without compensation; CBT, lisdexamfetamine in selected adults.
- **ARFID** (avoidant/restrictive food intake disorder).
- **Screen** with SCOFF (Sick, Control, One stone, Fat, Food): ≥ 2 positive answers. Suspect in athletes, adolescents, diabetics (insulin omission = diabulimia), unexplained weight loss, GI complaints, amenorrhoea, osteoporosis.

### 8.2 Insomnia and sleep disorders

Insomnia: difficulty initiating or maintaining sleep or early waking, ≥ 3 nights per week for ≥ 3 months, with daytime impairment. **CBT for insomnia (CBT-I)** is first-line (sleep restriction, stimulus control, cognitive restructuring). Short-term hypnotics only for ≤ 2–4 weeks. Consider depression, anxiety, sleep apnoea, restless legs, circadian disorders, medications, substance use, pain, nocturia.

### 8.3 Personality disorders

Enduring patterns of inner experience and behaviour that deviate markedly from cultural expectations, present from adolescence or early adulthood. **Borderline personality disorder**: unstable relationships and self-image, affective instability, impulsivity, recurrent self-harm and suicidal behaviour, chronic emptiness, fear of abandonment. Treat with structured psychotherapy (DBT, MBT, schema therapy); drugs do not treat the disorder (short-term for comorbid illness or crisis only). In the acute setting: calm, consistent, validating responses; clear boundaries; avoid repeated admissions that reinforce harm; chronic suicidality is not necessarily imminent risk, but never dismiss.

### 8.4 Neurodevelopmental conditions

**ADHD** (inattention and/or hyperactivity-impulsivity from childhood, in two or more settings; persists into adulthood in about 50%; underdiagnosed in women): stimulants (methylphenidate, lisdexamfetamine), atomoxetine, guanfacine; monitor BP, pulse, weight, misuse risk. **Autism spectrum disorder**: social communication difficulties, restricted/repetitive behaviours; adults often present with anxiety, depression, burnout, or sensory issues; adapt consultations (clear communication, predictable structure, sensory considerations).

## 9. Suicide, self-harm and violence: risk assessment and emergencies

### 9.1 Suicide risk assessment

Ask directly; asking does not increase risk. Use a structured approach (C-SSRS, ASQ for young people, SAD PERSONS is discouraged as a predictor).

**Assess:**

1. **Ideation**: passive ("wish I were dead") vs active; frequency, intensity, duration, controllability.
2. **Plan**: method, specificity, lethality, access to means, preparations (note, will, giving away possessions, rehearsal).
3. **Intent**: expectation of dying, reasons for living, deterrents, ambivalence.
4. **Previous attempts**: the strongest predictor; nature, circumstances, medical lethality.
5. **Risk factors**: mental illness (depression, bipolar, schizophrenia, substance use, personality disorder, eating disorder, PTSD), hopelessness, recent discharge from psychiatric hospital (first week/first month: peak risk), recent loss (relationship, job, financial, bereavement), chronic pain or terminal illness, access to lethal means (firearms, medications, height), family history of suicide, history of abuse or trauma, social isolation, male sex (completed suicide), age extremes, LGBTQ+ youth and other marginalised groups, impulsivity, agitation, insomnia, recent self-harm.
6. **Protective factors**: social support, children at home, religious or cultural beliefs, engagement in treatment, future orientation, employment, pets. These do not override acute risk.
7. **Current mental state**: severe depression, psychosis (command hallucinations), intoxication, agitation, hopelessness.

**Formulate risk** (low, moderate, high; acute vs chronic) and plan accordingly: a risk assessment is a clinical formulation that changes over time, not a number.

**Management.**

- **Immediate safety**: do not leave a high-risk patient alone; remove means; constant observation; safe environment (ligature points); inpatient admission, using mental health legislation if the patient lacks capacity or is at serious risk and refuses.
- **Safety planning intervention** (Stanley–Brown): warning signs, internal coping strategies, social contacts for distraction, people to ask for help, professionals and crisis lines, and means restriction. This is more effective than "no-suicide contracts", which are not recommended.
- **Means restriction**: secure firearms, dispose of stockpiled medication, limit quantities of dangerous drugs (e.g., paracetamol, TCAs), barriers at high-risk sites.
- **Treat the underlying disorder**: lithium for bipolar, clozapine for schizophrenia, CBT/DBT, antidepressants, ECT.
- **Follow-up**: contact within 24–72 hours after discharge from emergency or hospital; caring contacts (brief messages) reduce reattempts.
- **Document** the risk assessment, reasoning, plan, and communication with family and other professionals.

### 9.2 Self-harm

Non-suicidal self-injury (NSSI) is common among adolescents and young adults; it is a coping strategy but a strong risk factor for later suicide. Approach with empathy, curiosity and without judgement. Assess intent, method, function, risk of suicide, medical needs (wound care; treat overdose per toxicology guidelines; paracetamol overdose requires N-acetylcysteine; do not rely on patient's account of timing and dose), psychosocial context. Offer a safety plan and psychological therapies (DBT, CBT). Substituting "ice cubes or snapping bands" is not recommended as a coping technique, since it replicates the pain pattern. After any overdose, assess capacity, intent and ongoing risk before discharge from the general hospital; liaison psychiatry.

### 9.3 Assessing and managing violence and agitation

**Risk factors**: previous violence, psychosis with persecutory delusions or command hallucinations, intoxication, impulsivity, personality disorder, delirium, dementia, acute stressors, ownership of weapons, threats. Warning signs: pacing, clenched fists, loud speech, staring, restlessness, muttering, tense body language.

**De-escalation** is first-line: calm, non-threatening posture, adequate space, exits for both, a team nearby, a quiet environment, short and clear sentences, active listening, validation of feelings, offering choices (food, drink, medication), avoid argument or confrontation, remove audience, remove dangerous objects. Never put yourself between the patient and the door.

**Pharmacological management of acute agitation** (after de-escalation fails): offer oral first (lorazepam 1–2 mg, olanzapine 5–10 mg, risperidone 1–2 mg, or haloperidol 5 mg + promethazine/lorazepam). Intramuscular if refusing or very distressed: olanzapine 5–10 mg IM (not with parenteral benzodiazepine in close succession), haloperidol 5 mg IM with lorazepam or promethazine, lorazepam 1–2 mg IM, or midazolam/droperidol per local protocol (droperidol is effective but QT prolonging). Dose-reduce in older adults, intoxication, hepatic impairment. Monitor vital signs, oxygen saturation, level of consciousness, and QTc. Treat the cause (intoxication, delirium, pain, hypoglycaemia, hypoxia).

**Physical restraint** only as a last resort, for the shortest time, with trained staff, never in a prone position, with continuous observation of airway and vital signs (positional asphyxia, excited delirium/ hyperactive delirium with severe agitation is a medical emergency with high risk of sudden cardiac arrest), and full documentation. Seclusion is regulated by law.

### 9.4 Capacity and involuntary treatment

**Capacity is decision-specific and time-specific.** A person has capacity if they can: (1) **understand** relevant information, (2) **retain** it long enough to decide, (3) **weigh** it as part of the decision, (4) **communicate** a choice. Presume capacity; support decision-making; unwise decisions are not evidence of incapacity. Psychiatric illness does not automatically remove capacity.

**Involuntary admission or treatment** is governed by national mental health law (e.g., Mental Health Act 1983 in England and Wales, state laws in the US, cantonal/national laws in Switzerland ("fürsorgerische Unterbringung/ placement à des fins d'assistance")). Principles: serious mental disorder, risk to self or others (or serious deterioration), no less restrictive alternative, procedural safeguards, right of appeal, regular review. Learn your local criteria, the forms, who can apply, and for how long. Treat under common-law necessity or emergency provisions only for urgent cases and in the person's best interest.

## 10. Psychiatric emergencies in the general hospital

| Emergency | Recognition | Key actions |
|---|---|---|
| **Suicide attempt / high risk** | Ideation with plan/intent, recent attempt | Safe environment, observation, medical stabilisation, psychiatric assessment |
| **Delirium tremens** | 48–96 h after last drink; confusion, hallucinations, autonomic hyperactivity | Benzodiazepines, thiamine, fluids, ICU if needed |
| **Neuroleptic malignant syndrome** | Antipsychotic exposure, hyperthermia, rigidity, altered consciousness, high CK | Stop drug, ICU, cooling, fluids, benzodiazepines, dantrolene/bromocriptine |
| **Serotonin syndrome** | Serotonergic drug(s), onset within hours; clonus (inducible, ocular, spontaneous), hyperreflexia, tremor, agitation, diaphoresis, diarrhoea, hyperthermia (Hunter criteria) | Stop drugs, benzodiazepines, cyproheptadine, cooling, supportive care |
| **Lithium toxicity** | Tremor, ataxia, vomiting, confusion, seizures | Stop lithium, fluids, check levels/U&E, haemodialysis in severe cases |
| **Catatonia** | Stupor, mutism, posturing, negativism, waxy flexibility, excitement | Lorazepam challenge (1–2 mg IV), repeat; ECT; stop antipsychotics unless NMS excluded; treat cause |
| **Acute dystonia** | Oculogyric crisis, torticollis, laryngeal spasm | Procyclidine IM/IV, benzodiazepine |
| **Alcohol/benzodiazepine withdrawal seizures** | Seizure after cessation | Benzodiazepine, thiamine, glucose |
| **Anti-NMDA receptor encephalitis** | Young woman, psychosis, seizures, dyskinesias, autonomic instability | MRI, EEG, LP with antibody testing, immunotherapy, search for ovarian teratoma |
| **Postpartum psychosis** | Within days to weeks of delivery; confusion, delusions, mania, risk to infant | Psychiatric emergency; admission (ideally mother–baby unit); see chapter 57 |
| **Severe agitation / violence** | See 9.3 | De-escalation, rapid tranquillisation |

## 11. Psychotropic drugs: practical safety essentials

- **Start low, go slow; use the lowest effective dose for the shortest time; review regularly.**
- **Baseline and monitoring**: ECG (QT-prolonging drugs, TCAs, antipsychotics, citalopram), electrolytes (hyponatraemia with SSRIs, carbamazepine), weight/metabolic (antipsychotics, mirtazapine, valproate), renal/thyroid (lithium), LFTs (valproate, agomelatine, duloxetine), FBC (clozapine, carbamazepine), pregnancy status.
- **Interactions**: CYP inhibitors (fluoxetine, paroxetine, fluvoxamine, bupropion inhibit CYP2D6/1A2), inducers (carbamazepine, smoking, St John's wort), serotonergic combinations, QT-prolonging combinations, anticholinergic burden, additive CNS depression (benzodiazepines + opioids + alcohol), lithium with NSAIDs/ACE-inhibitors/thiazides.
- **Older adults**: increased sensitivity, falls, hyponatraemia, delirium, orthostatic hypotension, anticholinergic effects; Beers criteria. **Antipsychotics in dementia** increase mortality and stroke risk (boxed warning): use only for severe distress or danger after non-drug approaches, at the lowest dose, with planned review at 12 weeks.
- **Pregnancy and breastfeeding**: involve specialists; avoid valproate, carbamazepine, topiramate, paroxetine in early pregnancy; lithium needs careful level monitoring; untreated illness also carries risk.
- **Discontinuation**: taper antidepressants, benzodiazepines, antipsychotics, mood stabilisers; abrupt withdrawal may cause rebound or relapse.
- **Overdose**: TCAs (QRS widening, arrhythmia, seizures: sodium bicarbonate), SSRIs (generally low toxicity except citalopram QT and seizures), venlafaxine, lithium, antipsychotics (QT, sedation), benzodiazepines (flumazenil rarely used), paracetamol co-ingestion. Contact poison centre.

## Worked cases

### Case 1: "Just tired" in a man in his fifties

**Presentation.** Mr J., 52, an engineer, attends his GP for fatigue and poor sleep for 2 months. On questioning he has lost 5 kg without trying, wakes at 4 a.m. and cannot return to sleep, feels worse in the mornings, has lost interest in cycling, and thinks "my family would be better off without me", though he denies any plan. His father died by suicide at 55. He drinks two glasses of wine nightly. TSH, FBC, glucose, B12 and U&E are normal. PHQ-9 = 21.

**Reasoning.** Severe major depressive episode with melancholic features and passive suicidal ideation, in a middle-aged man with a family history of suicide and alcohol use: high-risk profile. Exclude bipolar disorder (no history of hypomania on direct questioning and with his wife's collateral).

**Action.** Direct suicide risk assessment: no plan or intent today, but several risk factors; protective: wife, two children, engaged in care. Safety plan completed, firearms/medication access discussed, wife involved with his consent, crisis contacts given. Start sertraline 50 mg (increase to 100 mg at 2 weeks), referral for CBT, advice to reduce alcohol, review at 1 week by phone and weekly afterwards for the first month. Warn about initial side effects. Refer to psychiatry if no improvement, risk escalates, or psychotic features emerge. At 8 weeks PHQ-9 = 9; continue for at least 9–12 months after remission.

**Lessons.** Always ask directly about suicidal thoughts; middle-aged men often present with somatic complaints; rule out medical causes and bipolarity; involve the family.

### Case 2: The elderly woman who became "mad" overnight

**Presentation.** Mrs W., 84, lives alone, has mild memory problems. Two days ago she became drowsy, then agitated at night, seeing "children in the room". Today she cannot say the day or month backwards and drifts off during conversation. Temperature 37.8 °C, heart rate 104, BP 104/62, respiratory rate 22, saturation 93%, dry mucous membranes, suprapubic tenderness, bladder scan 580 mL. Recent new medications: oxybutynin and codeine. Urinalysis: leukocytes ++; CRP 94.

**Reasoning.** Delirium (acute onset, fluctuating, inattention, altered consciousness, visual hallucinations) superimposed on early dementia. Multiple precipitants: urinary retention (anticholinergic plus opioid), possible UTI or chest infection, dehydration, drugs (oxybutynin, codeine). Not primary psychosis.

**Action.** Catheterise (drain 580 mL), IV fluids, stop oxybutynin and codeine, paracetamol for pain, cultures and antibiotics based on source (UTI), chest X-ray (clear), glucose and electrolytes (sodium 128, corrected with fluid review), correct constipation, glasses and hearing aids returned, reorientation, night-time light/noise control, family at bedside, avoid antipsychotics (low-dose haloperidol 0.5 mg considered only if distress or danger). Mobilise daily with physiotherapy. Clearing over 5 days. Cognitive reassessment at 3 months; counsel family on risk of persistent decline.

**Lessons.** Think "delirium" in any acute behavioural change in an older adult; the cause is usually multifactorial; review medications first; non-drug care is the treatment.

### Case 3: A first psychotic episode

**Presentation.** Karim, 20, a university student, has withdrawn over 6 months, stopped attending lectures, and tells his brother that classmates are watching him and that voices comment on his actions. He sleeps little, is suspicious, and admits regular cannabis use (daily, high-potency) for 3 years. Physical examination normal. Urine drug screen: cannabis only. Bloods, TSH, HIV, syphilis, MRI brain normal. No family history. He is calm, with good rapport but guarded; he denies suicidal ideation and is willing to accept help voluntarily.

**Reasoning.** First-episode psychosis with persecutory delusions and third-person auditory hallucinations, a prodrome of 6 months with social decline, in a young man with heavy cannabis use. Differential: schizophreniform disorder/early schizophrenia vs cannabis-induced psychosis. Organic causes excluded.

**Action.** Refer urgently to early intervention in psychosis service. Start low-dose antipsychotic (e.g., aripiprazole 10 mg or risperidone 1–2 mg) after baseline weight, waist, BP, fasting glucose, lipids, prolactin, ECG; counsel on side effects. Family psychoeducation. Cannabis cessation support. Assess risk (none to others; self-neglect). Review at 1 week; assess response at 4–6 weeks. Plan to continue medication for at least 1–2 years. Employment/education support. Annual physical monitoring, smoking cessation.

**Lessons.** Early treatment improves long-term outcome. Always do a physical work-up, including an MRI in first-episode psychosis. Cannabis is a modifiable risk factor.

### Case 4: Palpitations and "fear of dying"

**Presentation.** Ms L., 29, was brought to the emergency department three times in two months with sudden episodes of chest tightness, palpitations, sweating, trembling, and a feeling that she was "going to die", each lasting 15–20 minutes. ECG, troponin, D-dimer (with low pretest probability), TSH and glucose were normal each time. She now avoids the subway and exercise. She drinks four coffees a day and is under work stress.

**Reasoning.** Panic disorder with agoraphobic avoidance, after exclusion of cardiac, respiratory, thyroid and metabolic causes (adequate, not repeated infinitely).

**Action.** Explain the diagnosis and physiology (adrenaline surge, hyperventilation). Reduce caffeine. Refer for CBT with interoceptive and in-vivo exposure. Start sertraline 25 mg for a week then 50 mg (warn about initial jitteriness), consider short-term low-dose benzodiazepine only if the anxiety is overwhelming, and plan to stop within two weeks. Follow up in 2 weeks. Screen for depression and alcohol use. Avoid further repeated cardiac testing unless new features appear.

**Lessons.** Rule out serious organic causes once and well; make a positive diagnosis; don't leave the patient with endless tests and unaddressed fear.

### Case 5: "I don't know what's wrong with me" after an overdose

**Presentation.** A 17-year-old girl is brought by ambulance after taking 20 tablets of paracetamol (500 mg) and two glasses of vodka one hour earlier after an argument with her boyfriend. She says she "did not really want to die" but "could not stand it". She has superficial scars on her forearms. She is alert, pulse 98, BP 112/68.

**Reasoning.** Intentional overdose in an adolescent with prior self-harm: a medical emergency and a suicide risk assessment. Paracetamol overdose may be asymptomatic initially but lethal. Co-ingestion of alcohol complicates assessment.

**Action.** Follow the poison centre/toxicology protocol: paracetamol level at 4 hours (or treat immediately if time uncertain, or dose > 150 mg/kg in a staggered or unknown-time overdose), start N-acetylcysteine according to the nomogram or local policy, check LFTs, INR, creatinine, glucose, pregnancy test. Ask about other drugs. After medical clearance, a psychosocial assessment by child and adolescent mental health services: intent, ongoing suicidal ideation, depression, trauma and abuse, bullying, substance use, family. Obtain collateral from parents, involve safeguarding if needed. Restrict home medications. Safety plan, follow-up within 48–72 hours. Do not discharge on a statement of "I'm fine" from a patient who has just attempted suicide.

**Lessons.** Treat the medical problem first, then evaluate the psychological one; do not assume low intent; adolescents' impulsive acts can be lethal.

### Case 6: Fever, rigidity and a new antipsychotic

**Presentation.** Mr H., 38, with schizophrenia, had his haloperidol raised from 10 to 20 mg ten days ago after relapse. Over 24 hours he became confused and mute, sweating, with temperature 40.1 °C, heart rate 128, BP 168/104 (labile), generalised "lead-pipe" rigidity, tremor. Labs: CK 9,800 U/L, WBC 16, creatinine 148 µmol/L, K 5.4.

**Reasoning.** Neuroleptic malignant syndrome. Differential: serotonin syndrome (clonus and hyperreflexia, more rapid onset), malignant hyperthermia, anticholinergic toxicity (dry skin), CNS infection, catatonia (malignant), heat stroke.

**Action.** Stop haloperidol. Admit to ICU/high dependency. Aggressive IV fluids, active cooling, benzodiazepines (lorazepam) for rigidity and agitation, treat hyperkalaemia, monitor renal function and CK, DVT prophylaxis; dantrolene or bromocriptine for severe cases; exclude infection (lumbar puncture, cultures) and consider ECT in refractory cases. After recovery wait at least 2 weeks before restarting an antipsychotic with low D2 potency (e.g., quetiapine, aripiprazole), at the lowest dose, slow titration; record the event.

**Lessons.** Fever plus rigidity plus altered mental status in a patient on antipsychotics is NMS until proven otherwise. Early recognition saves lives.

## Common pitfalls

1. **Diagnosing psychiatric disease before excluding delirium and medical causes**, especially in older adults, in acute-onset or late-onset presentations, and with visual hallucinations.
2. **Missing delirium** (especially hypoactive), mislabelling it as depression or dementia.
3. **Not asking directly about suicidal ideation**, or relying on "no-suicide contracts".
4. **Diagnosing unipolar depression without screening for mania or hypomania**, and then precipitating a switch with antidepressant monotherapy.
5. **Starting antidepressants without follow-up in the first weeks**, especially in under-25s and in high-risk patients.
6. **Prescribing benzodiazepines for long-term anxiety or insomnia.**
7. **Stopping psychotropics abruptly** (antidepressant discontinuation, benzodiazepine withdrawal seizures, antipsychotic relapse, clonidine/lithium rebound).
8. **Using antipsychotics routinely for agitation in dementia or delirium** without trying non-drug measures.
9. **Forgetting physical health in severe mental illness** (metabolic monitoring, smoking cessation, vaccinations, cancer screening).
10. **Giving glucose before thiamine** to an at-risk patient (Wernicke).
11. **Missing alcohol withdrawal** as a cause of post-operative agitation or seizures on day 2–3.
12. **Overlooking lithium toxicity** with dehydration, NSAIDs, ACE inhibitors, thiazides.
13. **Attributing physical symptoms to anxiety or "functional" illness** in someone who has not been properly examined, and attributing everything to a past psychiatric label (diagnostic overshadowing).
14. **Using physical restraint without airway/vital sign monitoring**, or prone restraint.
15. **Assuming lack of capacity because of a psychiatric diagnosis**, or assuming capacity because the patient is "talking sensibly".
16. **Dismissing self-harm as attention-seeking.**
17. **Failing to follow up** patients discharged after self-harm; the first week after discharge is the highest-risk period.

---

## Key points

- Start with a syndrome, exclude organic causes, take collateral, and do a mental state examination and risk assessment in every patient.
- Depression: PHQ-9 for screening and severity, always ask about mania, combine therapy and an SSRI in moderate-to-severe cases, review early, continue ≥ 6–12 months after remission, taper to stop.
- Bipolar disorder: avoid antidepressant monotherapy; lithium is the gold standard maintenance treatment but needs level and organ monitoring; valproate is contraindicated in women who may become pregnant.
- Psychosis: exclude secondary causes (MRI, bloods, drugs, autoimmune); treat early; monitor metabolic health.
- Delirium is common, dangerous and often missed: look for the cause (infection, drugs, retention, impaction, metabolic), use non-drug care, avoid antipsychotics unless necessary.
- Alcohol withdrawal: thiamine and benzodiazepines; Wernicke is under-diagnosed.
- Suicide risk assessment is ask-assess-plan: direct questions, means restriction, safety plan, follow-up after discharge.
- De-escalation first, rapid tranquillisation if needed, restraint last; monitor vital signs.
- Know the psychiatric emergencies: NMS, serotonin syndrome, lithium toxicity, catatonia, anti-NMDA receptor encephalitis, postpartum psychosis, delirium tremens.
- Capacity is decision-specific; apply your local mental health law.

## Self-assessment

**Questions**

1. A 68-year-old woman is "newly depressed", apathetic, and forgetful over two weeks, after being admitted with pneumonia. What diagnosis should you exclude before starting an antidepressant, and which bedside tests would help?
2. List five features that suggest bipolar rather than unipolar depression.
3. What is the proper order of action in a patient with suspected Wernicke encephalopathy and low blood glucose?
4. Name three common causes of drug-induced delirium in older adults and an alternative approach to each.
5. Describe the key elements of a safety plan after a suicide attempt, and why a "no-suicide contract" is not recommended.
6. A patient on lithium presents with tremor, vomiting, diarrhoea, ataxia and slurred speech. Which drugs are most likely to have precipitated this, and what do you do?
7. A young patient has hyperthermia, clonus, hyperreflexia, tremor and diarrhoea after combining an SSRI with tramadol. Name the syndrome, key distinguishing sign from NMS, and treatment.
8. What are the four components of decision-making capacity?
9. What baseline monitoring is required before and during antipsychotic treatment?
10. A man with alcohol dependence is admitted for pneumonia and develops tremor, tachycardia and visual hallucinations on day 3. Diagnosis and management?

**Answers**

1. Delirium (especially hypoactive) and dementia/cognitive impairment; test attention (months backwards, 4AT, CAM), orientation, level of alertness, collateral about baseline, look for persisting infection, hypoxia, electrolyte disturbances (hyponatraemia), drugs, urinary retention, constipation, pain. Treat delirium first; reassess mood after recovery.
2. Any five: onset before 25; family history of bipolar disorder; previous hypomanic/manic episodes; recurrent episodes; rapid onset and offset; psychotic features; atypical features (hypersomnia, psychomotor retardation); postpartum onset; antidepressant-induced agitation or switch; seasonal pattern; poor antidepressant response.
3. Give IV/IM thiamine (high dose) before or together with glucose; correct hypoglycaemia; replace magnesium and other vitamins; treat the withdrawal.
4. Anticholinergics (oxybutynin, TCAs, first-generation antihistamines: replace with mirabegron, SSRIs, non-sedating antihistamines or avoid); benzodiazepines/Z-drugs (taper slowly, use non-drug sleep measures); opioids (adjust dose, add paracetamol/regional methods, treat constipation and retention); steroids, H2 blockers, anticonvulsants, digoxin are other options.
5. Warning signs; internal coping strategies; social distraction; people to ask for help; professionals/crisis lines; making the environment safe (means restriction). Contracts do not predict or reduce suicide, may give false reassurance, and can damage therapeutic alliance.
6. Lithium toxicity; precipitants: dehydration, AKI, NSAIDs, ACE-inhibitors/ARBs, thiazide and loop diuretics, infection; stop lithium, rehydrate, check lithium level, U&E, ECG, observe; haemodialysis in severe cases (neurological symptoms, high levels, renal failure); avoid renal-toxic drugs.
7. Serotonin syndrome. Neuromuscular hyperactivity (clonus, hyperreflexia) with rapid onset (< 24 h), versus NMS's slow onset (days), "lead pipe" rigidity and hyporeflexia. Stop serotonergic drugs, benzodiazepines, cooling, fluids, cyproheptadine, ICU for severe cases.
8. Understand relevant information, retain it, weigh/use it in the decision, and communicate a choice.
9. Baseline weight/BMI, waist circumference, blood pressure, fasting glucose/HbA1c, lipids, prolactin if symptomatic, ECG (as indicated), assessment of movement disorders; repeat at 3 months, then at least annually (weight at 6 weeks, monthly for first 3 months with some drugs); clozapine requires ANC monitoring.
10. Alcohol withdrawal delirium (delirium tremens) developing 48–96 h after last drink, complicating pneumonia. Treat with benzodiazepines (symptom-triggered), parenteral thiamine, fluids, correct electrolytes, monitor in a high dependency setting, treat the pneumonia, and avoid antipsychotics as monotherapy (lower seizure threshold).
