# Appendix C: Normal Values and Reference Ranges

> **Read this first.** Reference ranges are statistical constructs: typically the central 95% of a healthy reference population, so about 1 in 20 healthy people falls outside any single range, and the more tests you order, the more "abnormal" results you will find in well people (see Chapter 4 and Chapter 69). Ranges differ by laboratory, analyser, assay, population, altitude, age and sex. **The range printed on your own laboratory report always overrides this appendix.** Values here are rounded, typical figures for teaching and quick orientation. Verify against local laboratory ranges and current guidelines before acting on any threshold.

Units are SI first, with conventional units in brackets where still widely used. Conversion factors are given at the end.

---

## C1. Adult vital signs (resting, awake)

| Parameter | Typical adult range | Comment |
|---|---|---|
| Heart rate | 60–100 /min | Fit people may be 40–60 without disease; sleep rates lower |
| Respiratory rate | 12–20 /min | Count for a full 60 s; >20 is abnormal, ≥25 is a strong marker of risk |
| Systolic BP | 100–139 mmHg | Compare with the patient's own baseline; repeat measures, correct cuff size |
| Diastolic BP | 60–89 mmHg | |
| Temperature (core/oral) | 36.1–37.8 °C | Diurnal variation about 0.5–1 °C, lowest early morning; fever usually ≥38.0 °C |
| SpO₂ (room air) | 95–100% | 92–96% may be normal in chronic lung disease; see target ranges in Chapter 40 |
| Pulse pressure | 30–60 mmHg | Narrow in tamponade, severe aortic stenosis, low output; wide in aortic regurgitation, thyrotoxicosis, sepsis (vasodilated) |
| Shock index (HR ÷ SBP) | 0.5–0.7 | >0.9–1.0 suggests significant physiological stress (Chapter 11) |
| Mean arterial pressure | 70–100 mmHg | MAP = DBP + ⅓(SBP – DBP); organ perfusion generally needs ≥65 |

**Blood pressure classification in clinic (typical, guideline-dependent):** optimal <120/80; normal <130/85; high-normal 130–139/85–89; hypertension ≥140/90 on repeated clinic measurements, confirmed with home or ambulatory monitoring (home/daytime average ≥135/85). Thresholds differ between European, US and other guidelines; check the one your service follows.

**Orthostatic hypotension:** fall of ≥20 mmHg systolic or ≥10 mmHg diastolic within 3 minutes of standing (supine for 5 minutes beforehand). In supine hypertension, a ≥30 mmHg systolic drop is often used.

## C2. Paediatric vital signs by age

Normal ranges move quickly with age. The table gives approximate ranges for awake, calm, afebrile children. A crying, febrile or frightened child will exceed them; the question is always "is this child's whole picture in keeping?"

| Age | Heart rate (/min) | Respiratory rate (/min) | Systolic BP, lower limit of normal (mmHg) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Newborn (0–28 days) | 100–180 | 40–60 | ~60 |
| Infant 1–12 months | 100–160 | 30–50 | ~70 |
| Toddler 1–2 years | 90–150 | 24–40 | ~72–76 |
| Preschool 3–5 years | 80–140 | 22–34 | ~76–80 |
| School age 6–12 years | 70–120 | 18–30 | ~82–90 |
| Adolescent 13–18 years | 60–100 | 12–20 | ~90 |

**Hypotension rule of thumb (children 1–10 years):** lower limit of systolic BP ≈ 70 + (2 × age in years) mmHg. Hypotension in a child is a late sign of decompensation; tachycardia, prolonged capillary refill, cool peripheries and altered behaviour come first (Chapters 37, 38 and 44).

**Other paediatric benchmarks**

| Parameter | Typical value / range |
|---|---|
| Capillary refill time | ≤2 s (central, e.g. sternum; peripheral readings are less reliable) |
| Temperature | Same adult range; any fever ≥38.0 °C in a baby under 3 months is an emergency until proven otherwise |
| SpO₂ | ≥95% beyond the neonatal period; newborns may take several minutes to exceed 90% after birth |
| Urine output | ≥1 mL/kg/h in children; ≥2 mL/kg/h in infants; >0.5 mL/kg/h in adults |
| Blood glucose (fasting, child) | 3.3–5.6 mmol/L; neonates: treat <2.6 mmol/L (policies vary) |

**Growth and development** (reference points only; use the growth charts your service uses, such as WHO or national charts):

- Birth weight typically 2.5–4.0 kg; most infants lose up to 10% in the first week, regaining birth weight by about 10–14 days.
- Weight roughly doubles by about 5 months and triples by 12 months.
- Failure to thrive is better defined by crossing centile lines downward on serial measurements than by a single low value.
- Typical milestones: social smile 6–8 weeks; sitting unsupported 6–9 months; walking 9–18 months; first words about 12 months; two-word phrases by about 24 months. Persistent delay, or loss of previously acquired skills, requires assessment (Chapter 74 and Part VII).

## C5. Arterial blood gas (adult, room air, sea level)

| Parameter | Typical range |
|---|---|
| pH | 7.35–7.45 |
| PaO₂ | 10.0–13.3 kPa (75–100 mmHg) |
| PaCO₂ | 4.7–6.0 kPa (35–45 mmHg) |
| HCO₃⁻ | 22–26 mmol/L |
| Base excess | –2 to +2 mmol/L |
| SaO₂ | 95–100% |
| A–a gradient | Approximately (age ÷ 4) + 4 mmHg; roughly 2–3 kPa in young adults |
| Lactate | 0.5–2.0 mmol/L |

**Compensation rules of thumb**

- *Metabolic acidosis:* expected PaCO₂ (mmHg) = 1.5 × HCO₃⁻ + 8 (± 2).
- *Metabolic alkalosis:* PaCO₂ rises about 0.7 mmHg per 1 mmol/L rise in HCO₃⁻.
- *Acute respiratory acidosis:* HCO₃⁻ rises about 1 mmol/L per 1.3 kPa (10 mmHg) rise in PaCO₂; chronic about 3.5–4.
- *Acute respiratory alkalosis:* HCO₃⁻ falls about 2 mmol/L per 1.3 kPa (10 mmHg) fall in PaCO₂; chronic about 4–5.

Venous gas pH is about 0.03 lower and PCO₂ about 0.8 kPa (6 mmHg) higher than arterial. Venous gas is acceptable for pH and bicarbonate trends, not oxygenation.

**Cerebrospinal fluid (adult)**

| Parameter | Normal | Bacterial | Viral | TB / fungal |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opening pressure | 10–20 cmH₂O (lateral decubitus) | ↑ | Normal or ↑ | ↑ |
| Appearance | Clear, colourless | Turbid | Clear | Clear to slightly turbid |
| White cells | 0–5 /µL (all mononuclear) | Often >1,000 (neutrophils) | 10–500 (lymphocytes; neutrophils early) | 10–500 (lymphocytes) |
| Protein | 0.15–0.45 g/L | >1.0 g/L | 0.4–1.0 g/L | >1.0 g/L (often very high) |
| Glucose | ≥ 60% of plasma | <40% (CSF:plasma ratio <0.4) | Normal | Low |

**Pleural fluid (Light's criteria)** — exudate if any of: pleural protein : serum protein >0.5; pleural LDH : serum LDH >0.6; pleural LDH >2/3 of the upper limit of normal serum LDH. Low pleural pH (<7.2) or glucose (<3.3 mmol/L) suggests empyema, rheumatoid disease, malignancy or TB. Additional pitfalls: diuresed heart failure can produce a false exudate; use the serum–pleural albumin gradient (>12 g/L favours transudate).

**Ascitic fluid.** Serum–ascites albumin gradient (SAAG) ≥11 g/L suggests portal hypertension (cirrhosis, heart failure, Budd-Chiari); <11 g/L suggests peritoneal disease (malignancy, TB, nephrotic syndrome). Neutrophils ≥0.25 × 10⁹/L (250/mm³) indicates spontaneous bacterial peritonitis.

## C7. Pregnancy: physiological changes and reference values

Many "abnormal" findings in pregnancy are normal adaptations. Trimester-specific, laboratory-specific ranges should be used when available.

| Parameter | Change in pregnancy | Typical findings |
|---|---|---|
| Heart rate | ↑ 10–20 /min | 80–100 /min at term |
| Blood pressure | ↓ in first and second trimester, returns toward baseline third trimester | Nadir ~mid-pregnancy; hypertension is ≥140/90 on two occasions at least 4 h apart |
| Cardiac output | ↑ 30–50% | Flow murmurs common; a systolic ejection murmur grade ≤2/6 is usually benign |
| Respiratory rate | Unchanged or slightly ↑ | A rate >20 and breathlessness at rest are not normal |
| PaCO₂ | ↓ | 4.0–4.5 kPa (30–34 mmHg): mild compensated respiratory alkalosis; "normal" 5.0 kPa suggests impending failure |
| Plasma volume | ↑ ~40–50% | Dilutional fall in Hb |
| Haemoglobin | ↓ | Anaemia: <110 g/L first and third trimester; <105 g/L second trimester |
| White cell count | ↑ | Up to 15 × 10⁹/L in pregnancy; up to 20–30 in labour |
| Platelets | Slight ↓ | Gestational thrombocytopaenia, usually >100 × 10⁹/L |
| D-dimer | ↑ progressively | Standard cutoff loses specificity; use pregnancy-adapted pathways (Chapter 70) |
| Fibrinogen | ↑ | 4–6 g/L at term |
| Creatinine | ↓ | 35–70 µmol/L; a "normal" 90 may reflect reduced function |
| eGFR | ↑ GFR 50% | CKD-EPI invalid in pregnancy |
| ALP | ↑ | Placental isoenzyme; GGT unaffected |
| Albumin | ↓ | 28–37 g/L |
| TSH | Lower in first trimester | Use trimester-specific ranges |
| Glucose | Fasting lower | Gestational diabetes thresholds follow local guidelines (e.g. fasting ≥5.1 mmol/L, 1 h ≥10.0, 2 h ≥8.5 mmol/L on 75 g OGTT per IADPSG/WHO) |
| Urinary protein | Slight ↑ | Pre-eclampsia concern if PCR >30 mg/mmol (0.3 mg/mg) or ACR >8 mg/mmol |
| Oedema, mild | Common | Sudden facial or hand oedema with hypertension is not benign |
| ECG | Left axis shift, Q wave in III, T-wave changes | May mimic pathology |

**Red flags in pregnancy** (urgent assessment): severe headache with visual disturbance or epigastric pain (pre-eclampsia), vaginal bleeding (especially with pain), reduced fetal movements, abdominal pain with shock, new breathlessness or pleuritic pain, unilateral leg swelling, fever with rigors, jaundice with itching in late pregnancy (cholestasis), and any trauma. See Chapters 59 and 72.

## C8. Older adults

| Parameter | Typical age-related change | Clinical implication |
|---|---|---|
| Heart rate response | Blunted; β-blockade and conduction disease common | Absence of tachycardia does not exclude sepsis or haemorrhage |
| Temperature | Baseline often lower; fever response blunted | A temperature of 37.5 °C may be significant; infections may present without fever |
| Blood pressure | Higher systolic, wider pulse pressure; orthostatic hypotension common | Check lying and standing |
| Creatinine / eGFR | Creatinine can look normal despite reduced GFR (low muscle mass) | Dose-adjust renally cleared drugs by eGFR/CrCl |
| Haemoglobin | Anaemia is not "normal ageing" | Always investigate |
| ESR | Rises with age | Use age-adjusted limits |
| Sodium | Increased risk of hyponatraemia (drugs, SIADH) | |
| Cognition | Mild slowing; delirium is never normal | See Chapters 33 and 61 |
| Pain response | Reduced or atypical; silent MI, painless peritonitis | Lower threshold to investigate |

| From | To | Multiply by |

| Triglycerides, mg/dL | mmol/L | 0.0113 (÷ 88.5) |
| Creatinine, mg/dL | µmol/L | 88.4 |
| Urea (BUN), mg/dL | mmol/L (urea) | 0.357 |
| Bilirubin, mg/dL | µmol/L | 17.1 |
| Calcium, mg/dL | mmol/L | 0.25 |
| Haemoglobin, g/dL | g/L | 10 |
| Albumin, g/dL | g/L | 10 |
| Uric acid, mg/dL | µmol/L | 59.5 |
| PaO₂/PaCO₂, mmHg | kPa | 0.133 |
| Temperature, °F | °C | (°F – 32) × 5/9 |
| Weight, lb | kg | 0.454 |
| Height, in | cm | 2.54 |

**Useful formulae**

- **BMI** = weight (kg) ÷ height² (m²). Adult categories: <18.5 underweight; 18.5–24.9 normal; 25–29.9 overweight; ≥30 obesity. Cut-offs may be lower for some Asian populations (e.g. ≥23 overweight, ≥27.5 obese). BMI does not distinguish fat from muscle.
- **Body surface area (Mosteller)** = √[height (cm) × weight (kg) ÷ 3600].
- **Cockcroft–Gault creatinine clearance** (mL/min) = [(140 – age) × weight (kg) × 1.23 (men) or 1.04 (women)] ÷ creatinine (µmol/L). Used for many drug-dosing decisions; use the method named in the product information.
- **Corrected calcium** (mmol/L) = measured Ca + 0.02 × (40 – albumin g/L).
- **Corrected sodium for hyperglycaemia** ≈ measured Na + 0.3 × (glucose mmol/L – 5.5) (approximation; 1.6 mmol/L per 5.6 mmol/L above normal is the traditional teaching).
- **Free water deficit** ≈ 0.6 × weight (kg) × [(Na ÷ 140) – 1] in men (0.5 in women and older adults).
- **Anion gap** = Na – (Cl + HCO₃⁻); correct for albumin as above.
- **Calculated osmolality** = 2 × Na + glucose + urea (mmol/L); osmolar gap = measured – calculated (>10 suggests toxic alcohols, mannitol, etc.).
- **Glasgow Coma Scale** = Eye (1–4) + Verbal (1–5) + Motor (1–6); range 3–15.
- **Maintenance fluid (adult, no losses)** ≈ 25–30 mL/kg/day, with electrolytes adjusted individually.
- **Pre-test probability, odds and likelihood ratios:** post-test odds = pre-test odds × LR; odds = p ÷ (1 – p) (Chapter 1).

## C10. How to use reference values wisely

1. **Ask the right question first.** A result is "abnormal" for the patient in front of you, in this context, not for the statistician's range.
2. **Look at trend and context.** A creatinine rising from 70 to 110 µmol/L is more important than a static 110; a haemoglobin of 118 g/L in a woman who was 150 last year is more important than a "normal" 125 in someone who is always 125.
3. **Know the false results.** Haemolysis raises potassium, LDH and AST; delayed processing lowers glucose; EDTA contamination lowers calcium and raises potassium; lipaemia and bilirubin interfere with many assays; biotin supplements distort thyroid and troponin immunoassays.
4. **Repeat before you act on an unexpected isolated result** unless it is dangerous in itself.
5. **Remember the multiple-testing problem.** With a 20-test panel, the probability of at least one abnormal result in a healthy person is about 64% (1 – 0.95²⁰).
6. **Match reference to population.** Children, pregnancy, ethnic variation (e.g. lower neutrophil counts in some people of African ancestry — "benign ethnic neutropenia"), altitude and athletic training all shift normal.
7. **Verify, then document.** Thresholds and drug doses change; confirm in current guidelines and local policy before relying on any figure in this appendix.
