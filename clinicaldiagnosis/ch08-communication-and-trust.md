## Chapter 8. Communication and Trust

### Learning objectives

After studying this chapter you should be able to:

1. Explain how communication quality affects diagnostic accuracy, adherence and outcomes.
2. Build rapport and respond to emotion using simple, learnable techniques.
3. Work effectively with professional interpreters, and avoid the common errors of using relatives or bilingual staff as ad hoc interpreters.
4. Explore the patient's cultural and personal explanatory model without stereotyping.
5. Communicate with patients who have hearing, vision, speech, cognitive or literacy barriers.
6. Take a collateral history appropriately, balancing confidentiality, consent and the need for information.
7. Deliver difficult news using a structured approach (SPIKES), and respond to anger, distrust and denial.
8. Recognise how bias and stigma distort history-taking and clinical judgement.

---

### 8.1 Communication is a clinical skill, not a courtesy

Communication is sometimes treated as the "soft" part of medicine, but its effects are measurable:

- **Diagnosis.** Most information needed for diagnosis comes from conversation. Patients who feel heard disclose more, and disclose the embarrassing or frightening things that matter most.
- **Adherence.** Patients who understand and agree with a plan are more likely to follow it. Many treatment "failures" are communication failures.
- **Safety.** Misunderstandings between clinician and patient, or between clinicians, contribute to errors and complaints.
- **Satisfaction and trust.** Patients complain more about communication than about clinical competence.
- **Clinician wellbeing.** Good communication techniques make consultations less stressful and less likely to run over time.

Communication has two linked tasks: **gathering information** (Chapters 6–7) and **building the relationship** (this chapter). Neither works without the other.

---

### 8.2 Building rapport and trust

Trust is built in small, repeated behaviours:

1. **Welcome and introduce.** Use the patient's name (check pronunciation), say your name and role, and explain what you are going to do.
2. **Give your full attention.** Silence the phone, avoid reading the screen throughout, face the patient, sit down. Sitting makes the consultation feel longer and more attentive to the patient, even if it is not.
3. **Be honest about time and agenda:** "I have about 15 minutes. Let's use it well."
4. **Show respect.** Ask permission before examining; ensure privacy; cover the patient; explain what you are doing.
5. **Be consistent and reliable.** Do what you said you would do (call with results, arrange referral).
6. **Admit uncertainty and error.** Saying "I don't know yet, but here is how we'll find out" builds more trust than false confidence. If something goes wrong, an honest and prompt disclosure is both ethical and the best way to preserve trust.

#### Non-verbal communication

Posture, eye contact (respecting that norms differ), tone, pace, facial expression, touch and personal space all communicate before you speak. Watch for the patient's non-verbal signals too: guarding, tears, avoidance of eye contact, clenched hands, a glance at a relative before answering.

---

### 8.3 Responding to emotion

Patients express emotion indirectly. A patient may not say "I'm frightened", but may say "My father died of cancer," or ask repeatedly about the results. Such statements are **cues**. A cue not acknowledged often reappears more intensely, or derails the consultation.

A simple, learnable framework is **NURSE:**

| Step | Example |
|---|---|
| **N**ame the emotion | "It sounds like you're really worried." |
| **U**nderstand | "That makes sense given what happened to your father." |
| **R**espect | "You've been coping with a lot, and you came in anyway." |
| **S**upport | "We'll go through this together." |
| **E**xplore | "Can you tell me more about what worries you most?" |

Practical points:

- **Empathy is not sympathy or agreement.** You can acknowledge feelings without endorsing every belief.
- **Avoid premature reassurance** ("Don't worry, it's nothing"). It cuts off the conversation and sounds dismissive if the patient's worry is real.
- **Use silence.** A pause after an emotional statement allows the patient to continue.
- **Do not hurry to fix.** Often the patient needs to be heard before they can hear.
- **Be aware of your own reactions** (fear, irritation, helplessness). Naming them to yourself prevents them from driving your behaviour.

### 8.4 Health literacy, explanation and shared decisions

#### Health literacy

A large proportion of the population has difficulty understanding health information, including educated people when anxious or unwell. Never assume; check.

**Plain language principles:**

- Use everyday words ("high blood pressure", not "hypertension"; "blood clot in the lung", not "pulmonary embolism"), or explain the term when you use it.
- **Chunk and check:** give two or three points, then ask for understanding.
- Put the most important message first.
- Use diagrams, models, and written summaries.
- Avoid statistical jargon: use natural frequencies ("about 4 out of 100 people") instead of percentages and relative risks.

#### Teach-back

Ask the patient to explain in their own words what they understood: "I want to make sure I explained it clearly. Can you tell me how you'll take this medicine?" Do not frame it as a test of the patient, but of your explanation. Repeat until the explanation is accurate.

#### Ask–Tell–Ask

1. **Ask** what the patient already knows or wants to know.
2. **Tell** the relevant information in small pieces.
3. **Ask** what they understood, and what questions they have.

#### Shared decision-making

Many diagnostic and treatment decisions have several reasonable options with different trade-offs (to test or not, watchful waiting or intervention). Shared decision-making involves:

1. Stating that a decision is needed and that there is more than one reasonable option.
2. Explaining the options, including benefits, harms and uncertainties, in understandable terms.
3. Exploring what matters to the patient (values, fears, practical constraints).
4. Reaching a decision together, or deferring if needed.
5. Reviewing the decision.

The clinician contributes knowledge and judgement; the patient contributes values and context. Neither is dispensable.

### 8.5 Working with interpreters

When you and the patient do not share a language, **the quality of the history is limited by the quality of the interpretation.** Errors in interpretation can result in misdiagnosis, wrong treatment and consent problems.

#### Use a professional interpreter

Whenever possible, use a **trained, professional interpreter** (in person, by telephone, or by video). Avoid:

- **Family members, particularly children.** They may lack the vocabulary, may filter or edit information, may be embarrassed to translate sensitive content, and may be placed in a position of unacceptable emotional burden. They may also have a conflict of interest, for example in cases of domestic violence.
- **Untrained bilingual staff,** who may not know medical terms and who may not be neutral.
- **Digital translation tools** for important clinical conversations; they may be useful for simple logistics, but they make unpredictable errors on medical content and cannot be relied on for consent, diagnosis or sensitive discussions.

In an emergency where no interpreter is available, use the best available option, record that you did so, and arrange a professional interpreter as soon as possible.

#### How to run an interpreted consultation

1. **Brief the interpreter** before the consultation: the purpose of the visit, any sensitive topics, and the need for confidentiality.
2. **Introduce everyone** and explain roles. Say that the interpreter will translate everything and keep it confidential.
3. **Seat the interpreter** slightly behind or beside the patient, forming a triangle so that you and the patient can make eye contact.
4. **Speak directly to the patient,** not to the interpreter ("How long have you had the pain?" not "Ask her how long she's had the pain").
5. **Use short sentences,** one idea at a time, and pause for interpretation.
6. **Avoid jargon, idioms and metaphors,** which may not translate.
7. **Check understanding** with teach-back, using the interpreter.
8. **Allow extra time;** an interpreted consultation takes roughly twice as long.
9. **Debrief** if the content was distressing.
10. **Document** the interpreter's name or identification number, and the method used.

Be aware that some concepts (for example, mental health terms, "heart attack", "cancer") have different meanings or stigma in different languages. A good interpreter will flag such difficulties.

### 8.6 Culture, beliefs and the explanatory model

#### Cultural humility

"Cultural competence" can mislead, because it suggests that one can master a list of facts about each group. Individuals within a group differ widely, and cultures change. **Cultural humility** is a better ideal: an attitude of curiosity, openness to learning from each patient, awareness of one's own assumptions, and respect for the patient's expertise about their own life.

Avoid two errors:

- **Ignoring culture** and assuming that all patients share your beliefs and values.
- **Stereotyping:** assuming that a patient holds beliefs because of their ethnicity, religion or country of origin.

#### Explanatory model questions

Arthur Kleinman proposed a set of questions to elicit the patient's own model of the illness:

1. What do you call the problem? What name does it have?
2. What do you think has caused it?
3. Why do you think it started when it did?
4. What do you think the illness does? How does it work?
5. How severe is it? Will it have a short or long course?
6. What are the chief problems it has caused?
7. What do you fear most about it?
8. What kind of treatment do you think you should receive? What are the most important results you hope to receive?

These questions are easy to remember and powerful. They identify beliefs that may differ from medical understanding (a fever caused by cold air; "weak blood" as a diagnosis; the "evil eye"; stigma attached to a diagnosis), and the patient's expectations.

#### The LEARN model

- **L**isten with sympathy and understanding to the patient's perception.
- **E**xplain your perceptions of the problem.
- **A**cknowledge and discuss differences and similarities.
- **R**ecommend treatment.
- **N**egotiate agreement.

#### Practical considerations

- **Religion and spirituality** can shape decisions about diet, fasting, blood products, modesty, end-of-life care and sexual and reproductive health. Ask: "Is there anything about your beliefs or customs that I should know to care for you properly?"
- **Gender and modesty.** Some patients prefer a clinician of the same gender; ask early, accommodate where possible, and offer a chaperone.
- **Family decision-making.** In some families, decisions are made collectively or by an elder. Respect this, but clarify the patient's own wishes, and ensure that the patient consents. Disclosure of diagnosis may be requested to go to the family first; explore the patient's preferences about information (some prefer not to be told details) while remaining honest.
- **Traditional and complementary medicine.** Ask openly and without judgement, as these may interact with prescribed treatments or delay care.
- **Time and punctuality, eye contact, touch, personal space, forms of address** vary; if in doubt, ask or observe.
- **Trust.** Historical and personal experiences of discrimination and mistreatment, including within health systems, affect trust. Acknowledge and earn it.

### 8.7 Barriers: hearing, vision, speech, cognition and literacy

| Barrier | Strategies |
|---|---|
| **Hearing loss** | Reduce background noise, face the patient, speak clearly (not shouting), check hearing aids are in and working, use written notes or a pocket amplifier; for sign-language users, book a **qualified sign-language interpreter** |
| **Visual impairment** | Introduce yourself and say when you approach or touch; describe what you are doing; offer large-print or audio information; guide, don't grab |
| **Speech difficulty** (dysarthria, aphasia, stuttering) | Allow time; do not finish sentences; use yes/no questions; picture boards; written or communication aids; involve a speech therapist |
| **Intellectual disability** | Use simple language, short sentences, pictures; speak to the patient, not only the carer; give extra time; use known communication passports; check consent and capacity |
| **Dementia or delirium** | Reduce noise; speak slowly and simply; one question at a time; use visual and orientation cues; involve a trusted carer; avoid correcting the patient unnecessarily |
| **Low literacy** | Avoid reliance on written leaflets; use pictograms and demonstrations; ask "Would you like me to go through it with you?" before handing forms |
| **Anxiety, pain, intoxication** | Treat pain; minimise distraction; repeat key information; use written instructions; reassess understanding later |
| **Children** | Speak at the child's level; use play; involve the child in the history as age allows; explain procedures; keep parents calm |

Always ask patients what **they** find helpful: "Is there anything that would make it easier for us to talk?"

### 8.8 Collateral history

A **collateral history** is information provided by someone other than the patient: a relative, friend, carer, witness, ambulance crew, care-home staff, general practitioner, or previous records. It is essential when the patient:

- is **unconscious, confused, delirious or demented;**
- has **amnesia** or cannot remember an event (seizure, syncope, head injury);
- is **a child** or **has an intellectual disability;**
- is **very ill or in pain** and unable to speak in detail;
- is **psychotic** or **lacks insight;**
- has **difficulties with language or speech;**
- may be **minimising or concealing** (alcohol, drugs, self-harm, abuse).

#### Principles

1. **Identify the source:** who they are, how well they know the patient, and when they last saw the patient well.
2. **Consent and confidentiality.** If the patient has capacity, ask their permission before obtaining information from or sharing information with others. If the patient lacks capacity, you may seek information in their best interests, sharing only what is necessary. In emergencies, take the information you need. **Listening to relatives is always allowed;** sharing details with them may be restricted. Follow your local law and policy.
3. **Interview the collateral source separately** if there is a possible conflict, such as suspected abuse or a family dispute.
4. **Ask specific questions:**
   - "What happened? What did you see?" (for events)
   - "What is the patient like normally?" (baseline function, cognition, mobility, speech)
   - "When was the patient last completely well? What has changed?"
   - "Any previous episodes? Any medical history, medicines, allergies, alcohol or drug use?"
   - "Who else saw it? Could I contact them?"
5. **Assess reliability:** a witness who saw a collapse differs from someone who heard of it second hand. Reports can be coloured by emotion or by the relationship.
6. **Record the source** and note discrepancies between the patient's account and the collateral.

#### Typical questions by scenario

**Seizure or collapse:** What was the patient doing before? Warning? Colour? Eyes open or closed? Jerking (how long, which limbs)? Incontinence? Tongue biting? Responsiveness? How long to recover? Confusion afterwards? Any injuries?

**Confusion in an older adult:** How is he different from his usual self? How fast did it change? Hallucinations? Sleeping pattern? Eating and drinking? Falls? New medicines? Constipation or urinary difficulty? Any recent illness?

**Child with fever:** Feeding, wet nappies, activity, rash, breathing, colour, drowsiness, contacts with sick people, immunisations.

**Intentional ingestion or injury:** Time, substance, quantity, containers found, other substances, state of mind, previous attempts, access to means.

### 8.9 Difficult conversations

#### Breaking bad news: SPIKES

A widely used six-step protocol (Baile and Buckman):

| Step | Actions |
|---|---|
| **S**etting | Private place, sit down, no interruptions, phone off, allow the patient to bring a support person, introduce everyone |
| **P**erception | "What is your understanding of what has been happening?" |
| **I**nvitation | "How much would you like me to tell you about the results?" Some want all details, some prefer headlines |
| **K**nowledge | Give a **warning shot** ("I'm afraid the news is not as good as we'd hoped"), then **short, clear statements**; avoid jargon; pause |
| **E**motions | Respond to emotions with empathy (NURSE); allow silence and tears; do not rush into treatment details |
| **S**trategy and **S**ummary | Outline next steps, agree a plan, summarise, check understanding, arrange follow-up and support |

Tips:

- Tell the patient the diagnosis **early** in the conversation, once the setting is established. Delay increases anxiety.
- **Avoid euphemism** ("growth", "shadow") if the patient wants the truth, but also avoid brutality: "I'm sorry to say that the biopsy showed cancer."
- After the news, patients may not retain much; offer written information and a follow-up appointment.
- Do not offer unrealistic hope, and do not take away all hope; focus on what can be done.
- Ask: "What worries you most now?"

#### The angry patient or relative

Anger is often a signal of fear, loss of control, pain or perceived neglect. Steps:

1. **Stay calm, sit or stand at a safe distance with an exit,** speak slowly and quietly.
2. **Listen** without interrupting, until the person has said what they need to say.
3. **Acknowledge the emotion:** "I can see you're very upset, and I want to understand what happened."
4. **Apologise** where appropriate ("I'm sorry you had to wait so long") without necessarily admitting fault.
5. **Clarify the problem** and what the person wants.
6. **Offer what you can do** and be honest about what you cannot.
7. **Set boundaries** if the behaviour becomes abusive or threatening: "I want to help, but I can't do so if I am shouted at."
8. **Ensure safety** (colleagues, security, documentation).

#### Denial

Denial can be a coping mechanism. Do not confront it harshly. Explore: "What do you think is going on?" Provide information at the patient's pace, while ensuring that serious risks (for example, refusal of urgent treatment) are discussed clearly. Return to the topic at later visits.

#### Distrust and refusal

Patients may distrust the system, the profession, or a specific clinician. Ask: "Is there something that worries you about this treatment, or about coming here?" Respect autonomy; ensure the patient is informed and has capacity. If a patient declines advice, document the discussion, offer alternatives and an open door.

#### Capacity

Adults are presumed to have **decision-making capacity** unless shown otherwise. For a particular decision, the patient must be able to:

1. **Understand** the relevant information.
2. **Retain** it long enough to decide.
3. **Weigh** it as part of the decision.
4. **Communicate** the decision.

Capacity is decision-specific and time-specific. An unwise decision does not by itself mean lack of capacity. Support patients to make their own decisions (simplify information, choose the best time, treat reversible causes) before concluding they cannot.

### 8.10 Bias, stigma and diagnostic error

Clinicians, like all humans, hold unconscious attitudes that influence how they listen, what they ask, and what they believe. Evidence shows disparities in how pain is assessed and treated by race, gender and other characteristics; in how symptoms of coronary disease are recognised in women; in how patients with mental illness, obesity, substance use disorders or disability are evaluated; and in how patients with limited English are examined and investigated.

Stigmatising labels ("frequent flyer", "drug-seeker", "non-compliant", "historian unreliable") make it easy to dismiss real symptoms (Chapter 2: affective bias and psych-out error). To reduce the effect:

- **Notice** your assumptions and emotional reaction to the patient.
- **Use neutral, descriptive language** in documentation, since the notes will be read and copied forward by other clinicians.
- **Apply the same diagnostic threshold** to all patients. Ask: "Would I do the same if this patient were different (age, sex, background, language)?"
- **Take every symptom seriously,** even in people with substance use, psychiatric illness or a record of many consultations; they are at increased risk of missed physical illness.
- **Seek feedback** and learn from complaints and near-misses.
- **Learn about the communities you serve.**

### 8.11 Communication in special settings

#### Telephone and video consultations

- Confirm identity and location; ensure privacy at both ends.
- Be explicit about the limits of remote assessment.
- Without visual cues, **verbalise** your listening (brief acknowledgements, summaries).
- Ask the patient to describe what you cannot see (colour, breathing, rash); on video, use the camera to observe breathing, speech and general appearance.
- Provide explicit safety-netting and the route to in-person care.

#### Written communication

Letters, discharge summaries and results should be clear, concise and patient-centred. Put the key message at the top. Copy letters to patients when appropriate.

#### Handover to colleagues

Use a structured format (SBAR: Situation, Background, Assessment, Recommendation) and state your uncertainty (Chapters 2 and 9). Close the loop: confirm that the recipient has understood the actions required.

### 8.12 Worked cases

#### Case 1: The son as interpreter

*A 66-year-old woman, a recent immigrant who speaks little English, attends with her 15-year-old son, who offers to translate. She reports "stomach pain." Her son says "she has a bit of a stomach ache, nothing much" and the doctor prescribes an antacid.*

Two days later she is admitted with an acute abdomen due to a perforated duodenal ulcer. Afterwards, with a professional interpreter, she explains that she had severe epigastric pain for weeks, vomited blood twice, and had taken large amounts of ibuprofen for her arthritis; she had not wished to alarm her son or discuss vomiting blood with him present.

*Lessons.* The son, out of embarrassment or protectiveness, filtered the information. The patient may not have felt able to speak freely. Using a professional interpreter and offering time with the interpreter alone would have uncovered the alarm features (haematemesis, NSAID use, duration) and altered management.

#### Case 2: Breaking bad news with SPIKES

*A 54-year-old man attends to hear the result of a lung biopsy, accompanied by his wife. CT showed a mass and enlarged lymph nodes. The biopsy confirms small cell lung cancer.*

**Setting:** a private room; the doctor sits, phone off, and introduces a specialist nurse.

**Perception:** "Before I go through the results, can you tell me what you understand so far?" Patient: "They said there was a shadow, and I think it might be serious, but I hoped it was an infection."

**Invitation:** "Would you like me to give you all the details, or the main points first?" Patient: "Just tell me straight."

**Knowledge:** "I'm afraid the news is not good. The biopsy shows that the shadow is a cancer of the lung." *(pause)*

**Emotions:** The wife starts to cry; the patient is silent. Doctor: "I can see this is a terrible shock. Take your time." *(waits)* Patient: "How long have I got?" Doctor: "That is an understandable question. We need a few more tests to know exactly how far the cancer has spread, and then I can give you a better idea. What I can say now is that this type of cancer is serious, but there are treatments that can help, and we'll be with you throughout."

**Strategy and summary:** The doctor outlines next steps (staging scans, a meeting with the oncology team within a week), provides a written summary and contact numbers, and arranges a follow-up call from the nurse the next day.

*Lessons.* Clear information at the patient's pace, a warning shot, silence and empathy, and a concrete plan make a painful conversation bearable.

#### Case 3: The explanatory model

*A 38-year-old woman attends repeatedly with fatigue and "weak blood." Her haemoglobin and ferritin are normal. She is frustrated and says doctors "don't listen."*

The doctor uses explanatory model questions: "What do you call this problem? What do you think is causing it?" She explains that in her family, "weak blood" is blamed on stress, grief and poor diet, and that her mother died young of a "blood disease." She fears she has the same condition. She is also grieving a recent bereavement and sleeping poorly.

*Assessment.* There is no evidence of anaemia. Her concern is the fear of inherited blood disease; her symptoms are consistent with grief, low mood and poor sleep (screen for depression; check for other causes such as thyroid disease). Explaining the normal blood results and exploring her mother's illness (perhaps obtaining records) addresses the fear. A plan for mood, sleep and support follows.

*Lessons.* A diagnostic label from the patient ("weak blood") does not necessarily map onto a medical diagnosis. Exploring beliefs reveals the real concern.

#### Case 4: Collateral history in a non-verbal patient

*A 84-year-old woman from a care home is brought to hospital, quiet, refusing food for two days, with a temperature of 37.4 °C and a pulse of 100. She cannot give a coherent history.*

The care-home nurse reports that she was walking independently and chatting until three days ago; she began to seem withdrawn, then started to cry out when she was moved from her chair. She has had a new prescription for a diuretic one week ago.

*Assessment.* An acute change from baseline suggests delirium. The cry on movement points to pain, perhaps a hip or pelvic fracture from an unwitnessed fall; the diuretic can cause hyponatraemia, dehydration or postural hypotension. Examination of hips and pelvis, imaging, and electrolytes are indicated.

*Lessons.* Collateral history gave the baseline and timeline, and redirected the work-up beyond "poor intake."

#### Case 5: Anger and fear

*A man shouts at the reception desk because his mother, 80, has waited seven hours with a swollen leg. The nurse calmly asks him to sit with her, listens, and says: "I understand you're frightened for your mother and that this wait is unacceptable. I'm sorry. I'll find out exactly where she is in the queue and let you know within ten minutes." She also checks on the mother, who is found to have a hot, swollen, tender calf and is moved up for urgent assessment for deep-vein thrombosis.*

*Lessons.* Listening to the angry relative led to the discovery of a clinically important situation. The response that worked was to acknowledge the emotion, take responsibility for action, and follow through.

### 8.13 Common pitfalls

- **Using family members (especially children) as interpreters.**
- **Not checking understanding** (nodding does not mean "I understand").
- **Information overload** at the end of a consultation.
- **Jargon and euphemism.**
- **Assuming culture from appearance or surname.**
- **Premature reassurance,** which closes the conversation.
- **Ignoring non-verbal cues and emotion.**
- **Failing to obtain a collateral history** where the patient is unable to give one.
- **Breaching confidentiality** by sharing information with relatives without justification.
- **Stigmatising language** in notes and handover.

---

### 8.14 Key points

- Communication affects diagnosis, safety, adherence and trust; it is a core clinical skill.
- Respond to emotional cues with a simple framework (NURSE), and use silence.
- Use plain language, teach-back and shared decision-making.
- Use professional interpreters, brief them, and speak to the patient directly.
- Practise cultural humility; elicit the patient's explanatory model rather than assuming.
- Adapt your communication to sensory, cognitive, speech and literacy barriers.
- Take a collateral history whenever the patient cannot give a reliable account, respecting confidentiality.
- Use a structured approach (SPIKES) for bad news, and a calm, empathic approach for anger.
- Be aware of bias and stigma, and apply the same diagnostic rigour to every patient.

### 8.15 Self-assessment

1. Why is a 15-year-old child a poor choice as interpreter for his mother's consultation?
   *Answer: The child may lack medical vocabulary, may filter or alter information out of embarrassment or protectiveness, is placed in an inappropriate emotional role, and the mother may withhold sensitive information in his presence.*

2. What does SPIKES stand for?
   *Answer: Setting, Perception, Invitation, Knowledge, Emotions, Strategy and Summary.*

3. A patient says "Don't worry, I'm sure it's just stress," and then looks away. What kind of communication event is this, and how might you respond?
   *Answer: A concealed emotional cue or an attempt to minimise. Respond with an open acknowledgement: "You seem a little worried. What's on your mind?" and allow silence.*

4. List four elements of capacity assessment.
   *Answer: Understand, retain, weigh the information, and communicate a choice.*

5. Give three situations in which a collateral history is essential.
   *Answer (any three): unconscious/confused patient; seizure or collapse with amnesia; child; dementia; psychosis; suspected concealment of substance use or self-harm; intellectual disability.*

6. Which questions from Kleinman's model help reveal fears and expectations?
   *Answer: "What do you fear most about it?" and "What kind of treatment do you think you should receive, and what results do you hope for?"*
