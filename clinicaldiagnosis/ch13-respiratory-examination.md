## Chapter 13. Respiratory Examination

### Learning objectives

After studying this chapter you should be able to:

1. Perform a complete respiratory examination in a logical sequence: general inspection, hands, face, neck, chest inspection, palpation, percussion, and auscultation.
2. Assess work of breathing and recognise signs of respiratory distress and impending respiratory failure.
3. Identify the normal and abnormal percussion notes, breath sounds and added sounds, and explain their mechanisms.
4. Recognise the characteristic bedside patterns of consolidation, pleural effusion, pneumothorax, lobar collapse, pulmonary fibrosis, airflow obstruction and pulmonary oedema.
5. Recognise signs of tension pneumothorax and life-threatening asthma as clinical emergencies.
6. Use simple bedside tests (peak flow, oximetry, sputum inspection) to support the examination.
7. Integrate findings with the history to choose the next investigation.

---

### 13.1 Approach and preparation

The respiratory examination answers five questions:

1. **How hard is the patient working to breathe, and is gas exchange adequate?**
2. **Is the airway patent?**
3. **Is the problem in the airways, the lung parenchyma, the pleura, the chest wall, the pulmonary circulation, or outside the chest?**
4. **Is there evidence of chronic disease or of its cause (smoking, occupational exposure, systemic disease)?**
5. **Is there a complication, such as respiratory failure, right heart failure, or malignancy?**

#### Preparation

- Introduce yourself, explain, obtain consent, and offer a chaperone where appropriate.
- Expose the **entire chest** to the waist (cover women with a sheet and uncover only as needed, Chapter 19). The back is examined with the patient sitting forward.
- Position the patient **sitting at 45°** first; later, sitting forward for the back.
- Good light; a quiet room; warm hands and stethoscope.
- Stand at the patient's **right**.

---

### 13.2 General inspection

Begin at the end of the bed and watch for 30–60 seconds (Chapter 10). Count the **respiratory rate for a full 60 seconds** while pretending to take the pulse (the patient may change breathing if aware).

#### Respiratory rate and pattern

| Finding | Description | Typical causes |
|---|---|---|
| **Normal** | Adults 12–20 breaths/min, quiet, regular | – |
| **Tachypnoea** | >20/min | Hypoxia, pneumonia, PE, heart failure, anxiety, pain, acidosis, fever, sepsis |
| **Bradypnoea** | <10/min | Opioids, raised ICP, hypothermia, respiratory exhaustion (ominous) |
| **Kussmaul breathing** | Deep, rapid, sighing | Metabolic acidosis (diabetic ketoacidosis, renal failure, poisoning) |
| **Cheyne–Stokes** | Cycles of crescendo-decrescendo breathing followed by apnoea | Heart failure, stroke, raised ICP, high altitude, opioid or terminal states |
| **Biot (ataxic) breathing** | Irregular, unpredictable pauses | Brainstem lesion, severe raised ICP |
| **Apneustic breathing** | Prolonged inspiratory pauses | Pontine lesions |
| **Obstructive pattern** | Prolonged expiration | Asthma, COPD |
| **Paradoxical (see-saw) breathing** | Chest and abdomen move in opposite directions | Diaphragmatic paralysis, severe respiratory muscle fatigue, spinal cord injury |

#### Work of breathing

Look for:

- **Accessory muscle use** (sternocleidomastoid, scalenes, trapezius), **intercostal and supraclavicular recession**, **nasal flaring**, **tracheal tug**, and in children **subcostal recession and head bobbing**.
- **Pursed-lip breathing** (COPD), which generates intrinsic PEEP.
- **Tripod position** (leaning forward with hands on knees).
- **Inability to speak in full sentences.**
- **Exhaustion, drowsiness, confusion** (late and dangerous).

#### Noise

- **Stridor:** a harsh, high-pitched, usually inspiratory sound from upper airway obstruction (foreign body, tumour, epiglottitis, croup, laryngeal oedema). It is an emergency.
- **Wheeze:** audible at the end of the bed in severe airflow obstruction.
- **Audible secretions, cough, hoarse voice, snoring.**

#### Colour

Central cyanosis (tongue), pallor, plethora (polycythaemia), flushing with CO₂ retention.

#### Objects around the bed

Oxygen (nasal cannulae, mask, home concentrator), inhalers, nebulisers, peak flow meter, **sputum pot** (inspect: purulent, mucoid, blood-stained, frothy pink, foul), cigarettes, tracheostomy, chest drain, CPAP machine.

### 13.3 The hands

| Finding | Significance |
|---|---|
| **Nicotine staining** | Tobacco use (heavy smoker) |
| **Clubbing** | Lung cancer, bronchiectasis, lung abscess, empyema, cystic fibrosis, pulmonary fibrosis, mesothelioma. Not due to COPD alone (Chapter 10) |
| **Hypertrophic pulmonary osteoarthropathy** | Wrist pain and swelling in lung cancer (and other intrathoracic disease) |
| **Peripheral cyanosis** | Cold, poor perfusion |
| **Flapping tremor (asterixis)** | CO₂ retention (hypercapnia), also liver or kidney failure |
| **Wasting of the small muscles of the hand and weakness** | Pancoast tumour infiltrating C8/T1 |
| **Tremor, tachycardia** | Beta-agonist use |
| **Warm, bounding hands** | CO₂ retention |
| **Rheumatoid hands, sclerodactyly** | Interstitial lung disease associated with connective tissue disease |
| **Pulse** | Tachycardia (hypoxia, PE, bronchodilators), pulsus paradoxus (severe asthma) |

Also note **pulse rate and rhythm**, and check for **pulsus paradoxus**: an exaggerated fall in systolic pressure on inspiration (>10 mmHg) occurs in severe asthma, tamponade and COPD exacerbations.

---

### 13.4 Face, neck and upper airway

#### Face and eyes

- **Central cyanosis:** examine the tongue and lips.
- **Horner syndrome** (ptosis, miosis, anhidrosis): Pancoast tumour at the lung apex compressing the sympathetic chain.
- **Pallor, plethora, facial swelling** (SVC obstruction).
- **Conjunctival or periorbital oedema.**
- **Nasal polyps** (asthma, aspirin sensitivity, cystic fibrosis).
- **Dentition** (aspiration, lung abscess).
- **Oral thrush** (inhaled steroids, immunosuppression, HIV).
- **Pursed lips, nasal flaring.**

#### Neck

- **Lymph nodes** (cervical and supraclavicular): lung cancer, TB, sarcoidosis, lymphoma (Chapter 10).
- **JVP:** raised in cor pulmonale, tension pneumothorax, SVC obstruction (non-pulsatile), PE (massive).
- **Trachea:** Position the tip of your finger in the suprasternal notch and slide it backwards; the trachea should be central. Compare the gaps on either side between the trachea and the sternoclavicular joints. Displacement away from the lesion suggests a **tension pneumothorax** or a large pleural effusion; displacement toward the lesion suggests lobar collapse, pulmonary fibrosis or pneumonectomy. The **cricosternal distance** (normally 3–4 finger breadths) is reduced in hyperinflation (COPD).
- **Tracheal tug:** downward movement of the trachea with inspiration in severe airflow obstruction.
- **Accessory muscle hypertrophy.**
- **Subcutaneous emphysema** (crepitus on palpation): pneumothorax, ruptured oesophagus, tracheal injury.
- **Thyroid enlargement or retrosternal goitre** (stridor, tracheal deviation).
- **Venous distension and collateral veins** across the chest and neck (SVC obstruction).

---

### 13.5 Inspection of the chest

Look from the front, sides and from above/behind.

| Feature | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Barrel chest** (increased anteroposterior diameter) | Hyperinflation (emphysema, severe asthma), but also normal in the elderly |
| **Pectus excavatum** | Sunken sternum; may reduce lung volume slightly; usually benign |
| **Pectus carinatum** (pigeon chest) | Childhood asthma, rickets, congenital |
| **Harrison's sulcus** | Horizontal groove along the diaphragm attachments: chronic childhood asthma, rickets |
| **Kyphoscoliosis** | Restrictive lung disease; chest wall deformity |
| **Asymmetric expansion or flattening** | Collapse, fibrosis, pleural thickening on the affected side |
| **Bulging intercostal spaces** | Large effusion, tension pneumothorax |
| **Intercostal indrawing** | Airway obstruction |
| **Scars** | Thoracotomy, lobectomy, pneumonectomy (flattened side), chest drain, thoracoplasty (TB), sternotomy, radiotherapy marks or skin tattoo |
| **Chest drain** | Pneumothorax, effusion, empyema |
| **Visible veins** | SVC obstruction |
| **Skin lesions** | Herpes zoster, neurofibromas, spider naevi, gynaecomastia |
| **Muscle wasting** | Chronic respiratory disease, cachexia |

### 13.6 Palpation

#### Chest expansion

Place your hands on the lower chest, with fingers around the sides and thumbs pointing toward the spine, and meet in the midline on expiration. Ask the patient to take a deep breath. Normal: each side moves symmetrically by at least 5 cm (a total of about 5 cm on tape measurement at the nipple line). **Reduced or delayed expansion on one side** indicates disease on that side (collapse, consolidation, fibrosis, effusion, pneumothorax, pain). **Bilaterally reduced** expansion: COPD, ankylosing spondylitis, restrictive disease, neuromuscular disease. Repeat at the upper chest (apical expansion) and posteriorly.

#### Apex beat and heaves

Check the position of the apex beat (displacement implies mediastinal shift or cardiac enlargement; Chapter 12). A parasternal heave suggests right ventricular hypertrophy from pulmonary hypertension.

#### Tactile vocal fremitus

Place the ulnar edge of the hand flat on the chest wall, and ask the patient to say "ninety-nine" (or "one-one-one"). Compare symmetrical sites. **Increased** fremitus: consolidation (solid lung conducts better). **Decreased** fremitus: pleural effusion, pneumothorax, emphysema, thick chest wall, bronchial obstruction. It is less sensitive than vocal resonance and often unnecessary if auscultation is done carefully.

#### Tenderness and crepitus

Local rib tenderness (fracture, metastasis, costochondritis). Crepitus (subcutaneous emphysema).

### 13.7 Percussion

**Technique.** Place the middle finger of your non-dominant hand flat on the chest wall, in an intercostal space (not over a rib), fingers pressed firmly. Strike the **middle phalanx** with the tip of the middle finger of your other hand, with a quick, relaxed wrist movement. Compare symmetrical areas, working from the top to the bottom, in front, laterally (axillae) and behind (patient sitting forward with arms folded across the chest to move the scapulae). Percuss the clavicles directly (apical).

#### Percussion notes

| Note | Quality | Cause |
|---|---|---|
| **Resonant** | Normal lung | – |
| **Hyper-resonant** | Louder, drum-like | Pneumothorax, emphysema, asthma (hyperinflation) |
| **Dull** | Soft, short | Consolidation, collapse, pleural thickening, fibrosis, mass |
| **Stony dull** | Very dull, "like percussing a thigh" | **Pleural effusion** |
| **Tympanic** | Hollow | Gastric bubble (normal on the left), very large pneumothorax |

#### Liver and cardiac dullness

Determine the **upper level of liver dullness** (normally the 5th–6th rib in the right mid-clavicular line) and cardiac dullness. Loss of cardiac dullness suggests hyperinflation or left-sided pneumothorax; liver pushed down suggests hyperinflation.

#### Level of an effusion

The upper margin of a pleural effusion may be recognised as a transition from stony dullness to resonance, often with a curving upper border (the "Ellis line"; not reliably seen). Percuss to find the upper level of dullness.

### 13.8 Auscultation

#### Technique

- Use the **diaphragm**. Place it firmly on the bare chest wall.
- Ask the patient to **breathe deeply through an open mouth**, but not too fast (avoid dizziness).
- Listen to **at least one full inspiration and expiration at each site**, comparing left and right in a symmetrical zig-zag pattern: apices, upper zones, mid zones, lower zones, axillae (front), then the back.
- Listen to the whole chest, including the **back**, where most of the lung lies, particularly the lower lobes.
- Attend to the **quality**, **intensity**, **inspiration-to-expiration ratio**, and **added sounds**.

#### Normal breath sounds

- **Vesicular (normal)** breath sounds: soft, rustling, with inspiration longer than expiration; expiration is quiet, with no gap between them.
- **Bronchial breath sounds** (normal over the trachea and upper sternum): harsh, hollow, with equally long inspiration and expiration, and a distinct pause between the two phases.
- **Bronchovesicular** sounds over the main bronchi are an intermediate pattern.

#### Abnormal breath sounds

| Finding | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Reduced or absent breath sounds** | Pleural effusion, pneumothorax, collapse (obstructed bronchus), emphysema, obesity, large mass, severe asthma ("silent chest": an ominous sign) |
| **Bronchial breathing** (over the lung periphery) | Consolidation (pneumonia), localised fibrosis, collapse with a patent bronchus; above a pleural effusion |
| **Prolonged expiration** | Airflow obstruction (asthma, COPD) |
| **Amphoric breathing** | Large cavity or pneumothorax with bronchopleural fistula (rare) |

#### Added (adventitious) sounds

| Sound | Description | Cause |
|---|---|---|
| **Wheeze** | Continuous, musical, high-pitched (usually expiratory) | Narrowed airways: asthma, COPD, bronchiolitis, cardiac failure ("cardiac asthma"), foreign body (monophonic, localised), tumour (fixed monophonic wheeze) |
| **Polyphonic wheeze** | Multiple notes, widespread | Diffuse airway obstruction (asthma, COPD) |
| **Stridor** | Harsh, inspiratory (or biphasic) sound, loudest over the neck | Upper airway obstruction |
| **Fine crackles** ("Velcro") | Late inspiratory, high-pitched, repeated, not cleared by coughing | Interstitial pulmonary fibrosis, asbestosis, hypersensitivity pneumonitis, connective tissue disease; sometimes early pulmonary oedema |
| **Medium crackles** | Mid-to-late inspiratory | Pulmonary oedema, pneumonia |
| **Coarse crackles** | Early inspiratory (and expiratory), low-pitched, bubbling, may clear with coughing | Bronchiectasis, chronic bronchitis, pneumonia with secretions, heart failure |
| **Pleural friction rub** | Creaking, leathery, both phases, localised, associated with pleuritic pain | Pleurisy: pneumonia, pulmonary embolism, connective tissue disease, malignancy |
| **Pleuropericardial rub** | Synchronous with the heartbeat rather than the breath | Pericarditis |
| **Rhonchi** | Low-pitched continuous snoring sounds | Secretions in large airways (often clear after coughing) |

#### Vocal resonance

Ask the patient to say "ninety-nine" while you auscultate. Normally the words are muffled and indistinct.

- **Increased vocal resonance**, with words clear: consolidation.
- **Whispering pectoriloquy:** the whispered words are heard clearly through the stethoscope (consolidation).
- **Aegophony ("E to A" change):** the patient says "ee" and you hear "ay" over the upper edge of an effusion or consolidation.
- **Decreased vocal resonance:** effusion, pneumothorax.

These signs add little when bronchial breathing is clearly heard but can confirm consolidation.

### 13.9 Putting it together: bedside patterns

| Condition | Trachea | Expansion | Percussion | Breath sounds | Added sounds | Vocal resonance |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Normal** | Central | Equal | Resonant | Vesicular | None | Normal |
| **Lobar pneumonia (consolidation)** | Central | Reduced on affected side | **Dull** | **Bronchial** | Coarse crackles; pleural rub | **Increased** |
| **Pleural effusion** | Central or shifted **away** if large | Reduced | **Stony dull** | **Reduced or absent**; bronchial at the upper margin | None (rub early) | **Decreased** (aegophony at upper edge) |
| **Simple pneumothorax** | Central | Reduced | **Hyper-resonant** | **Reduced or absent** | None | Decreased |
| **Tension pneumothorax** | **Shifted away** | Reduced, hyperexpanded | Hyper-resonant | Absent | None | Decreased |
| **Lobar collapse** | Shifted **toward** | Reduced | Dull | Reduced or absent (if bronchus blocked) | – | Decreased (if bronchus blocked) |

### 13.10 Bedside tests to support the examination

- **Pulse oximetry:** target 94–98% for most patients; 88–92% for patients at risk of hypercapnic respiratory failure (COPD, obesity hypoventilation, neuromuscular disease). Beware its limitations (Chapter 11).
- **Peak expiratory flow (PEF):** a simple measure of airflow obstruction. Compare with the predicted value (based on age, sex, height) or the patient's personal best. Values <50% of best indicate a severe exacerbation; <33% is life-threatening in acute asthma.
- **Sputum inspection:** purulent (infection), mucoid, rusty (pneumococcal pneumonia), blood-stained (haemoptysis: cancer, bronchiectasis, TB, PE, pneumonia), pink frothy (pulmonary oedema), foul-smelling (anaerobic abscess), black (coal dust).
- **Forced expiratory time:** ask the patient to exhale fully and quickly through an open mouth; listen over the trachea: >6 seconds suggests airflow obstruction.
- **Counting ability:** how high can the patient count in one breath (normally >40)? A rough measure of vital capacity in neuromuscular disease.
- **Single breath count and ability to speak in sentences:** simple markers of severity.
- **Spirometry:** obstructive pattern (FEV₁/FVC <0.7), restrictive (reduced FVC, normal ratio).
- **Arterial blood gas:** pH, PaO₂, PaCO₂, bicarbonate.
- **Chest X-ray, CT, ultrasound** (Chapters 66–67).

### 13.11 Respiratory emergencies recognised at the bedside

#### Tension pneumothorax

A **clinical diagnosis**; do not wait for an X-ray. Signs: severe respiratory distress, hypotension, tachycardia, raised JVP, tracheal deviation (late), hyper-resonant percussion with absent breath sounds on the affected side, asymmetrical chest movement, cyanosis. Treatment: **immediate needle decompression** (or finger thoracostomy) followed by a chest drain.

#### Life-threatening asthma

Features: **silent chest**, cyanosis, poor respiratory effort, exhaustion, arrhythmia, hypotension, confusion or altered consciousness, PEF <33%, SpO₂ <92%, normal or rising PaCO₂ (a "normal" CO₂ in a breathless asthmatic is ominous). Treatment: oxygen, high-dose nebulised bronchodilators, systemic steroids, IV magnesium, early senior and ICU involvement.

#### Upper airway obstruction

Stridor, drooling, inability to swallow, muffled voice, tripod position, anxiety. Call for senior and anaesthetic/ENT help; do not examine the throat of a child with suspected epiglottitis; keep the patient calm and upright.

#### Massive haemoptysis

Position with the bleeding side down, secure the airway, and obtain urgent specialist help.

#### Hypercapnic respiratory failure

Drowsiness, flapping tremor, warm bounding hands, bounding pulse, headaches, papilloedema (late). Give controlled low-flow oxygen (target SpO₂ 88–92%) and consider non-invasive ventilation.

### 13.12 Worked cases

#### Case 1: Pneumonia

*A 58-year-old man has had 3 days of fever, productive "rusty" sputum, and right-sided pleuritic chest pain. RR 28, SpO₂ 90% on air, temperature 39.1 °C, pulse 110. Right lower zone: reduced expansion, dull percussion, bronchial breathing, coarse crackles, and increased vocal resonance.*

**Reasoning.** Dullness, bronchial breathing, increased vocal resonance and crackles in one area indicate **lobar consolidation**: community-acquired pneumonia (likely pneumococcal given the rusty sputum and abrupt onset). The pleuritic pain suggests pleural involvement.

**Next steps.** Assess severity (CURB-65; Chapter 70): confusion, urea, RR ≥30, BP, age ≥65. Chest X-ray, blood tests, blood cultures and urine antigens as appropriate, oxygen, antibiotics within the first hour for severe disease. Look for complications (effusion: "stony dull" at the base, failure to improve).

#### Case 2: Pleural effusion

*A 66-year-old woman has progressive breathlessness over 6 weeks, a dry cough and weight loss. The left lower chest: reduced expansion, stony dull percussion extending to the mid-zone, reduced tactile fremitus, absent breath sounds, with bronchial breathing at the upper margin. The trachea is mildly shifted to the right. A left mastectomy scar is noted.*

**Reasoning.** Features indicate a **large left pleural effusion.** The mastectomy scar suggests **malignant effusion** from breast cancer recurrence. Other possibilities: heart failure (usually bilateral), pneumonia/empyema, pulmonary embolism, TB, cirrhosis, nephrotic syndrome.

**Next steps.** Chest X-ray and ultrasound-guided diagnostic aspiration (protein, LDH, pH, glucose, cell count, cytology, culture). Apply Light's criteria to distinguish exudate from transudate. Therapeutic drainage if symptomatic; discuss pleurodesis or an indwelling catheter for recurrent malignant effusions.

#### Case 3: Tension pneumothorax

*A 28-year-old tall, thin man collapses after a car crash with severe chest pain. He is distressed, RR 40, pulse 140, BP 70/40, SpO₂ 82% on oxygen. His JVP is distended, the trachea is deviated to the left, the right hemithorax is hyper-resonant with absent breath sounds.*

**Reasoning.** Hypotension, hypoxia, raised JVP, tracheal deviation, hyper-resonance and absent breath sounds = **right tension pneumothorax** until proved otherwise. This is a clinical diagnosis. The delay for imaging can be fatal.

**Action.** Immediate needle or finger decompression (second intercostal space mid-clavicular line, or fifth intercostal space in the anterior axillary line, according to local protocol), followed by chest drain insertion and a chest X-ray after decompression.

#### Case 4: Pulmonary fibrosis

*A 70-year-old man has had 18 months of progressive exertional breathlessness and dry cough. He has finger clubbing. Chest expansion is reduced bilaterally; percussion is normal. There are fine "Velcro" crackles at both bases, late inspiratory, unaltered by coughing. No wheeze. SpO₂ is 93% at rest, falling to 85% on walking.*

**Reasoning.** The combination of clubbing, fine basal late-inspiratory crackles, restrictive breathlessness and exertional desaturation suggests **interstitial lung disease**, most likely **idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis** (or fibrosis associated with asbestos, drugs such as amiodarone or nitrofurantoin, connective tissue disease, or hypersensitivity pneumonitis).

**Next steps.** High-resolution CT chest, spirometry with gas transfer (DLCO), autoantibodies, exposure history, multidisciplinary review.

#### Case 5: Acute severe asthma

*A 19-year-old woman with asthma has a three-day history of worsening wheeze, now unable to speak in full sentences. RR 32, pulse 128, SpO₂ 91% on air, PEF 140 L/min (her best is 450). Chest: hyperinflated, with widespread polyphonic wheeze. Thirty minutes later, after initial treatment, she appears exhausted; the chest is almost silent.*

**Reasoning.** PEF ~31% of best, inability to speak in sentences, tachycardia and tachypnoea indicate **acute severe asthma**; the new silent chest and exhaustion indicate progression to **life-threatening asthma** (minimal airflow). The reduction of wheeze is not an improvement here.

**Action.** Senior and ICU involvement, oxygen, continuous nebulised salbutamol and ipratropium, IV hydrocortisone or oral prednisolone, IV magnesium, arterial blood gas (normal or high CO₂ is ominous), chest X-ray to exclude pneumothorax, consider IV aminophylline or salbutamol, prepare for intubation.

#### Case 6: Dyspnoea with a normal chest examination

*A 34-year-old woman using oral contraception returns from a long-haul flight. She has sudden-onset breathlessness and mild right-sided pleuritic pain. RR 26, pulse 112, SpO₂ 93% on air. The chest is clear. Her right calf is mildly swollen and tender.*

**Reasoning.** A normal chest examination does not exclude significant respiratory pathology. The combination of risk factors (long flight, oestrogen), unilateral leg swelling, tachycardia, hypoxia and pleuritic pain indicates **pulmonary embolism**. Chest signs are often absent, or limited to a rub.

**Next steps.** Pre-test probability (Wells), D-dimer where appropriate, CT pulmonary angiography (or V/Q scan), leg ultrasound, ECG, and anticoagulation started early if suspicion is high (Chapters 45–46, 70).

### 13.13 Common pitfalls

- **Counting the respiratory rate poorly** (or not at all).
- **Examining the front only.** Most of the lung, and most pneumonia and effusion, is behind.
- **Percussing over a rib** or tapping too hard.
- **Mistaking a displaced trachea for a normal one** by failing to compare the sides.
- **Misinterpreting a silent chest** in asthma as improvement.
- **Missing crackles because of poor technique** (bell vs. diaphragm, patient breathing too quietly).
- **Assuming that crackles that clear on coughing are significant,** or that crackles do not exist because they were not heard.
- **Waiting for an X-ray in suspected tension pneumothorax.**
- **Giving high-flow oxygen** uncritically to patients at risk of hypercapnia.
- **Reassurance from a normal chest** in a patient with a high pre-test probability of pulmonary embolism.
- **Failing to examine for cause or complications:** lymph nodes, clubbing, hepatomegaly, bone tenderness, neurological signs in suspected lung cancer.

---

### 13.14 Key points

- Count the respiratory rate and assess the work of breathing before touching the patient.
- Follow a sequence: general inspection, hands, face and neck (trachea, lymph nodes, JVP), chest inspection, palpation (expansion, fremitus), percussion, auscultation (front and back), then supporting tests.
- Percussion note and breath sounds together identify the pattern: consolidation (dull, bronchial), effusion (stony dull, absent), pneumothorax (hyper-resonant, absent), fibrosis (fine crackles), airflow obstruction (wheeze, prolonged expiration).
- Tracheal position separates large effusion/tension pneumothorax (away) from collapse/fibrosis (toward).
- Tension pneumothorax and life-threatening asthma are clinical diagnoses requiring immediate treatment.
- A normal chest examination does not exclude pulmonary embolism or early disease.
- Combine bedside signs with oximetry, peak flow and imaging.

### 13.15 Self-assessment

1. Describe the percussion note and breath sounds over (a) lobar pneumonia and (b) a large pleural effusion.
   *Answer: (a) Dull percussion with bronchial breathing and increased vocal resonance; (b) stony dull percussion with reduced or absent breath sounds and decreased vocal resonance.*

2. Which direction does the trachea move in tension pneumothorax, and in lobar collapse?
   *Answer: Away from the tension pneumothorax; toward the side of collapse.*

3. What type of crackles are characteristic of interstitial pulmonary fibrosis?
   *Answer: Fine, late-inspiratory ("Velcro") crackles at the bases that do not clear with coughing.*

4. List four features of life-threatening asthma.
   *Answer (any four): silent chest, cyanosis, poor respiratory effort, exhaustion, confusion, arrhythmia, hypotension, PEF <33%, SpO₂ <92%, normal or raised PaCO₂.*

5. Why can a normal chest examination coexist with pulmonary embolism?
   *Answer: A pulmonary embolus may not cause consolidation, effusion or wheeze; findings may be limited to tachycardia, tachypnoea, hypoxia and leg signs.*

6. Why is a low-flow oxygen target (88–92%) used in some patients?
   *Answer: Patients with chronic hypercapnic respiratory failure (e.g. COPD) may retain CO₂ with high-flow oxygen, causing respiratory acidosis.*
