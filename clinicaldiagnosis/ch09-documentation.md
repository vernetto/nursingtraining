## Chapter 9. Documentation

### Learning objectives

After studying this chapter you should be able to:

1. Explain the purposes of clinical documentation and the qualities of a good record.
2. Write a clear SOAP note, and adapt the structure for admission, progress, outpatient and emergency settings.
3. Create and maintain a problem list, and write an assessment that shows diagnostic reasoning.
4. Document consent, capacity, refusal, uncertainty and safety-netting.
5. Use a structured handover tool (SBAR, I-PASS) and convey illness severity and contingency plans.
6. Recognise the risks of electronic records (copy-forward, template bloat) and avoid them.
7. Avoid abbreviations, ambiguities and judgemental language that lead to errors.

---

### 9.1 Why we document

The clinical record is not an administrative chore. It is a **clinical tool, a communication medium, and a legal document.** A good record does at least six things:

1. **Supports continuity of care.** Another clinician (or you, three weeks later) can understand what was found, what you thought, and what you planned.
2. **Supports diagnostic reasoning.** Writing the assessment forces you to organise your thoughts, and reveals gaps.
3. **Communicates between team members**, across shifts, specialties and settings.
4. **Provides a legal and professional record** of what was done and why. The common adage is: *"If it wasn't written down, it wasn't done."*
5. **Enables audit, teaching, research and quality improvement.**
6. **Is a record the patient may read.** In many systems, patients have access to their notes, so the language must be respectful and accurate.

A poor record is a patient-safety hazard. Missing information, ambiguity and inaccuracy are recurring causes of diagnostic delays, medication errors and failures at handover.

---

### 9.2 Principles of good documentation

| Principle | In practice |
|---|---|
| **Accurate** | Record what was observed or said, not what was assumed. Distinguish fact from inference |
| **Contemporaneous** | Write at the time or as soon as possible afterwards; if late, say so ("Late entry, written at 14:30 for events at 11:00") |
| **Attributable** | Sign, print your name, grade/role, and contact number (or electronic identifier) |
| **Dated and timed** | Every entry has date and time (24-hour clock). Time matters in emergencies |
| **Legible** | Handwriting must be readable; electronic entries are preferable but need accuracy |
| **Complete** | Include the key positives and relevant negatives; the plan; who was informed |
| **Concise** | Say what matters; avoid padding |
| **Objective and respectful** | Use neutral, descriptive terms; avoid judgemental words ("uncooperative", "drug-seeker", "frequent flyer") |
| **Clear on uncertainty** | State what you think and how sure you are |
| **Secure and confidential** | Protect patient information; do not access records without a legitimate reason |
| **Corrections visible** | Never erase or overwrite; on paper, a single line through the error, signed and dated; in electronic records use the amendment function |

Further good habits:

- Record **who gave the history** (patient, relative, interpreter) and whether an interpreter was used.
- Quote **key patient statements** in their own words.
- Record **vital signs with values** (not "obs stable").
- Record **what you told the patient** and what they understood or decided.
- Record **time-critical events** (time of arrival, time antibiotics given, time of call to the senior).
- **Never alter a record after an adverse event** to make it look better. Alterations are discoverable, and unethical.

### 9.3 The SOAP note

The **SOAP** format, derived from Lawrence Weed's problem-oriented medical record, is the most widely used structure for clinical entries.

| Section | Content |
|---|---|
| **S**ubjective | What the patient says: chief complaint, history of present illness, relevant past, medication and social history, review of systems; patient's ideas, concerns, expectations |
| **O**bjective | What you observe and measure: vital signs, examination findings, test results, imaging |
| **A**ssessment | Your interpretation: a summary statement (problem representation), the leading diagnosis, the differential, the reasoning, and the severity/risk |
| **P**lan | What happens next: investigations, treatment, referral, patient education, safety-netting, follow-up |

#### Example (outpatient)

> **S:** 58 M, 2 weeks of central chest pressure on exertion (climbing stairs, carrying shopping), lasting ~5 min, relieved by rest. Radiates to L arm. Mild breathlessness with episodes. No rest pain, no syncope, no palpitations. No pleuritic pain, no cough, no leg swelling. Episodes increasing in frequency over 2 weeks. Hypertension, ex-smoker (20 pack-years, quit 5 yrs). Father died of MI at 60. Meds: amlodipine 5 mg od. NKDA. Patient worried "it's my heart like Dad".
>
> **O:** Looks well. HR 78 reg, BP 148/88 (R arm), RR 14, SpO₂ 98% RA, T 36.6 °C. JVP not raised. HS I + II + 0, no murmurs. Chest clear. No peripheral oedema, calves soft. Abdomen soft, non-tender. ECG: sinus rhythm, rate 76, no ST/T changes, no Q waves.
>
> **A:** 58 M with hypertension and family history of premature CAD, 2-week history of exertional chest pressure with arm radiation, relieved by rest and increasing in frequency. **Probable unstable (crescendo) angina — high risk.** Differential: stable angina (less likely given progression), pulmonary embolism (no pleuritic pain, no risk factors, low probability), aortic stenosis (no murmur), GORD/musculoskeletal (pain pattern is exertional). Resting ECG normal does not exclude ischaemia.
>
> **P:** Same-day referral to chest pain / cardiology service. Aspirin 300 mg stat (no contraindication; no allergy). GTN spray prescribed, instructions given. Troponin today. Advised no strenuous activity until assessment. Safety-net: call emergency services if pain at rest, > 15 min, or not relieved by GTN. Explained working diagnosis; patient understands and agrees. Informed on-call cardiologist (Dr X) at 11:20 — will see within 4 h. *Signed [name, role], 10:55 on 05/10/2026.*

This note is short but does four things well: it states the key positives and negatives, it records the reasoning, it makes the plan concrete, and it documents safety-netting and communication.

#### Variations on the format

- **SOAPIER** adds Intervention, Evaluation, Revision (common in nursing, allied health and mental health).
- **APSO** puts Assessment and Plan first; useful for readers who want the conclusion immediately.
- **SBAR** (see 9.6) is a communication format rather than a note format.
- **DAR** (Data, Action, Response) is often used in nursing.
- **Admission note ("H&P")**: chief complaint, HPI, past medical/surgical history, medications, allergies, family history, social history, review of systems, examination, results, assessment and plan (by problem).
- **Progress note**: interval events, subjective, vitals/exam, results, assessment and plan (by problem).
- **Emergency department note**: triage time, presenting problem, key history, exam, work-up, differential, treatments with times, response, disposition, and instructions given.
- **Procedure note**: indication, consent, operator, assistants, technique, findings, complications, specimens, post-procedure plan.
- **Telephone/telehealth note**: identity verification, mode, who was present, symptoms, advice, safety-netting, follow-up.

### 9.4 Writing a good assessment

The Assessment is the most valuable and the most often neglected part. A note without an assessment is just data. A good assessment contains:

1. **A one-line summary (problem representation)** as in Chapter 3: age, sex, key background, time course, key features.
2. **Your leading diagnosis (or the problem) and how certain you are** ("probable", "possible", "cannot yet exclude").
3. **The differential diagnoses**, especially the dangerous ones, with a brief explanation of why they are more or less likely.
4. **The key supporting and contradicting findings.**
5. **Severity or risk** (e.g. CURB-65 score, Wells score, early warning score).
6. **What is unknown** and how you will resolve it.

#### Weak versus strong

| Weak | Strong |
|---|---|
| "Chest pain, ?cardiac." | "58 M, exertional chest pressure for 2 wk, increasing frequency, hypertension, FH premature CAD. Probable crescendo angina. PE and dissection considered, less likely (no pleuritic features, no risk factors, no tearing pain, equal arm BPs). ECG normal; troponin pending." |
| "UTI." | "78 F, 2 days of new confusion and dysuria. Dipstick leucocytes +++, nitrite +. Probable UTI as cause of delirium, but other sources not yet excluded (chest clear, abdomen soft, no new drugs). Urine and blood cultures sent." |
| "Anxious, no organic cause." | "No red flags on history or examination; normal ECG and thyroid function. Symptoms (episodic palpitations, tingling in hands, fear) consistent with panic episodes, supported by triggers. Organic causes less likely but would revisit if exertional symptoms develop." |

Avoid the following:

- **Label without reasoning** ("?viral").
- **Question marks used as a substitute for a stated probability.** Use words ("possible", "probable").
- **Anchoring labels** copied from a previous note.
- **"Social admission", "poor historian"** and similar phrases that substitute for assessment.

### 9.5 The problem list

The **problem-oriented medical record** organises the chart around the patient's *problems* rather than around chronological events. The **problem list** is a living summary at the front of the record that lists:

- **Active problems** (diagnoses and unresolved symptoms or abnormal findings) with date of onset;
- **Chronic conditions** with status;
- **Resolved problems** (kept for history);
- **Significant risk factors**, allergies, and psychosocial issues (e.g. lives alone, carer role, language needs).

#### Rules for a useful list

1. **Prioritise:** most urgent or most clinically important first.
2. **Be specific:** "Type 2 diabetes mellitus, on metformin + gliclazide, HbA1c 62 mmol/mol (7.8%), retinopathy mild" is better than "diabetes".
3. **Use a diagnosis when you have one; a symptom or finding when you do not** ("Unexplained weight loss, 6 kg over 3 months"; "Iron-deficiency anaemia — cause not yet identified").
4. **Keep it current:** add new problems, resolve old ones, remove errors and duplicates. A cluttered, outdated list undermines safety.
5. **Link plan to problem:** each problem should have a plan (investigate, treat, monitor, or "no action; reviewed").
6. **Retire diagnostic labels** that were not confirmed. A "suspected" diagnosis that persists through successive notes without confirmation becomes a false fact (diagnostic momentum, Chapter 2).

#### Example

| # | Problem | Status / plan |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Acute exertional chest pain, probable unstable angina | Active; troponin, cardiology review today, aspirin, GTN |
| 2 | Hypertension | Active; BP 148/88 on amlodipine; review after acute episode |
| 3 | Ex-smoker, 20 pack-years | Risk factor; consider lung cancer screening eligibility per local policy |
| 4 | Family history of premature CAD | Risk factor; lipid profile to check |
| 5 | Right inguinal hernia repair, 2011 | Resolved |

### 9.6 Handover

**Handover** (handoff) is the transfer of responsibility and information from one clinician or team to another: at shift change, transfer between wards, referral, or discharge. Failures of handover are a leading cause of avoidable harm. Information is lost, uncertainties are converted to certainties, and outstanding tasks are forgotten.

#### Principles

- **Do it face to face** (or by direct call) when patients are high risk; written alone is not enough.
- **Use a structured format.** Structure reduces omissions and makes the listener's task easier.
- **Prioritise the sickest patients and the most pressing tasks.**
- **Be concise and specific.**
- **Distinguish facts, working diagnoses and plans.**
- **Say what might go wrong and what to do about it** (contingency).
- **Allow questions** and ask the receiver to summarise.
- **Avoid interruptions.**
- **Document** that handover occurred.

#### SBAR

| Letter | Content | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **S**ituation | Who you are, who the patient is, and the immediate problem | "I'm calling about Mr Rossi, 72, bed 14. He has become hypotensive and tachycardic in the last hour." |
| **B**ackground | Relevant history, admission reason, treatments, results | "Admitted yesterday with pneumonia, on IV co-amoxiclav. History of heart failure and CKD stage 3." |
| **A**ssessment | What you think is happening and how severe | "BP 82/50, HR 124, RR 28, temp 38.9 °C, SpO₂ 92% on 4 L, lactate 4.2. I think he is septic and deteriorating." |
| **R**ecommendation | What you want or suggest, and by when | "I'd like you to review him now. I've started fluids and called for senior help. Could you consider ICU referral?" |

#### I-PASS

Developed for inpatient shift handover, I-PASS has been associated with reductions in medical errors and preventable adverse events.

| Letter | Content |
|---|---|
| **I**llness severity | Stable / "watcher" (concerned) / unstable |
| **P**atient summary | Events, history, assessment, plan, working diagnosis and its certainty |
| **A**ction list | To-do tasks with who/when |
| **S**ituation awareness and contingency planning | "If X happens, then do Y"; what to look out for tonight |
| **S**ynthesis by receiver | The receiver restates, asks questions, and confirms |

**Example:**

> **Illness severity:** Watcher.
> **Patient summary:** Mrs Okafor, 81, admitted 2 days ago with community-acquired pneumonia, CURB-65 of 3. Known heart failure, CKD 3 (baseline creatinine 130 µmol/L). On IV antibiotics, day 2. Improving but rising creatinine to 168 this morning.
> **Action list:** (1) Repeat U&E at 20:00. (2) Review fluid balance at 22:00. (3) Hold furosemide until renal review in the morning.
> **Situation awareness and contingency:** If urine output < 0.5 mL/kg/h over 6 h, or creatinine > 200, call medical registrar. If RR > 24 or SpO₂ < 92%, assess for fluid overload versus worsening pneumonia. Treatment ceiling: for full escalation, discussed with family.
> **Synthesis:** "So you'd like me to repeat bloods at 8 pm, keep furosemide on hold, and call the registrar if urine output falls or creatinine exceeds 200. Is she for ICU if needed?" — "Yes, full escalation."

#### Handover of diagnostic uncertainty

Use words that convey the degree of certainty. "Working diagnosis: possible pneumonia. Not yet confirmed. Alternatives not yet excluded: pulmonary embolism, heart failure." This prevents anchoring in the receiving clinician.

#### Discharge summaries and referral letters

A discharge summary should be sent promptly (ideally within 24–48 hours) and should include: reason for admission, diagnoses (with certainty), key findings and investigations, treatments, **medication changes with reasons**, **pending results with a named person responsible**, follow-up arrangements, and **advice given to the patient**. The most useful discharge summaries start with the actions required of the primary-care clinician.

A referral letter should state: the question you want answered, the urgency, relevant history, examination, results, current medications, allergies, social situation, the patient's concerns and expectations, and what the patient has been told.

### 9.7 Documenting special situations

#### Consent

Record: what the procedure or treatment is, the information given (benefits, risks, alternatives, including doing nothing), the questions asked, the patient's decision, who was present, whether an interpreter was used, and that the patient had capacity and was not under pressure. A signed form is evidence, not a substitute for the conversation.

#### Capacity assessment

Record the specific decision, the information given, the patient's ability to understand, retain, weigh and communicate, the supports used, and your conclusion. If the patient lacks capacity, record the best-interests process and who was consulted.

#### Refusal of treatment or advice

Document: the recommendation, your explanation of the risks (including potentially fatal consequences if relevant), the patient's reasons, evidence of capacity, alternatives offered, an offer to reconsider at any time, and any safety-net advice given. For patients who leave against advice, record the discussion and the information provided.

#### Interpreters and chaperones

Document who interpreted (name or identifier, and mode) and who acted as chaperone (name, role), and that the patient agreed.

#### Safety-netting

Record the specific advice given: "Advised to call 999 if chest pain at rest, lasting >15 min or not relieved by GTN; return within 24 h if fever persists." Safety-netting that was given but not documented cannot be shown to have been given.

#### Uncertainty and shared decisions

Record the options discussed, the patient's preferences and the decision. Example: "Discussed options of imaging now versus watchful waiting with review in 2 weeks. Patient prefers to wait. Red flags explained. Review 19/10."

#### Safeguarding concerns

Record facts objectively: what was said (verbatim quotes), what you observed (describe injuries precisely: size, shape, colour, location), who was present, and what you did. Do not speculate in the record; do follow local procedures and escalate.

#### Death and resuscitation decisions

Document resuscitation status, the discussion (with patient and family), the decision-maker, and the date and time; make it visible and easy to find.

#### Medication entries

Use generic names, full doses, units written out, route, frequency and indication. Document allergies and reactions.

### 9.8 Abbreviations and ambiguity

Abbreviations save time but cause errors. Some are universal (BP, ECG), others are local ("SOB", "CP") and many have multiple meanings ("MS" can mean multiple sclerosis, mitral stenosis, morphine sulfate, magnesium sulfate).

Follow the following rules:

- **Use only standard abbreviations** approved by your institution.
- **Never use** abbreviations on "do not use" lists. Commonly prohibited examples:
  - **U** or **u** for units (can be mistaken for 0, 4 or cc) → write "units".
  - **IU** (mistaken for IV or 10) → write "international units".
  - **QD**, **Q.D.**, **QOD** (mistaken for QID or each other) → write "daily", "every other day".
  - **Trailing zero** (5.0 mg may be read as 50 mg) → write "5 mg".
  - **Missing leading zero** (.5 mg may be read as 5 mg) → write "0.5 mg".
  - **MS, MSO₄, MgSO₄** → write "morphine sulfate" or "magnesium sulfate".
- **Write micrograms in full** ("mcg" is acceptable in many places; "µg" can be misread as "mg").
- **Avoid ambiguous words:** "stable", "normal" (describe what was found), "fine", "ok".
- **State laterality** in full ("right", "left"), not "R" and "L".
- **Avoid symbols** such as ">" and "<" if they can be misread; consider "greater than" in critical entries.
- **Avoid unexplained acronyms** at the first mention.

### 9.9 Electronic records: benefits and traps

Electronic health records (EHRs) improve legibility, accessibility, decision support and audit. But they create their own risks:

| Problem | Description | Mitigation |
|---|---|---|
| **Copy-forward / copy-paste** | Old text is pasted into new notes, bringing forward outdated or incorrect information (a fever that resolved, a diagnosis that was ruled out) | Review and edit every line; do not paste examination findings you did not make |
| **Note bloat** | Auto-populated data (labs, vitals, medication lists) buries the clinical narrative | Keep the assessment and plan short and clear; put key information at the top |
| **Template tyranny** | Check-boxes lead to "normal exam" entries for systems not examined | Document only what you did; use free text for key findings |
| **Alert fatigue** | Repeated, low-value alerts are ignored, including the important ones | Customise; override with reasons; report poor alerts |
| **Wrong-patient errors** | Selecting the wrong chart | Verify two identifiers every time |
| **Hidden information** | Important data are stored in tabs few people open | Use problem list and summary banners |
| **Scribing and dictation errors** | Speech recognition mishears (hypotension/hypertension) | Proofread |
| **Privacy breaches** | Curiosity-driven access, screen visible to others | Access only as required; lock screens; log out |
| **Copying the label** | Diagnoses (e.g. "UTI") are propagated through multiple notes without verification | Re-verify and restate diagnoses with level of certainty |

An EHR does not replace thinking. Writing in your own words, at least in the assessment, is a form of reasoning.

### 9.10 Writing for the patient, and the future reader

Patients often read their notes. Write as if they will, and as if a colleague, a lawyer and an auditor might. This does not mean being bland; it means being **accurate, respectful, and factual**.

- Replace "patient refused" with "Patient declined after discussion of risks."
- Replace "complains of" with "reports" or "describes." (The word "complains" implies a judgement.)
- Replace "poor historian" with "history difficult to obtain because of ... ; collateral from ..."
- Replace "non-compliant" with "not taking medication as prescribed; reasons: ..."
- Replace "frequent flyer" or "heartsink" with the factual number and reasons for attendance.
- Replace "drug-seeker" with a factual description of the request and your assessment.
- Document behaviour in observable terms ("patient raised their voice and said ...") instead of labels ("aggressive").

### 9.11 Worked cases

#### Case 1: A poor note and a better note

*A 45-year-old man attends with abdominal pain.*

**Poor note:**

> Abdo pain. ?gastritis. Pt well. Obs stable. Abdo soft. Omeprazole. Review PRN.

What is wrong: no history details (location, onset, red flags), no vital signs, "stable" is meaningless, no examination details (tenderness? guarding?), no differential, no safety-net, no time or signature.

**Better note:**

> **S:** 45 M. 3 days epigastric burning pain, worse after meals and at night, relieved partly by antacids. No vomiting, haematemesis, melaena, weight loss or dysphagia. Takes ibuprofen 400 mg tds for back pain for 3 wk. Alcohol ~12 units/wk. Non-smoker. No chest pain/breathlessness.
> **O:** Afebrile. HR 76, BP 128/82, RR 14, SpO₂ 99% RA. Abdomen soft; mild epigastric tenderness; no guarding or rebound; no masses; bowel sounds normal. Stool not examined; no melaena reported.
> **A:** Dyspepsia, probable NSAID-associated gastritis or peptic ulcer. No alarm features (age <55; no bleeding, dysphagia, weight loss, anaemia). Cardiac cause considered unlikely (pain unrelated to exertion; ECG not indicated in absence of cardiac features, but low threshold if pain changes).
> **P:** Stop ibuprofen; alternative analgesia (paracetamol). PPI (omeprazole 20 mg daily for 4 wk). Advice on alcohol. H. pylori test (stool antigen) after PPI washout per local policy. Safety-net: return urgently if vomiting blood, black stools, severe pain, persistent vomiting, or swallowing difficulty. Review in 4 wk. Patient agrees with plan. *[Name, role], 05/10/2026 15:40.*

#### Case 2: Updating a problem list

*A 70-year-old woman's problem list reads: "1. Chest infection, 2. Query heart failure, 3. Hypertension, 4. Back pain, 5. ?anaemia." At review, chest X-ray and bloods show no infection, echo shows a normal ejection fraction, her haemoglobin is normal, and the back pain resolved.*

**Revised list:**

1. Hypertension — BP controlled; continue medication.
2. Mild breathlessness — heart failure with reduced EF excluded (echo EF 60%), infection excluded; likely deconditioning; follow-up spirometry pending.
3. Resolved: chest infection (Sept 2026), lumbar back pain (Aug 2026), query anaemia (Hb 128 g/L, normal).

*Lesson.* Removing unconfirmed labels (query heart failure, query anaemia) prevents future clinicians from treating non-existent conditions.

#### Case 3: Handover failure

*On a night shift, the outgoing doctor says: "Mr Hall in bed 6 is fine, just needs bloods in the morning." He does not mention that the patient had a single episode of black stool yesterday and a haemoglobin that fell from 135 to 112 g/L in 24 hours; this is recorded only in the electronic notes.*

At 03:00 the nurse reports he is pale and dizzy. The night doctor, unaware of the earlier concern, initially attributes this to a "vasovagal episode" and does not examine for bleeding for another hour. He is found to have hypotension and a further melaena; the delay in resuscitation and endoscopy is documented in the incident review.

*What should have been said (I-PASS).* "Illness severity: watcher. Possible upper GI bleed. Haemoglobin dropped by 23 g/L in 24 h; one black stool yesterday. Action: repeat Hb at 22:00 and 04:00; keep two IV lines; group and save done. Contingency: if pulse > 100, BP < 100 systolic, or further melaena, call the registrar, start resuscitation and arrange urgent endoscopy."

*Lesson.* "Fine" is an unsafe word. Handover conveys severity and contingency, not only a list of diagnoses.

#### Case 4: Documenting a refusal

*A 62-year-old man with 3 hours of crushing chest pain and ST elevation on ECG declines admission and treatment. He says, "I'm not going into hospital; I have a business meeting in the morning."*

**Documentation:**

> 18:15. Patient has ST elevation in leads II, III, aVF. Diagnosis: inferior STEMI. Explained that this means a heart attack with a blocked coronary artery, that delay risks death or permanent heart damage, and that treatment (emergency angioplasty) is most effective now. Patient states he understands, can repeat back the risks ("I might die or my heart might be damaged"), and has weighed the consequences; he says his business meeting is the priority. Capacity assessed: understands, retains, weighs, communicates; no evidence of intoxication, delirium or mental illness. Offered: rescheduling the meeting by phone (accepted a phone call, still declined admission); contact with family (declined); aspirin 300 mg (accepted); GTN. Told that he may change his mind at any time and that the door is open. Consultant cardiologist (Dr Y) spoke to patient at 18:35; same conclusion. Patient signed refusal-of-treatment form. Safety-net: call 999 immediately if pain worsens or recurs. *[Name, role], 18:50.*

*Lessons.* The note shows information given, capacity, alternatives offered and an open door. Compare with the entry "Patient refused admission, left AMA", which protects nobody.

#### Case 5: Copy-forward error

*A patient admitted with pneumonia has "fever 39 °C, productive cough" copied through five daily notes, including the day after discharge planning when she is afebrile. The discharge summary lists "ongoing fever and cough" and the GP, reading it, orders a repeat chest X-ray and unnecessary antibiotic.*

*Lesson.* Copying forward yields misleading records. Each note should reflect the patient's state today. Time-stamped data (temperature trend) should come from the observation chart, not from yesterday's text.

### 9.12 Common pitfalls

- **Notes that contain data but no assessment.**
- **"Normal exam" entries** for systems that were not examined.
- **Undocumented conversations** (consent, safety-netting, refusal).
- **Ambiguous abbreviations and units.**
- **Using "stable", "fine", "unremarkable" without values.**
- **Copy-forward errors and persistence of unconfirmed labels.**
- **Handover by "story"** rather than by structure, severity and contingency.
- **Blaming or labelling language.**
- **Late, undated or unsigned entries.**
- **Altering records after the event.**

---

### 9.13 Key points

- Documentation is a clinical tool, a communication medium and a legal record.
- Write contemporaneously, clearly, accurately, and with date, time, and signature.
- Use SOAP (or a variant) and make the Assessment explicit: problem representation, leading and alternative diagnoses, reasoning, and certainty.
- Maintain a concise, current, prioritised problem list; remove unconfirmed labels.
- Use structured handover (SBAR, I-PASS) with illness severity and contingency plans, and state uncertainty.
- Document consent, capacity, refusal, interpreters, chaperones and safety-netting.
- Avoid ambiguous abbreviations and judgemental language.
- In electronic records, beware copy-forward, templates and alert fatigue.

### 9.14 Self-assessment

1. What are the four parts of a SOAP note?
   *Answer: Subjective, Objective, Assessment, Plan.*

2. Rewrite "Pt non-compliant with meds" in a better form.
   *Answer (example): "Patient reports taking ramipril about 3 days per week; reasons: dry cough and forgetting doses. Discussed options."*

3. What does the "S" in the second place of I-PASS stand for, and why does it matter?
   *Answer: Situation awareness and contingency planning: it tells the receiver what might happen and what to do ("if X, do Y"), enabling timely action overnight.*

4. Why is "5.0 mg" a hazardous way to write a dose?
   *Answer: A trailing zero can lead to misreading as 50 mg; write "5 mg". Similarly write "0.5 mg" not ".5 mg".*

5. Name four items you should document when a competent patient declines recommended treatment.
   *Answer (any four): recommendation; risks explained; patient's understanding and reasons; capacity assessment; alternatives offered; offer to reconsider; safety-net advice; who else was involved.*

6. How can copy-forward harm patients?
   *Answer: It propagates outdated or incorrect findings and diagnoses, creating false records that others rely on, with risks of wrong treatment or missed changes.*
