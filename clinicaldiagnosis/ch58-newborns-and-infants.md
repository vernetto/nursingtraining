# Chapter 58. Newborns and Infants

## Learning objectives

After studying this chapter you should be able to:

1. Perform the immediate assessment of a newborn (Apgar, transition, thermoregulation) and a systematic newborn and 6-week examination.
2. Recognise neonatal danger signs and the sick-looking infant, and apply a low threshold to investigate and treat suspected sepsis.
3. Assess neonatal jaundice: separate physiological from pathological, use bilirubin nomograms, and recognise when to give phototherapy or exchange transfusion.
4. Assess feeding problems, weight loss and failure to thrive, and recognise dehydration and hypernatraemia in the breastfed infant.
5. Recognise respiratory distress, congenital heart disease (including duct-dependent lesions), and the common surgical emergencies of the newborn.
6. Evaluate neonatal hypoglycaemia, seizures and hypotonia.
7. Evaluate the febrile infant under 3 months and the infant with a possible serious bacterial infection.
8. Recognise non-accidental injury, inborn errors of metabolism, and sudden unexpected death risk factors, and know the basics of newborn screening and safe sleep.

> **Note on thresholds and doses.** Neonatal thresholds depend on gestational age, postnatal age, weight and risk factors. Bilirubin charts, antibiotic doses and fluid regimens must come from your national neonatal guideline (e.g., NICE, AAP, WHO IMCI/Pocket Book). Figures here illustrate reasoning; verify before acting.

## 1. Definitions and the first hour

**Neonatal period**: first 28 days. **Infant**: first year. **Term**: 37+0 to 41+6 weeks; **preterm** < 37 weeks (moderate-to-late 32–36; very preterm 28–31; extremely preterm < 28); **low birthweight** < 2,500 g; **very low** < 1,500 g; **small for gestational age (SGA)** birthweight < 10th centile; **large for gestational age (LGA)** > 90th centile.

### 1.1 Transition and the Apgar score

At birth the lungs must clear fluid, pulmonary vascular resistance must fall, and the ductus arteriosus and foramen ovale begin to close. About 10% of newborns need some help to breathe; about 1% need extensive resuscitation.

**Apgar score** (at 1 and 5 minutes; 10 minutes if low): Appearance (colour), Pulse (heart rate), Grimace (reflex irritability), Activity (tone), Respiration; each 0–2. A score of 7–10 is reassuring, 4–6 moderately depressed, 0–3 severely depressed. The Apgar score describes the condition of the baby, **not** a trigger for resuscitation (start resuscitation on the basis of breathing and heart rate in the first 30 seconds).

**Neonatal resuscitation essentials** (see local Neonatal Life Support algorithm): dry and stimulate, keep warm (prevent hypothermia, particularly in preterm infants: plastic wrap, hat, warm room), open the airway (neutral head position), if not breathing adequately or heart rate < 100/min give 5 inflation breaths then ventilate at 30–60/min; if heart rate < 60/min after effective ventilation for 30 seconds, start chest compressions (3:1) with 100% oxygen; give adrenaline if heart rate remains < 60 despite 30 seconds of compressions. **Most newborn arrests are respiratory: ventilation is the priority.** Delay cord clamping by at least 30–60 seconds for vigorous infants.

Routine care at birth: skin-to-skin contact, early breastfeeding, vitamin K (phytomenadione 1 mg IM to prevent vitamin K deficiency bleeding), eye care per policy, identification, hepatitis B vaccine (± HBIG), BCG where applicable, hip and cardiac screening.

### 1.2 Thermoregulation and glucose

Newborns have high surface-area-to-weight ratio and little brown fat reserve; hypothermia (< 36.5 °C axillary) causes hypoglycaemia, apnoea, acidosis and poor feeding. Keep skin-to-skin with the mother, hats and blankets, avoid draughts, and check temperature in any unwell baby. Kangaroo mother care reduces mortality in low-birthweight infants.

## 2. The newborn examination

Perform within 72 hours of birth (full examination) and at 6–8 weeks. Wash hands, warm hands and room, examine the baby with the mother present, and ask about maternal history, antenatal scans, risk factors for infection, family history of congenital disease or deafness, feeding, passage of urine and meconium.

### 2.1 General and vital signs

- **Appearance**: colour (pink, pale, plethoric, jaundice at < 24 h is pathological, cyanosis), posture (flexion in term infants), activity, cry (high-pitched cry suggests neurological disease), skin (milia, erythema toxicum, Mongolian blue spots, naevus simplex, vernix, lanugo, petechiae/purpura).
- **Vital signs**: heart rate 100–160/min (asleep 90–120), respiratory rate 40–60/min (periodic breathing normal; > 60 abnormal), temperature 36.5–37.5 °C, SpO₂ ≥ 95% in room air after the first hours, capillary refill ≤ 3 s, blood glucose ≥ 2.6 mmol/L (47 mg/dL) after the first hours.
- **Growth**: weight, length, head circumference (plot on centile charts); expect up to 7–10% weight loss in the first 3–5 days, back to birthweight by 10–14 days.

### 2.2 Head-to-toe examination

| System | What to look for |
|---|---|
| **Head** | Fontanelles (anterior open, flat, soft; bulging suggests raised pressure; sunken suggests dehydration), sutures (overriding normal after delivery), caput succedaneum (crosses sutures, resolves in days), cephalohaematoma (does not cross sutures, appears at 12–24 h, resolves over weeks, may cause jaundice), subgaleal haemorrhage (boggy, expanding, can cause shock), microcephaly/macrocephaly, dysmorphic features |
| **Face and eyes** | Symmetry (facial nerve palsy), red reflex bilaterally (absent or white = cataract, retinoblastoma, glaucoma: urgent), epicanthic folds, coloboma, sticky eyes, subconjunctival haemorrhage (benign) |
| **Mouth and palate** | Palpate and inspect for cleft palate (including submucous), tongue-tie, natal teeth, Epstein pearls, thrush |
| **Ears and neck** | Low-set ears, pits/tags, torticollis, webbing, hearing screening |
| **Chest and breathing** | Respiratory rate, recession, grunting, nasal flaring, symmetrical expansion, breath sounds, clavicular fracture (crepitus) |
| **Heart** | Heart rate and rhythm, precordial activity, **four-limb pulses (femoral pulses essential)**, murmurs (a soft murmur is common in the first 48 h; any murmur with symptoms, abnormal saturation, or absent pulses needs echocardiography), pre- and post-ductal oxygen saturation (right hand vs foot) |
| **Abdomen** | Soft, liver edge (1–2 cm), spleen not palpable, kidneys may be palpable, masses, umbilicus (two arteries one vein; discharge, erythema, granuloma, hernia), abdominal wall defects (gastroschisis, exomphalos) |
| **Genitalia and anus** | Male: urethral meatus (hypospadias, epispadias), testes descended, hydrocele, ambiguous genitalia (emergency: CAH). Female: labia, clitoris, vaginal skin tag, white discharge/withdrawal bleed (normal). Anus patent and in normal position |
| **Spine and sacrum** | Midline sacral pits, tufts of hair, swelling, naevi (spina bifida occulta, tethered cord); pit above the natal cleft with a visible base is usually benign, but a deep or high pit needs ultrasound |
| **Hips** | Barlow (dislocatable) and Ortolani (reducible) tests, asymmetrical skin folds, limited abduction; ultrasound at 4–6 weeks if risk factors (breech, family history, foot deformities) or abnormal findings |
| **Limbs and extremities** | Number of digits (polydactyly, syndactyly), palmar creases, talipes equinovarus (structural) versus positional, limb length, movement symmetry (Erb palsy: waiter's tip) |
| **Neurological** | Tone (floppy or stiff), posture, spontaneous movement, primitive reflexes (Moro, rooting, sucking, grasp, stepping), alertness, seizures (subtle: lip smacking, bicycling, apnoea, eye deviation) |
| **Skin** | Jaundice, pallor, rash, birthmarks, petechiae, neonatal acne, harlequin colour change |

### 2.3 Red flags at routine examination

- **Absent red reflex**; **absent or weak femoral pulses** (coarctation); **cyanosis or SpO₂ < 95%**; **pathological murmur**; **dysmorphism or multiple anomalies** (genetic evaluation); **ambiguous genitalia** or hypospadias with undescended testes; **bilious vomiting**; **delayed meconium** (> 48 h; Hirschsprung disease, meconium ileus in cystic fibrosis); **no urine in 24 h** (obstructive uropathy, posterior urethral valves in boys); **hip instability**; **head circumference abnormality**; **abnormal tone**; **jaundice in the first 24 h**; **petechiae or bruising**.

## 3. The sick newborn: recognising danger signs

Newborns cannot localise illness; they show **non-specific signs** that must be taken seriously:

- Poor feeding or sudden refusal to feed, vomiting (especially bilious or projectile).
- Lethargy, irritability, floppiness, weak or high-pitched cry, seizures.
- Temperature instability: fever (≥ 38.0 °C) or hypothermia (< 36.0–36.5 °C).
- Respiratory distress: tachypnoea (> 60/min), grunting, nasal flaring, chest recession, apnoea (> 20 s or with bradycardia/desaturation), cyanosis.
- Colour: pallor, mottling, central cyanosis, grey colour, jaundice appearing within 24 h or persistent.
- Cardiovascular: tachycardia, delayed capillary refill, weak pulses, hypotension (late sign).
- Abdominal distension, absent bowel sounds.
- Reduced urine output (< 1 mL/kg/h), no wet nappy in 8–12 hours.
- Skin: pustules, spreading erythema, umbilical redness, petechial rash.
- Bulging fontanelle, neck stiffness (rare in neonates).

**WHO IMCI/Young infant danger signs**: not feeding well, convulsions, fast breathing (≥ 60/min), severe chest indrawing, fever (≥ 37.5 °C), low body temperature (< 35.5 °C), movement only on stimulation, no movement at all, central cyanosis, jaundice below the umbilicus or on palms/soles. In any of these: urgent referral and the first dose of antibiotics.

**Rule: a sick-looking infant is septic until proven otherwise.** Treat early, investigate in parallel.

## 4. Neonatal sepsis and infection

### 4.1 Early-onset and late-onset

- **Early-onset sepsis (EOS)**: within 72 hours (most within 24 h); vertical transmission; organisms: Group B streptococcus (GBS), *E. coli*, *Listeria*, *Haemophilus*, enterococci. Risk factors: maternal GBS colonisation or bacteriuria, preterm birth, prolonged rupture of membranes (> 18 h), maternal fever or chorioamnionitis, previous infected infant, maternal sepsis, inadequate intrapartum prophylaxis.
- **Late-onset sepsis (LOS)**: after 72 hours (up to 3 months); hospital- or community-acquired; organisms: coagulase-negative staphylococci (central lines), *S. aureus*, GBS, *E. coli*, Klebsiella, Candida, HSV.

### 4.2 Clinical assessment

Use serial observation: respiratory rate, heart rate, temperature, capillary refill, tone, feeding, glucose. Tools: Kaiser sepsis calculator, NICE risk-based pathway, local scores.

**Red flag signs** (treat immediately): clinical signs of sepsis, seizures, apnoea, need for respiratory support, shock, or maternal sepsis in labour. **Other risk factors** guide observation for 12–24 hours.

### 4.3 Investigation and treatment

1. Blood culture (before antibiotics; ≥ 1 mL), blood glucose, FBC with differential, CRP (serial, at 18–24 and 24–48 h; one normal value does not exclude sepsis; procalcitonin where available), blood gas and lactate.
2. **Lumbar puncture** when stable in suspected sepsis, or if cultures positive, or clinical signs suggest meningitis (especially late-onset). Do not delay antibiotics.
3. Urine culture (suprapubic aspirate or catheter sample; late onset), chest X-ray if respiratory signs, swabs (umbilical, skin), stool if diarrhoea, HSV PCR if skin vesicles, maternal seizures, hepatitis, or sepsis picture with negative cultures.
4. **Antibiotics immediately** when sepsis is suspected: EOS: IV benzylpenicillin (or ampicillin) plus gentamicin; LOS: cefotaxime or ceftriaxone (caution with jaundice and calcium infusions; avoid ceftriaxone in neonates with hyperbilirubinaemia) plus amoxicillin for Listeria coverage in infants < 1 month, vancomycin if line-related. Add aciclovir 20 mg/kg q8h if HSV is possible. Doses depend on gestational and postnatal age. Review at 36–48 h: stop if cultures negative and clinical picture is reassuring (unnecessary antibiotics have harms).
5. Supportive care: oxygen, fluids (10 mL/kg boluses, reassessing after each), glucose, temperature control, inotropes, ventilation as needed.
6. Duration: sepsis 7–10 days; meningitis 14–21 days depending on organism; consider cranial ultrasound and hearing assessment.

### 4.4 Specific infections

- **Congenital infections (TORCH)**: toxoplasmosis, rubella, CMV, HSV, syphilis, parvovirus, Zika. Features: SGA, microcephaly, hepatosplenomegaly, jaundice, petechiae/"blueberry muffin" rash, chorioretinitis, deafness, intracranial calcifications. Congenital CMV is the commonest congenital infection and a major cause of non-genetic sensorineural hearing loss: send urine or saliva PCR within 21 days of birth.
- **Neonatal HSV**: skin, eye, mouth (SEM), CNS, or disseminated; presentation at days 5–14 with fever, lethargy, seizures, vesicles (absent in 30%). High mortality; start aciclovir early.
- **Omphalitis**: umbilical erythema spreading to abdominal wall, purulent discharge; IV antibiotics, because of risk of necrotising fasciitis and portal vein thrombosis.
- **Neonatal conjunctivitis (ophthalmia neonatorum)**: day 1–2: chemical; day 2–5: gonococcal (severe purulent, emergency; systemic ceftriaxone); day 5–14: chlamydial (treat with oral macrolide). See chapter 55.
- **Tetanus neonatorum**: inability to suck, rigidity, spasms after non-sterile cord care; prevent via maternal immunisation and clean birth.
- **Congenital syphilis**: rhinitis ("snuffles"), rash on palms and soles, hepatosplenomegaly, long-bone osteochondritis; treat with IV penicillin.
- **Bacterial meningitis**: lethargy, poor feeding, irritability, bulging fontanelle (late), seizures, apnoea; neck stiffness uncommon.
- **Urinary tract infection** is common in febrile infants (7% of febrile infants under 3 months); associated with anatomical anomalies.

## 5. Neonatal jaundice

### 5.1 Physiology and classification

About 60% of term and 80% of preterm infants are clinically jaundiced in the first week. Unconjugated bilirubin rises because of high red-cell turnover, immature glucuronyl transferase, and enterohepatic recirculation. Visible jaundice appears when serum bilirubin is ≈ 85 µmol/L (5 mg/dL), spreading cephalocaudally (face → trunk → limbs). Visual estimation underestimates; use transcutaneous bilirubinometry (TcB) for screening and serum bilirubin (total and conjugated) to confirm.

| Timing | Type | Causes |
|---|---|---|
| **< 24 hours** | Always pathological | Haemolytic disease (Rh, ABO, G6PD deficiency, spherocytosis), infection, congenital infection |
| **24 hours–2 weeks** | Physiological (peak at day 3–5 in term, day 5–7 in preterm) or pathological | Physiological, breastfeeding jaundice (inadequate intake), breast milk jaundice (later), polycythaemia, cephalohaematoma/bruising, sepsis, haemolysis, hypothyroidism, Gilbert/Crigler–Najjar |
| **> 2 weeks (term), > 3 weeks (preterm)** | Prolonged jaundice | Breast milk jaundice, hypothyroidism, UTI, galactosaemia, **biliary atresia**, neonatal hepatitis, cholestasis, hypopituitarism, TPN |

### 5.2 Assessment

- **History**: gestational age, birth weight, family history of jaundice, G6PD deficiency, haemolytic disease, mother's blood group, previous sibling treated, feeding, stool and urine colour.
- **Examination**: degree and extent of jaundice, pallor, hepatosplenomegaly, bruising, cephalohaematoma, dehydration, signs of sepsis, hypotonia, poor feeding, lethargy, opisthotonos/high-pitched cry (bilirubin encephalopathy).
- **Investigations**: serum total bilirubin (TSB) and conjugated (direct) bilirubin; plot against **gestational age- and postnatal age-specific treatment threshold charts** (NICE, AAP hour-specific nomogram). Baby blood group and Coombs (direct antiglobulin) test, mother's group and antibodies, FBC and film (spherocytes), reticulocyte count, G6PD, TSH, urine culture, blood culture if unwell.
- **Neurotoxicity risk factors**: gestation < 38 weeks, haemolysis, sepsis, acidosis, hypoalbuminaemia, instability, G6PD deficiency.

### 5.3 Management

- **Phototherapy** (blue light 460–490 nm, irradiance ≥ 30 µW/cm²/nm, maximal skin exposure, eyes covered): convert unconjugated bilirubin to excretable isomers. Start at the gestation- and age-specific threshold; monitor TSB 4–6 hours after starting then 6–12-hourly; stop when TSB is 50 µmol/L (3 mg/dL) below the threshold; check rebound at 12–18 h in high-risk babies. Encourage frequent feeds; supplement if poor intake; do not stop breastfeeding.
- **Intensive phototherapy** and **IV immunoglobulin** (0.5–1 g/kg) in Rh/ABO isoimmune haemolysis.
- **Exchange transfusion** if TSB reaches the exchange threshold or signs of acute bilirubin encephalopathy (lethargy, hypotonia, poor suck, then hypertonia, arching, high-pitched cry, fever, seizures), preferably in a neonatal intensive care unit. Prepare early (crossmatched blood, central access).
- **Kernicterus**: chronic bilirubin encephalopathy (athetoid cerebral palsy, high-frequency hearing loss, upward gaze paralysis, dental enamel dysplasia); preventable.
- **Prolonged jaundice**: if > 14 days (term) or > 21 days (preterm): fractionate bilirubin. **If conjugated bilirubin > 25 µmol/L (or > 20% of total), act within 24–48 hours** (pale stools, dark urine, hepatomegaly): think **biliary atresia** (Kasai portoenterostomy before 60 days gives the best outcome), neonatal hepatitis, metabolic and endocrine disease, alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency, cystic fibrosis, galactosaemia (Escherichia coli sepsis, cataract, hypoglycaemia). Stool colour cards help detect pale stools. Check TSH, urine culture, G6PD.

## 6. Feeding problems, dehydration and failure to thrive

### 6.1 Breastfeeding assessment

Ask: frequency (8–12 feeds per 24 h), duration, latch (wide gape, chin touching breast, asymmetric areola, rhythmic swallowing), maternal pain, nipple trauma, milk supply, sleepy baby, pacifier or formula use, wet nappies (day 1: 1; day 2: 2; day 3: 3; day 4: 4; day 5 and after: ≥ 6 heavy), stool colour change (meconium → transitional → yellow by day 4–5). Weigh: > 10% loss at day 3–5 or failure to regain birthweight by 14 days needs assessment of feeding, medical review and lactation support.

**Breastfeeding failure causes**: ineffective latch, ankyloglossia, maternal factors (delayed lactogenesis in diabetes, obesity, retained placenta, Sheehan syndrome, insufficient glandular tissue, breast surgery), infant factors (prematurity, sleepiness, neurological disease, cleft palate, cardiac or respiratory disease, infection, jaundice), and medications.

### 6.2 Neonatal hypernatraemic dehydration

An important and preventable emergency in exclusively breastfed babies with inadequate intake: weight loss > 10–15%, jaundice, lethargy, irritability, dry mucous membranes, sunken fontanelle (late), reduced urine, high-pitched cry, seizures from sodium shifts. Serum sodium > 150 mmol/L, raised urea and creatinine, metabolic acidosis, hyperbilirubinaemia. **Correct slowly**: sodium should fall no faster than 10–12 mmol/L per 24 h (0.5 mmol/L/h) to avoid cerebral oedema; rehydrate over 48 hours with isotonic-to-hypotonic fluids, oral feeds if possible (expressed milk or formula).

### 6.3 Vomiting in infants

| Feature | Think of |
|---|---|
| **Bilious (green) vomiting** | **Surgical emergency until proven otherwise**: malrotation with volvulus, intestinal atresia, necrotising enterocolitis, Hirschsprung disease, meconium ileus. Do not wait: urgent surgical review, nasogastric decompression, IV access, antibiotics, upper GI contrast study or ultrasound |
| Projectile non-bilious vomiting, hungry after vomiting, weeks 2–8, visible peristalsis, palpable "olive" | **Pyloric stenosis** (male:female 4:1; first-born; hypochloraemic hypokalaemic metabolic alkalosis; ultrasound diagnosis: pyloric muscle thickness ≥ 3 mm, length ≥ 15–17 mm). Correct fluids and electrolytes first, then pyloromyotomy |
| Effortless regurgitation (posseting), thriving | Gastro-oesophageal reflux (physiological in 40–50% of infants): reassurance, feeding advice, thickened feeds |
| Vomiting with blood in stool, colicky pain, drawing up legs, "redcurrant jelly" stool, lethargy | **Intussusception** (peak 3 months–3 years) |
| Vomiting plus diarrhoea | Gastroenteritis (rotavirus), UTI, sepsis |
| Vomiting with hypoglycaemia, acidosis, lethargy, unusual odour | Inborn errors of metabolism (see section 9) |
| Vomiting with bulging fontanelle, irritability, poor feeding | Meningitis, raised intracranial pressure (hydrocephalus, subdural haematoma from abuse) |
| Cow's milk protein allergy | Vomiting, eczema, blood and mucus in stool, poor weight gain; elimination trial |
| Salt-wasting CAH | Vomiting, poor feeding, dehydration, hyponatraemia, hyperkalaemia, hypoglycaemia at 1–2 weeks; ambiguous genitalia in girls |

### 6.4 Growth faltering (failure to thrive)

Weight crossing two major centile lines downward or below the 2nd centile (or < 0.4th), or weight loss. Causes: **inadequate intake** (most common, inadequate calories, feeding difficulties, poverty, neglect), **inadequate absorption** (cystic fibrosis, coeliac disease, cow's milk protein allergy), **increased losses/requirements** (congenital heart disease, chronic infection, renal tubular acidosis, hypothyroidism, diabetes, malignancy), **neurological or genetic**. Assess: detailed feeding and psychosocial history, observe a feed, plot growth, full examination, then targeted tests (FBC, ferritin, U&E, bicarbonate, TSH, coeliac serology if on gluten, urine culture, sweat test if indicated, HIV in endemic areas). Refer for dietetic and multidisciplinary support; consider safeguarding. In low-resource settings, use MUAC and WHO weight-for-height z-scores to classify severe acute malnutrition (see chapter 59).

## 7. Respiratory distress, cyanosis and congenital heart disease

### 7.1 Respiratory distress in the newborn

Signs: tachypnoea (> 60/min), grunting, nasal flaring, intercostal and subcostal recession, see-saw breathing, cyanosis, apnoea. Use the **Silverman–Anderson** or **Downes** score to grade.

| Cause | Clues |
|---|---|
| **Transient tachypnoea of the newborn (TTN)** | Term/late preterm, caesarean birth, onset within hours, resolves 24–72 h; X-ray: fluid in fissure, hyperinflation, perihilar streaking |
| **Respiratory distress syndrome (RDS)** | Preterm, surfactant deficiency; worsening over 24–48 h; "ground-glass" X-ray with air bronchograms; antenatal steroids prevent; treatment: CPAP, surfactant |
| **Meconium aspiration syndrome** | Post-term, meconium-stained liquor; patchy infiltrates, hyperinflation, pneumothorax; pulmonary hypertension |
| **Pneumonia/sepsis** | Risk factors for infection; X-ray patchy; treat with antibiotics |
| **Pneumothorax** | Sudden deterioration, asymmetric chest, shifted heart sounds; transillumination; X-ray |
| **Congenital diaphragmatic hernia** | Scaphoid abdomen, bowel sounds in chest, heart displaced; do not give bag-mask ventilation; insert nasogastric tube; intubate |
| **Persistent pulmonary hypertension of the newborn (PPHN)** | Severe hypoxia unresponsive to oxygen, pre/post-ductal saturation difference; inhaled nitric oxide |
| **Choanal atresia** | Cyanosis when quiet, relieved by crying; cannot pass nasal catheter |
| **Congenital heart disease** | See below |
| **Tracheo-oesophageal fistula** | Excessive frothy secretions, choking with feeds, cannot pass NG tube (coiled in pouch) |
| **Anaemia, hypoglycaemia, hypothermia, metabolic acidosis, sepsis** | Tachypnoea may be "non-respiratory" |

Initial management: warmth, oxygen to saturations 90–95% (preterm; lower targets at 90–94%), CPAP or ventilation, glucose, cultures and antibiotics, X-ray, blood gas.

### 7.2 Congenital heart disease (CHD)

CHD affects about 8 per 1,000 live births. Critical CHD requires intervention in the first month: these babies may look well at discharge and collapse when the ductus arteriosus closes (days 2–14).

- **Cyanotic**: transposition of the great arteries (cyanosis in the first day, little distress, no murmur), tetralogy of Fallot (cyanotic "tet spells" later), pulmonary atresia, tricuspid atresia, total anomalous pulmonary venous return (obstructed form: severe cyanosis and pulmonary oedema).
- **Duct-dependent systemic circulation**: coarctation of the aorta, interrupted aortic arch, critical aortic stenosis, hypoplastic left heart: present with **shock, acidosis, poor feeding, tachypnoea, weak or absent femoral pulses** at days 2–14 as the duct closes.
- **Left-to-right shunts** (VSD, PDA, AVSD): heart failure at 4–8 weeks as pulmonary vascular resistance falls: tachypnoea, sweating on feeding, poor weight gain, hepatomegaly, murmur.

*Screening*: pulse oximetry at 24–48 hours (right hand and either foot): pass if SpO₂ ≥ 95% in both and difference ≤ 3%; fail if < 90% in either; repeat if 90–94% or difference > 3%. Pulse oximetry misses coarctation in up to 30–40%, so check femoral pulses.

*Hyperoxia test*: PaO₂ < 150 mmHg (20 kPa) on 100% oxygen suggests cyanotic CHD.

*Management of suspected duct-dependent lesion*: **start prostaglandin E1 (alprostadil) infusion** (0.01–0.05 µg/kg/min; watch for apnoea; intubate if needed) and urgent echocardiography and cardiology; avoid high-flow oxygen in suspected left-sided obstructive lesions (promotes ductal closure and pulmonary overcirculation); treat acidosis; support circulation.

## 8. Neonatal hypoglycaemia, seizures and hypotonia

### 8.1 Hypoglycaemia

Risk factors: preterm, SGA, infants of diabetic mothers, LGA, perinatal stress/hypoxia, hypothermia, sepsis, polycythaemia, inborn errors, hyperinsulinism, adrenal or pituitary insufficiency. Signs: jitteriness, tremor, lethargy, hypotonia, poor feeding, apnoea, seizures, cyanosis, hypothermia, high-pitched cry; or none. Operational threshold: plasma glucose < 2.6 mmol/L (47 mg/dL) in the first 48 hours (policies vary; < 2.0–2.8). Check glucose in at-risk infants at 1–2 hours of life, then pre-feed.

*Management*: feed immediately (breast, expressed milk, formula); if persistent or symptomatic: IV 10% dextrose 2 mL/kg bolus then infusion 6–8 mg/kg/min; buccal glucose gel 40% (0.5 mL/kg) for asymptomatic mild hypoglycaemia. For persistent hypoglycaemia > 7 days or high glucose requirements (> 8–10 mg/kg/min): investigate hyperinsulinism, hypopituitarism, cortisol deficiency, inborn errors (critical sample: glucose, insulin, cortisol, GH, ketones, lactate, ammonia, free fatty acids, acylcarnitines, urine organic acids).

### 8.2 Neonatal seizures

Subtle (apnoea, eye deviation, lip-smacking, cycling), clonic, tonic, myoclonic. Causes: **hypoxic–ischaemic encephalopathy (HIE)** (first 24 h; therapeutic hypothermia within 6 hours for moderate-to-severe HIE ≥ 36 weeks), **stroke**, intracranial haemorrhage, **infection** (meningitis, HSV, TORCH), **metabolic** (hypoglycaemia, hypocalcaemia, hypomagnesaemia, hyponatraemia, inborn errors, pyridoxine dependency), **drug withdrawal**, malformations, genetic epilepsies (KCNQ2). Work-up: glucose, electrolytes with calcium, magnesium, gas, ammonia, lactate, cultures, LP, cranial ultrasound/MRI, EEG (amplitude-integrated EEG), metabolic screen; treat: correct glucose/calcium, then phenobarbital (20 mg/kg load) or levetiracetam as first-line; trial of pyridoxine if refractory.

### 8.3 The floppy infant

Differentiate **central** (encephalopathy, HIE, chromosomal e.g., Down, Prader–Willi; metabolic; hypotonia with preserved reflexes and weak antigravity strength) from **peripheral** (spinal muscular atrophy: tongue fasciculation, absent reflexes, bell-shaped chest; congenital myopathies; myotonic dystrophy; myasthenia; neuropathies; botulism: constipation, poor suck, ptosis, descending weakness after honey or dust exposure). Check for sepsis, hypothyroidism, hypoglycaemia, electrolyte disorders first. Investigate with CK, TSH, metabolic screen, genetic testing (SMN1 deletion, methylation studies), MRI, EMG as indicated. Early diagnosis of SMA allows gene therapy/nusinersen/risdiplam before symptoms.

## 9. The febrile infant and inborn errors of metabolism

### 9.1 Fever in infants under 3 months

Fever ≥ 38.0 °C (rectal) in a young infant carries a 10–25% risk of serious bacterial infection (UTI most common; bacteraemia; meningitis 0.5–1%) plus viral infections (HSV, enterovirus, parechovirus, RSV, influenza). Neonates (< 28 days) are at highest risk; clinical examination alone is unreliable.

*Practical approach* (follow local pathway; examples: NICE traffic-light, Step-by-Step, PECARN, Rochester):

- **< 28 days**: full sepsis work-up (blood, urine, CSF cultures, inflammatory markers), IV antibiotics, admit, consider aciclovir.
- **29–60 days**: risk-stratify by clinical appearance, urinalysis, CRP, procalcitonin, ANC; well-appearing infants with low-risk markers may be observed or managed with outpatient antibiotics according to protocol; lumbar puncture if markers are abnormal or high risk.
- **61–90 days**: assess with the same markers; consider RSV/influenza testing.
- Check for hypothermia as an equally concerning sign.
- Do not rely on antipyretic response. Do not give antibiotics without cultures unless unstable.
- Parents must be told warning signs: poor feeding, floppiness, drowsiness, rash, fast breathing, colour change, reduced wet nappies.

### 9.2 Inborn errors of metabolism (IEM)

Suspect in a previously well term infant who becomes ill after a latent period of hours to days after starting feeds with: poor feeding, vomiting, lethargy, hypotonia, seizures, respiratory distress (tachypnoea from acidosis or hyperammonaemia), apnoea, hypothermia, hepatomegaly, unusual odour, hypoglycaemia, jaundice, sepsis-like picture, or sibling death. IEM mimics sepsis; treat sepsis and test for IEM simultaneously.

*First-line tests*: glucose, gas (metabolic acidosis, anion gap), lactate, **ammonia**, ketones in urine (ketosis in a neonate is abnormal), urea, electrolytes, LFTs, FBC, urine reducing substances, plasma amino acids, urine organic acids, acylcarnitine profile (save blood, urine and plasma before treatment).

*Initial management*: stop protein intake, give IV glucose (10% dextrose with insulin if needed) to reverse catabolism, treat hyperammonaemia (> 150–200 µmol/L: sodium benzoate/phenylbutyrate, arginine, haemodialysis if > 400), correct acidosis, avoid fasting, discuss with a metabolic specialist urgently.

Newborn bloodspot screening (heel prick at 48–72 h) tests for PKU, congenital hypothyroidism, CAH, MCADD, cystic fibrosis, haemoglobinopathies, SMA, SCID, and others per national programme; **a normal screening result does not exclude a symptomatic IEM** (repeat if baby unwell).

## 10. Surgical emergencies and congenital anomalies

| Condition | Clue | Action |
|---|---|---|
| Oesophageal atresia ± TOF | Polyhydramnios, excessive secretions, choking with first feed, NG tube cannot pass | Suction upper pouch, nil by mouth, surgical referral, VACTERL screen |

## 11. Non-accidental injury, safe sleep and the first year

- **Abuse in infants** is often missed. Concerning features: bruising in a non-mobile baby ("those who don't cruise rarely bruise"), bruises on the ears/neck/torso, torn frenulum, retinal haemorrhages, rib fractures (posterior), metaphyseal corner fractures, subdural haematoma with encephalopathy ("abusive head trauma"/shaken baby), inconsistent or changing history, delay in seeking care. Action: examine fully, document with body maps and photographs per policy, perform skeletal survey (age < 2 years), neuroimaging, ophthalmology, coagulation and bone profile to exclude medical mimics, and **involve safeguarding teams and senior paediatricians**. Do not discharge a baby with suspicious injuries without a safeguarding plan.
- **Sudden unexpected death in infancy (SUDI/SIDS)**: peak 2–4 months. Risk factors: prone or side sleeping, soft bedding, bed-sharing (especially with smoking, alcohol, sofas), maternal smoking, prematurity, overheating, absence of breastfeeding, no pacifier. **Safe sleep**: on the back, in own cot in parents' room for first 6 months, firm flat mattress, nothing else in the cot, smoke-free, no overheating. Differential for sudden death includes infection, IEM, cardiac channelopathies, abuse, and accidental suffocation: full death investigation protocol.
- **Immunisations**: follow the national schedule (e.g., DTaP-IPV-Hib-HepB, pneumococcal, rotavirus, MenB/MenACWY, MMR); fever after vaccination is common and usually benign.
- **Development**: see chapter 59 (milestones); at the 6–8-week check, examine hips, eyes (red reflex), heart, testes, fontanelle, tone, social smile, feeding, weight; ask about maternal mental health (screen with EPDS) and domestic abuse.
- **Common benign findings**: milia, erythema toxicum, neonatal acne, Mongolian spots, salmon patch, stork bite, hydrocele, breast enlargement, vaginal discharge/bleeding, physiological jaundice, "snuffles", sneezing, hiccups, startles, benign sleep myoclonus, positional talipes and plagiocephaly.
- **Colic**: crying > 3 h/day, > 3 days/week for > 3 weeks in an otherwise healthy, thriving infant; peaks at 6 weeks; resolves by 4–5 months. Exclude organic causes (infection, hair tourniquet, corneal abrasion, hernia, intussusception, reflux, cow's milk allergy); reassure, support parents; avoid shaking the baby.

## Worked cases

### Case 1: A jaundiced baby on day 1

**Presentation.** A term baby (39 weeks, 3.2 kg) born to a blood group O RhD-positive mother is noted to be yellow at 16 hours old. Feeding well; wet nappies adequate; mother had an uneventful labour. Baby is alert, no hepatosplenomegaly, mild pallor. TcB is 190 µmol/L (11 mg/dL); serum bilirubin 215 µmol/L (12.6 mg/dL).

**Reasoning.** Jaundice within 24 hours is pathological until proven otherwise. Likely ABO incompatibility (mother O, baby A or B), also consider G6PD deficiency, other haemolytic disease, sepsis. Rising rapidly (> 8.5 µmol/L/h). The risk of neurotoxicity rises with haemolysis.

**Action.** Send urgent TSB, FBC, film (spherocytes), reticulocytes, DAT, baby's blood group, G6PD, glucose; start phototherapy at once, ideally intensive, plot on gestation- and hour-specific nomogram; repeat bilirubin in 4–6 hours; keep feeding or supplement; IVIG if bilirubin approaches exchange threshold (0.5–1 g/kg); prepare for exchange if near threshold. DAT positive with Hb 138 g/L confirms ABO disease. Bilirubin plateaus at 270 µmol/L after 24 h and falls; discharge with follow-up and hearing/neurodevelopmental review. Late anaemia may occur at 2–6 weeks: check Hb.

**Lessons.** Jaundice < 24 hours: treat as haemolysis. Use the correct chart for age in hours.

### Case 2: "He just won't feed"

**Presentation.** A 9-day-old term boy, breastfed, is brought to the emergency department because he is feeding poorly and sleepy for 12 hours. Temperature 38.4 °C, HR 178, RR 66, capillary refill 4 s, mottled, floppy; weight is 8% below birthweight.

**Reasoning.** Late-onset neonatal sepsis/meningitis (including HSV) until proven otherwise; differential: UTI, pneumonia, IEM, duct-dependent CHD (absent femoral pulses?), dehydration, adrenal crisis. Poor perfusion = septic shock.

**Action.** ABCDE, high-flow oxygen, IV/IO access, glucose 4.6 mmol/L, cultures (blood, urine by catheter), gas (lactate 4.5), CRP, FBC, electrolytes, ammonia. **Do not delay antibiotics for LP:** give IV cefotaxime plus amoxicillin (or per local protocol), add aciclovir; fluid bolus 10 mL/kg 0.9% saline, repeat as needed; check femoral pulses (present). LP when stable: 120 white cells/µL with Gram-positive cocci; culture grows GBS (late-onset meningitis). Continue IV penicillin/ceftriaxone for 14–21 days, cranial ultrasound, hearing test, neurodevelopmental follow-up. Screen mother and siblings per local policy.

**Lessons.** A baby who "won't feed" and is sleepy has sepsis until proven otherwise. Treat first, investigate in parallel.

### Case 3: Collapse at day 5

**Presentation.** A term infant discharged at 36 hours is brought to hospital at day 5 grey, tachypnoeic (RR 80), with poor feeding and decreased activity. HR 180, SpO₂ 88% in air, no murmur. Right arm BP 90/50, leg BP 55/30; femoral pulses very weak; liver 3 cm below costal margin. Lactate 9, pH 7.12.

**Reasoning.** Duct-dependent systemic circulation (critical coarctation/interrupted arch/HLHS) with ductal closure, now cardiogenic shock. Sepsis can look similar; consider both.

**Action.** Call neonatal and cardiology teams; start **prostaglandin E1 infusion** (0.05 µg/kg/min initially) and consider intubation for anticipated apnoea; treat acidosis; give antibiotics; avoid excess oxygen; cautious fluids; echo confirms critical coarctation; transfer to cardiac centre for surgery. Femoral pulses should have been checked on routine discharge examination and pre/post-ductal saturations obtained.

**Lessons.** A normal newborn check does not exclude duct-dependent CHD. Check femoral pulses and saturations in every collapsed infant, and start prostaglandin early.

### Case 4: Green vomit

**Presentation.** A 10-day-old infant with 6 hours of bilious vomiting. Mildly distended abdomen, tender, pink and alert. Passed stool yesterday.

**Reasoning.** Bilious vomiting in a neonate is malrotation with midgut volvulus until proven otherwise, even if the baby looks well; delay risks bowel necrosis and short bowel syndrome.

**Action.** Nil by mouth, large-bore NG tube on free drainage, IV access, bloods (gas, lactate, FBC, U&E, group and save), IV fluids, antibiotics, immediate paediatric surgical consult, **upper GI contrast study** (corkscrew duodenum, malpositioned duodenojejunal flexure) or ultrasound (whirlpool sign). Emergency laparotomy (Ladd procedure). If shocked, resuscitate and proceed straight to theatre.

**Lessons.** Never attribute bilious vomiting to reflux. A well-looking baby may have a threatened gut.

### Case 5: Jaundice at 3 weeks

**Presentation.** A 23-day-old breastfed girl, born at term, is still jaundiced. Thriving; weight gain adequate; but stools are pale yellow-white and urine dark. Liver edge 3 cm palpable, firm.

**Reasoning.** Prolonged jaundice with pale stools and dark urine implies conjugated hyperbilirubinaemia; biliary atresia must be excluded urgently. Breast milk jaundice is unconjugated and has normal stools.

**Action.** Split bilirubin: total 190, conjugated 140 µmol/L; INR prolonged → vitamin K; ultrasound (absent or small gallbladder, triangular cord sign); infection screen, TSH, alpha-1 antitrypsin phenotype, metabolic screen, galactosaemia; refer same day to a paediatric hepatology centre; HIDA scan/liver biopsy/intraoperative cholangiogram; Kasai portoenterostomy ideally before 60 days; fat-soluble vitamin supplementation. Show parents the stool colour chart.

**Lessons.** Every jaundiced baby > 14 days needs fractionated bilirubin. Pale stools = urgent hepatology referral.

### Case 6: Fever at 6 weeks

**Presentation.** A 6-week-old, previously well, febrile at 38.3 °C rectal for 4 hours; feeding slightly less, alert, smiling, no focus. HR 160, RR 48, CRT 2 s. Urine dipstick: leukocytes 2+, nitrites negative. CRP 52 mg/L, WCC 17, procalcitonin 1.6 ng/mL.

**Reasoning.** Febrile infant 29–60 days, with abnormal inflammatory markers and pyuria: high risk of serious bacterial infection (UTI ± bacteraemia). Cannot be sent home without cultures.

**Action.** Catheter urine for microscopy and culture, blood culture, LP (not mandatory in all pathways but recommended for abnormal markers; at least consider), start IV ceftriaxone/cefotaxime, admit; urine culture grows *E. coli*; CSF clear; switch to oral antibiotics per guideline after clinical improvement (total 7–14 days); renal ultrasound at 4–6 weeks (and VCUG only if atypical or recurrent UTI). Parents informed of warning signs.

**Lessons.** Fever in a young infant must be investigated according to a pathway; do not rely on a happy appearance.

## Common pitfalls

1. **Dismissing non-specific signs** (poor feeding, sleepiness, temperature instability) in infants.
2. **Missing jaundice** because of visual estimation, dark skin, or poor lighting; not using hour-specific nomograms.
3. **Treating jaundice < 24 h as physiological.**
4. **Failing to check femoral pulses and pre/post-ductal saturations**; relying on absence of murmur.
5. **Attributing bilious vomiting to reflux or "colic".**
6. **Delaying antibiotics for lumbar puncture** or waiting for CRP.
7. **Stopping breastfeeding** for jaundice without evidence; also failing to recognise inadequate intake and hypernatraemic dehydration.
8. **Rapid correction of hypernatraemia** (cerebral oedema and seizures).
9. **Giving high-flow oxygen** to a cyanotic infant with suspected duct-dependent lesion.
10. **Bag-mask ventilation in diaphragmatic hernia.**
11. **Missing hip dysplasia** or red reflex abnormalities at routine checks.
12. **Overlooking metabolic disease** because of a normal newborn screen.
13. **Failure to consider non-accidental injury** in unexplained bruises, fractures or encephalopathy in infants.
14. **Giving antipyretics or oral antibiotics to febrile infants** without investigation, masking serious illness.
15. **Ignoring maternal mental health and safe sleep counselling** at routine visits.

## Key points

- A sick newborn shows non-specific signs; presume sepsis and treat early; use risk factors and serial observation.
- Jaundice in the first 24 hours is pathological; use hour-specific charts; fractionate bilirubin if prolonged beyond 14 days; pale stools mean biliary atresia until proven otherwise.
- Always check femoral pulses and pulse oximetry; collapse at day 2–14 means duct-dependent heart disease or sepsis; start prostaglandin.
- Bilious vomiting is a surgical emergency.
- Establish feeding and growth in the first weeks; recognise hypernatraemic dehydration.
- Fever in an infant < 3 months needs a structured work-up; neonates need full sepsis evaluation and IV antibiotics.
- Suspect inborn errors in an infant who is ill after a symptom-free interval; test ammonia, lactate, glucose, ketones and gas before treating.
- Safe sleep and safeguarding are part of every infant assessment.

## Self-assessment

**Questions**

1. List five danger signs in a young infant that require urgent referral according to WHO IMCI.
2. A term baby is yellow at 18 hours. What are the likely causes and what do you do?
3. Describe the approach to prolonged jaundice at 3 weeks. Which result needs action within 24–48 hours?
4. A 4-day-old infant is lethargic, with weak femoral pulses and SpO₂ 90% in air. What are the diagnosis and the first drug?
5. What is the safe rate of correction of hypernatraemia, and why?
6. Outline the work-up of a febrile 20-day-old infant.
7. Name three conditions that cause bilious vomiting in neonates, and the investigation of choice for malrotation.
8. When should metabolic disease be suspected in a newborn, and which samples must be collected before treatment?
9. What are the screening criteria for critical congenital heart disease by pulse oximetry?
10. List four features that should raise suspicion of non-accidental injury in an infant.

**Answers**

1. Any five of: not feeding well; convulsions; fast breathing (≥ 60/min); severe chest indrawing; fever (≥ 37.5 °C); hypothermia (< 35.5 °C); movement only when stimulated or no movement; central cyanosis; jaundice below the umbilicus or on palms/soles (also umbilical redness extending to skin, pus from eyes).
2. Haemolysis (ABO/Rh/G6PD/spherocytosis), sepsis, congenital infection, bruising. Urgent total/direct serum bilirubin, FBC, film, reticulocytes, DAT, blood groups, G6PD, glucose, infection screen; start phototherapy and plot on an hour-specific chart; consider IVIG or exchange if near threshold.
3. Check for risk (cholestasis, hypothyroidism, UTI), examine stool and urine colour, fractionate bilirubin. If conjugated bilirubin > 25 µmol/L (or > 20% total), act within 24–48 h: refer to hepatology; exclude biliary atresia (ultrasound, HIDA, biopsy), infection, metabolic disease; give vitamin K.
4. Duct-dependent systemic circulation, e.g., critical coarctation, interrupted arch, HLHS, in cardiogenic shock. First drug: prostaglandin E1 (alprostadil) infusion; also antibiotics (rule out sepsis), correct acidosis, avoid excess oxygen, and arrange echo and cardiac surgery.
5. No faster than about 10–12 mmol/L per 24 hours (≈ 0.5 mmol/L/h). Faster correction moves water into brain cells and risks cerebral oedema and seizures.
6. Admit for full sepsis work-up: examination, vital signs, blood culture, FBC, CRP/procalcitonin, urine (catheter) microscopy and culture, lumbar puncture, glucose, gas, ± chest X-ray and viral PCR; IV antibiotics (ampicillin/benzylpenicillin + gentamicin or cefotaxime) plus aciclovir if HSV risk; reassess after 48 h.
7. Malrotation with volvulus, intestinal atresia (duodenal/jejunal), necrotising enterocolitis, Hirschsprung disease, meconium ileus. Upper GI contrast study (or ultrasound for whirlpool sign) for malrotation; urgent surgery if volvulus.
8. A previously well infant with deterioration after feeds start, with vomiting, lethargy, hypotonia, seizures, hyperventilation, hypothermia, unusual odour, hypoglycaemia, ketonuria, high anion gap acidosis, hyperammonaemia, or family history of neonatal death. Collect before treatment: blood (glucose, gas, lactate, ammonia, amino acids, acylcarnitines, save plasma), urine (ketones, organic acids, reducing substances), and consider skin biopsy/DNA.
9. Measure preductal (right hand) and postductal (foot) saturations after 24 h: pass if both ≥ 95% and difference ≤ 3%; fail if either < 90% or persistent 90–94% / difference > 3% on repeat; fail requires echo.
10. Bruising in a non-mobile infant; bruises to ears, neck, torso, buttocks; injuries inconsistent with history or developmental stage; delay in presentation; torn frenulum; multiple or posterior rib fractures; metaphyseal fractures; subdural haemorrhage with retinal haemorrhages; unexplained encephalopathy or apnoea; pattern injuries (any four).
