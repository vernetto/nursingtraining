## Chapter 27. Gastrointestinal Bleeding and Jaundice

### Learning objectives

After studying this chapter you should be able to:

1. Distinguish upper, lower and occult gastrointestinal bleeding by presentation, and identify the haemodynamic signs that determine urgency.
2. Resuscitate a patient with acute GI bleeding, apply the Glasgow-Blatchford score, and decide who needs urgent endoscopy.
3. List the common causes of upper and lower GI bleeding and recognise the special features of variceal bleeding.
4. Use transfusion thresholds, reversal of anticoagulation and early endoscopy appropriately.
5. Explain bilirubin metabolism and classify jaundice into pre-hepatic, hepatocellular and cholestatic types.
6. Interpret the pattern of liver tests, and take a history and perform an examination that narrows the cause of jaundice.
7. Recognise the emergencies in jaundice: acute liver failure, cholangitis, and haemolytic crisis, and plan investigation stepwise.

# Part A: Gastrointestinal Bleeding

### 27.1 Terminology

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Haematemesis** | Vomiting of blood: bright red (active bleeding) or **coffee-ground** (blood altered by gastric acid) |
| **Melaena** | Black, tarry, sticky, foul-smelling stool; blood digested during transit (usually from the upper GI tract, occasionally the small bowel or right colon); requires roughly 50–100 mL of blood |
| **Haematochezia** | Passage of fresh red or maroon blood per rectum; usually lower GI source, but brisk upper GI bleeding (often with haemodynamic compromise) can cause it |
| **Occult bleeding** | Blood not visible, detected by faecal occult blood test or iron deficiency anaemia |
| **Obscure bleeding** | Bleeding of unknown source after upper and lower endoscopy (usually small bowel) |

**Anatomical classification:**

- **Upper GI bleeding:** proximal to the ligament of Treitz (oesophagus, stomach, duodenum).
- **Middle (small bowel):** between the ligament of Treitz and the ileocaecal valve.
- **Lower GI bleeding:** colon, rectum and anus.

---

### 27.2 Initial assessment: how bad is it?

The first task is to **assess haemodynamic status and begin resuscitation**, not to find the source.

#### Estimating blood loss and shock

| Class | Blood loss | Heart rate | Blood pressure | Other |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| I | <15% (<750 mL) | Normal | Normal | Mild anxiety |
| II | 15–30% | >100 | Normal (narrow pulse pressure) | Anxious, delayed refill |
| III | 30–40% | >120 | Decreased | Confused, cool, oliguria |
| IV | >40% | >140 | Markedly decreased | Lethargic, collapsed |

**Shock index** (heart rate ÷ systolic BP) >0.9–1.0 is a marker of significant volume depletion. Older patients and those on **beta-blockers** may not mount a tachycardia; **hypotension may be a late sign.** Orthostatic changes, cold peripheries, delayed capillary refill, oliguria, altered mental state and **lactate** indicate hypoperfusion.

**Haemoglobin** may be normal in the first hours because plasma volume has not yet equilibrated; do not be reassured by a normal initial value. A low MCV suggests chronic blood loss.

#### Key early questions

- Amount and colour of blood; duration; number of episodes.
- Associated symptoms: dizziness, syncope, abdominal pain, chest pain (ischaemia triggered by anaemia), vomiting before haematemesis (Mallory–Weiss), weight loss, dysphagia, change in bowel habit.
- **Drugs:** NSAIDs, aspirin, other antiplatelets, **anticoagulants** (warfarin, DOACs, heparins), steroids, SSRIs, bisphosphonates, iron (black stool), bismuth (black stool).
- **Alcohol, liver disease, varices, previous GI bleed or ulcer, *H. pylori*, prior GI surgery** (including aortic graft surgery: **aortoenteric fistula**).
- **Comorbidities:** cardiovascular disease, renal failure, coagulopathy, cancer, recent endoscopy or polypectomy, radiation therapy.

#### Examination

- **Vital signs,** postural BP, capillary refill.
- **Signs of chronic liver disease:** jaundice, spider naevi, palmar erythema, ascites, splenomegaly, caput medusae, encephalopathy.
- **Pallor, signs of chronic anaemia.**
- **Abdomen:** tenderness, masses, hepatosplenomegaly.
- **Digital rectal examination** (colour of stool, melaena, masses, haemorrhoids): do it even when the patient has haematemesis.
- **Skin and mucosa:** telangiectasia (Osler–Weber–Rendu), pigmentation (Peutz–Jeghers), bruising, purpura.

---

### 27.3 Resuscitation and initial management

1. **ABC.** Secure the airway in patients with massive haematemesis or reduced consciousness (consider early intubation before endoscopy). Give **oxygen**.
2. **Two large-bore IV cannulae** (14–16G). **Send bloods** (see below).
3. **Fluids:** resuscitate with **isotonic crystalloid** initially to restore perfusion; avoid over-resuscitation (raising portal pressure in varices and increasing rebleeding). Early blood products in massive bleeding (major haemorrhage protocol).
4. **Blood transfusion:** a **restrictive strategy** is recommended for most patients: transfuse when Hb <70 g/L, with a target Hb of 70–90 g/L. In patients with cardiovascular disease, consider a higher threshold (about 80 g/L) and target (e.g. 100 g/L). Over-transfusion in variceal bleeding raises portal pressure and rebleeding. Transfuse immediately regardless of Hb in exsanguinating haemorrhage.
5. **Correct coagulopathy:**
   - **Warfarin:** prothrombin complex concentrate (PCC) plus IV vitamin K 5–10 mg for life-threatening bleeding (avoid FFP alone as a first choice).
   - **DOAC-related bleeding:** supportive; idarucizumab (dabigatran), andexanet alfa or PCC (factor Xa inhibitors) in life-threatening bleeds, per local protocol.
   - **Platelets** <50 × 10⁹/L with active bleeding; consider platelet transfusion in antiplatelet-treated patients with life-threatening bleeding (limited evidence).
   - **Cirrhotic coagulopathy:** do not correct INR with FFP routinely; it does not reflect bleeding risk.
6. **Tranexamic acid** is **not** recommended for routine GI bleeding (the HALT-IT trial showed no reduction in death from bleeding and an increased risk of venous thromboembolism).
7. **Proton pump inhibitor (PPI):** IV PPI before endoscopy is commonly started in suspected upper GI bleeding (reduces high-risk stigmata but not mortality); high-dose IV PPI **after** endoscopic therapy for high-risk ulcers.
8. **Suspected variceal bleeding:** add vasoactive drug (terlipressin, somatostatin or octreotide) and prophylactic antibiotics before endoscopy (see 27.5).
9. **Hold** anticoagulants, antiplatelet drugs (decision about aspirin for secondary prevention should be made early, usually resuming within days after haemostasis), NSAIDs, and antihypertensives as appropriate.
10. **Monitor:** urine output, repeated vital signs, haemoglobin, lactate; consider HDU/ICU for unstable patients.
11. **Nil by mouth.**
12. **Early involvement** of gastroenterology, surgery and interventional radiology.

#### Bloods to send

FBC, group and cross-match (at least 2 units), coagulation (PT/INR, APTT, fibrinogen), U&E (**urea raised out of proportion to creatinine**, urea:creatinine ratio >100 (mmol:µmol) suggests an upper GI source because of digestion of blood), liver function tests, glucose, lactate and blood gas, troponin and ECG (older patients), ferritin.

### 27.4 Risk stratification

#### Glasgow-Blatchford score (GBS)

Used at presentation (before endoscopy) to identify patients at **low risk who can be managed as outpatients** (GBS 0 or ≤1 in many guidelines) versus those who need admission and urgent endoscopy.

| Variable | Points |
|---|---|
| **Blood urea (mmol/L):** 6.5–7.9 / 8.0–9.9 / 10.0–24.9 / ≥25 | 2 / 3 / 4 / 6 |
| **Haemoglobin, men (g/L):** 120–129 / 100–119 / <100 | 1 / 3 / 6 |
| **Haemoglobin, women (g/L):** 100–119 / <100 | 1 / 6 |
| **Systolic BP (mmHg):** 100–109 / 90–99 / <90 | 1 / 2 / 3 |
| **Pulse ≥100** | 1 |
| **Melaena** | 1 |
| **Syncope** | 2 |
| **Hepatic disease** | 2 |
| **Cardiac failure** | 2 |

A score of **0** (or 1) identifies patients who are very unlikely to need intervention; scores above that suggest an increasing need for transfusion, endoscopic therapy or surgery.

#### Rockall score

Post-endoscopy score combining age, shock, comorbidity, diagnosis and endoscopic stigmata to estimate rebleeding and mortality.

#### AIMS65

Albumin <30 g/L, INR >1.5, altered mental status, SBP ≤90 mmHg, age ≥65: predicts in-hospital mortality.

#### Oakland score (lower GI bleeding)

Combines age, sex, previous lower GI bleed, DRE findings, heart rate, systolic BP and haemoglobin: a score of ≤8 identifies patients suitable for discharge.

### 27.5 Upper GI bleeding: causes and management

#### Common causes

| Cause | Features |
|---|---|
| **Peptic ulcer disease** (duodenal more than gastric) | Most common cause; NSAIDs, aspirin, *H. pylori*; epigastric pain, melaena, haematemesis |
| **Oesophageal and gastric varices** | Cirrhosis, portal hypertension; massive haematemesis, haemodynamic compromise, high mortality |
| **Gastric (portal hypertensive) gastropathy, erosive gastritis, oesophagitis, duodenitis** | Alcohol, NSAIDs, stress, reflux |
| **Mallory–Weiss tear** | Retching or vomiting before the bleed; usually self-limiting |
| **Dieulafoy lesion** | Large submucosal artery protruding through a small defect; sudden massive bleeding |
| **Angiodysplasia, gastric antral vascular ectasia (GAVE)** | Older, CKD, aortic stenosis (Heyde syndrome) |
| **Malignancy** (oesophageal, gastric) | Weight loss, dysphagia, anaemia |
| **Aorto-enteric fistula** | Previous aortic graft; "herald" bleed followed by catastrophic haemorrhage |
| **Hereditary haemorrhagic telangiectasia** | Epistaxis, family history, mucocutaneous telangiectasia |
| **Haemobilia, pancreatic pseudocyst rupture** | Pain, jaundice, post-instrumentation |

#### Peptic ulcer bleeding

- **Endoscopy within 24 hours** of presentation after resuscitation (earlier for unstable patients), with therapy for high-risk stigmata (Forrest Ia–IIb: active bleeding, visible vessel, adherent clot): injection (adrenaline), thermal coagulation or clips, often combined (dual therapy).
- **High-dose PPI** (e.g. 80 mg bolus then 8 mg/h infusion, or intermittent high-dose IV) for about 72 hours after successful endoscopic therapy.
- **Test and treat *H. pylori*;** stop NSAIDs; reintroduce aspirin for secondary prevention within days if possible; long-term PPI if continuing antiplatelet or NSAIDs.
- **Failed endoscopic therapy or rebleeding:** repeat endoscopy; **angiographic embolisation** or surgery.

#### Variceal bleeding

- **Suspect in any patient with cirrhosis or portal hypertension** who has haematemesis; mortality at 6 weeks is about 15–20%.
- **Immediate measures:** resuscitation with a restrictive transfusion strategy (Hb target 70–80 g/L), airway protection, **vasoactive drug** started as soon as variceal bleeding is suspected (terlipressin, octreotide or somatostatin, continued 2–5 days), **antibiotic prophylaxis** (ceftriaxone 1 g/day for up to 7 days; reduces infections, rebleeding and mortality), **endoscopy within 12 hours**: **endoscopic variceal band ligation** for oesophageal varices (sclerotherapy or cyanoacrylate glue injection for gastric varices).
- **Failure to control bleeding:** balloon tamponade (Sengstaken–Blakemore or Minnesota tube) or a self-expanding metal stent as a bridge, **early TIPS** (transjugular intrahepatic portosystemic shunt) within 72 hours (pre-emptive TIPS in high-risk patients, Child–Pugh C 10–13 or B with active bleeding).
- **Prevention of complications:** lactulose/rifaximin for hepatic encephalopathy; avoid over-transfusion; monitor for hepatorenal syndrome, infection.
- **Secondary prophylaxis:** non-selective beta-blocker (propranolol or carvedilol) plus repeated band ligation until varices are eradicated; consider TIPS if recurrent.
- **Primary prophylaxis** of variceal bleeding in cirrhosis: screening endoscopy; beta-blockers for medium–large varices or band ligation.

#### Mallory–Weiss tear

Usually settles spontaneously; endoscopic therapy occasionally needed.

#### Aorto-enteric fistula

A "herald bleed" in a patient with an aortic graft requires urgent CT angiography and vascular surgical input: **do not wait for endoscopy** in an unstable patient.

### 27.6 Lower GI bleeding

#### Causes

| Cause | Features |
|---|---|
| **Diverticular disease** | Painless, often profuse, sudden bleeding in older patients; usually stops spontaneously (about 75%) |
| **Angiodysplasia** | Older patients, painless, recurrent, may be right colon; associated with aortic stenosis, CKD |
| **Colitis** (ischaemic, infectious, IBD, radiation) | Bloody diarrhoea, abdominal pain, fever; ischaemic colitis in older patients with sudden left-sided pain |
| **Colorectal cancer and polyps** | Altered bowel habit, weight loss, anaemia; right-sided lesions cause occult bleeding, left-sided bright red blood |
| **Haemorrhoids** | Bright red blood on paper or in the pan after defecation, painless |
| **Anal fissure** | Painful defecation, bright red blood on the stool surface |
| **Post-polypectomy bleeding** | Within 1–2 weeks of the procedure |
| **Rectal ulcers, varices, Meckel diverticulum** (children and young adults: painless large-volume maroon stools), **intussusception**, **small bowel tumours or angiodysplasia** | |
| **Brisk upper GI bleed** | Haemodynamic compromise with haematochezia; always consider |

#### Management of acute lower GI bleeding

1. **Resuscitate** as above; stop anticoagulants and NSAIDs as appropriate.
2. **Identify and treat a brisk upper GI source:** consider **upper endoscopy first** in unstable patients with haematochezia.
3. **Risk stratify** (Oakland score): low-risk patients may be investigated as outpatients; others admitted.
4. **Colonoscopy** after bowel preparation (within 24 hours for severe bleeding) provides diagnosis and therapy (clips, bands, thermal therapy, injection).
5. **CT angiography** (or radionuclide scan) for ongoing brisk bleeding and haemodynamic instability, followed by **angiographic embolisation** if a source is found. Surgery (segmental or subtotal colectomy) is a last resort; try to localise first.
6. **Anorectal causes:** proctoscopy/flexible sigmoidoscopy.
7. **Bleeding that stops:** outpatient colonoscopy for those over 40 or with risk factors, to exclude cancer.

#### Iron-deficiency anaemia as a presentation of occult GI bleeding

In men and postmenopausal women, **iron-deficiency anaemia is gastrointestinal cancer or a bleeding lesion until proven otherwise** (Chapter 2, case 2). Investigate with upper endoscopy (with duodenal biopsy for coeliac disease) and colonoscopy; if negative, consider capsule endoscopy and review medications (NSAIDs). Also consider non-GI causes (menstruation, diet, haematuria, malabsorption).

---

### 27.7 Obscure and recurrent GI bleeding

- Repeat endoscopy (second-look), **capsule endoscopy,** device-assisted enteroscopy, CT/MR enterography, **Meckel scan** (technetium) in young patients, angiography.
- Common sources: angiodysplasia, small bowel tumours (GIST, lymphoma, carcinoid), Crohn's disease, NSAID enteropathy, Dieulafoy lesions, Meckel diverticulum.
- Treat anaemia, iron replacement (IV if intolerant), octreotide or thalidomide for recurrent angiodysplasia in selected cases.

# Part B: Jaundice

### 27.8 Bilirubin metabolism and the classification of jaundice

**Bilirubin** is produced mainly from the breakdown of haemoglobin in the reticuloendothelial system (about 250–400 mg/day). It is:

1. **Unconjugated (indirect),** lipid-soluble, bound to albumin in blood, **not excreted in urine.**
2. Taken up by hepatocytes and **conjugated** with glucuronic acid by UDP-glucuronosyltransferase (UGT1A1) to **conjugated (direct) bilirubin**, water-soluble.
3. Excreted into bile, converted by gut bacteria into urobilinogen and stercobilin (stool pigment), and partly reabsorbed (urobilinogen into urine).

**Jaundice** (icterus) is yellow discolouration of sclerae, skin and mucous membranes, visible when serum bilirubin exceeds about 40–50 µmol/L (2.5–3 mg/dL) (Chapter 10).

#### Classification

| Type | Mechanism | Predominant bilirubin | Urine | Stool | Causes |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Pre-hepatic** | Overproduction of bilirubin exceeding hepatic capacity | **Unconjugated** | Normal colour (**acholuric**; no bilirubin) | Normal or dark | **Haemolysis** (autoimmune haemolytic anaemia, sickle cell, thalassaemia, G6PD deficiency, hereditary spherocytosis, malaria, transfusion reaction, mechanical valve), ineffective erythropoiesis, large haematoma resorption |
| **Hepatic: impaired uptake/conjugation** | Reduced hepatic uptake or conjugation | **Unconjugated** | Normal (acholuric) | Normal | **Gilbert syndrome** (benign, 3–7% of people; mild jaundice with fasting, illness, stress), Crigler–Najjar, neonatal physiological jaundice, drugs (rifampicin) |
| **Hepatocellular** | Liver cell injury; impaired excretion and conjugation | **Mixed, conjugated predominates** | Dark | Normal or pale | Viral hepatitis (A, B, C, D, E, EBV, CMV), alcohol-related liver disease, drug-induced liver injury, autoimmune hepatitis, non-alcoholic steatohepatitis, Wilson disease, haemochromatosis, ischaemic hepatitis, cirrhosis, acute fatty liver of pregnancy, liver tumours |
| **Cholestatic (intrahepatic)** | Impaired bile formation or flow in the liver | **Conjugated** | Dark | **Pale** | Primary biliary cholangitis (PBC), primary sclerosing cholangitis (PSC), drug-induced cholestasis (amoxicillin-clavulanate, flucloxacillin, anabolic steroids, chlorpromazine), sepsis, intrahepatic cholestasis of pregnancy, infiltrative disease, total parenteral nutrition |
| **Obstructive (extrahepatic cholestasis)** | Mechanical obstruction of the bile ducts | **Conjugated** | Dark | **Pale, "putty" coloured**; steatorrhoea | **Gallstones in the common bile duct**, pancreatic head cancer, cholangiocarcinoma, ampullary tumours, biliary strictures, chronic pancreatitis, Mirizzi syndrome, parasitic infection, biliary atresia (neonate) |

Many patients have **mixed** patterns (for example, severe hepatitis with a cholestatic phase). Check bilirubin fractionation to differentiate unconjugated from conjugated hyperbilirubinaemia.

### 27.9 History in a jaundiced patient

- **Onset and course:** sudden or gradual; preceding prodrome (fever, malaise, nausea, anorexia, aversion to cigarettes or alcohol, arthralgia, rash) suggests viral hepatitis.
- **Pain:** **painless jaundice** (pancreatic or biliary cancer, PBC, hepatocellular) vs painful (biliary colic, cholangitis, hepatitis, pancreatitis, liver congestion).
- **Itch (pruritus):** cholestasis (often precedes jaundice in PBC and cholestasis of pregnancy).
- **Urine and stool colour:** dark urine and pale stool: obstruction or cholestasis.
- **Fever, rigors:** cholangitis, hepatitis, liver abscess, haemolytic crisis, sepsis.
- **Weight loss, anorexia:** malignancy, chronic disease.
- **Alcohol intake** (quantity, duration), **risk factors for hepatitis** (injecting drug use, tattoos, transfusion, sexual exposure, travel, unsafe food and water, shellfish, healthcare work), contact with jaundiced persons.
- **Drugs and toxins:** **paracetamol** (intentional or therapeutic excess), amoxicillin-clavulanate, flucloxacillin, isoniazid and rifampicin, methotrexate, amiodarone, NSAIDs, statins, valproate, azathioprine, anabolic steroids, oral contraceptives, herbal and dietary supplements (green tea extract, kava, turmeric), recreational drugs (ecstasy), mushrooms (*Amanita phalloides*), industrial solvents.
- **Past history:** gallstones, biliary surgery, IBD (PSC), autoimmune disease, haemolytic disease or anaemia, liver disease, cancer, diabetes, obesity, previous jaundice, Gilbert syndrome, family history (haemochromatosis, Wilson disease, hereditary haemolytic anaemias, A1AT deficiency).
- **Pregnancy:** third trimester pruritus and jaundice (intrahepatic cholestasis of pregnancy); abdominal pain, hypertension, vomiting, hypoglycaemia (HELLP, acute fatty liver).
- **Mental state:** confusion, sleep reversal (hepatic encephalopathy).

### 27.10 Examination

- **General:** distress, fever, wasting, confusion, asterixis, foetor hepaticus.
- **Jaundice** (sclera, under the tongue), scratch marks, xanthelasma (PBC), pallor, bruising.
- **Stigmata of chronic liver disease:** clubbing, leuconychia, palmar erythema, Dupuytren contracture, spider naevi, gynaecomastia, testicular atrophy, loss of body hair, parotid enlargement, ascites, caput medusae, oedema, splenomegaly (Chapter 14).
- **Liver:** size (enlarged, tender in acute hepatitis and congestion; hard and nodular in cancer; small and shrunken in advanced cirrhosis).
- **Gallbladder:** **Courvoisier law** (a palpable, non-tender gallbladder in a jaundiced patient suggests malignant obstruction rather than stones).
- **Spleen:** haemolysis, portal hypertension, lymphoma.
- **Virchow node,** other lymphadenopathy.
- **Abdominal tenderness, masses.**
- **Kayser–Fleischer rings (slit lamp),** neurological signs (Wilson disease); signs of haemochromatosis (bronze skin, arthropathy, diabetes); cardiac failure (JVP); rectal examination (pale stool).

### 27.11 Investigations

#### First-line

| Test | Purpose |
|---|---|
| **Fractionated bilirubin** (total, conjugated/direct) | Unconjugated vs conjugated |
| **Liver enzymes: ALT, AST, ALP, GGT** | Pattern of injury |
| **Albumin, INR/PT** | Synthetic function (prognostic; INR not corrected by vitamin K in hepatocellular failure) |
| **FBC, film, reticulocytes, LDH, haptoglobin, direct antiglobulin (Coombs) test** | Haemolysis |
| **U&E, glucose, CRP, lactate** | Complications, infection |
| **Hepatitis serology:** HAV IgM, HBsAg and anti-HBc IgM, HCV antibody ± RNA, HEV IgM; also EBV, CMV | Viral hepatitis |
| **Paracetamol level** | If unexplained acute liver injury |
| **Pregnancy test** | Women of reproductive age |
| **Ultrasound abdomen (first-line imaging)** | Bile duct dilatation (intra- and extrahepatic), gallstones, liver texture, masses, spleen, ascites, hepatic and portal veins |

#### Interpretation of liver test patterns

Calculate the **R factor** = (ALT ÷ upper limit of normal) ÷ (ALP ÷ upper limit of normal):

- **R >5:** hepatocellular pattern.
- **R <2:** cholestatic pattern.
- **R 2–5:** mixed.

| Pattern | Liver tests | Causes |
|---|---|---|
| **Hepatocellular** | ALT/AST markedly raised (often >10× normal in acute hepatitis); ALP normal or mildly raised | Viral hepatitis, ischaemic hepatitis (very high levels, rapid fall), paracetamol, autoimmune hepatitis, drug-induced, alcohol (AST:ALT >2:1, AST rarely >300 U/L), NAFLD (ALT>AST, mild), Wilson disease, haemochromatosis |
| **Cholestatic** | ALP and GGT disproportionately raised; bilirubin high; ALT mildly raised | Obstruction, PBC, PSC, drug-induced cholestasis, infiltrative disease |
| **Isolated unconjugated hyperbilirubinaemia** | Bilirubin raised, other tests normal | Gilbert syndrome, haemolysis |
| **Isolated raised ALP** | ALP elevated, GGT normal | Bone disease (Paget, metastases), pregnancy, growth |
| **Mixed** | | Many |

#### Second-line

- **MRCP** (magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography): best non-invasive imaging of the bile ducts (stones, strictures, PSC, tumours).
- **CT abdomen** (with contrast): pancreatic head mass, liver tumours, staging.
- **Endoscopic ultrasound (EUS)**: small ampullary or pancreatic lesions, bile duct stones, biopsy.
- **ERCP:** therapeutic (stone extraction, stent) — avoid as purely diagnostic because of risk (pancreatitis).
- **Autoimmune screen:** ANA, anti-smooth muscle antibody, anti-mitochondrial antibody (AMA, PBC), IgG levels, ANCA, immunoglobulins.
- **Metabolic:** ferritin and transferrin saturation (haemochromatosis), caeruloplasmin (Wilson disease; age <40), alpha-1 antitrypsin level and phenotype, lipid profile.
- **Liver biopsy:** unexplained persistent abnormalities, autoimmune hepatitis, drug-induced liver injury, staging.
- **Elastography (FibroScan)** for fibrosis assessment.
- **Haemolysis work-up:** G6PD, haemoglobin electrophoresis, flow cytometry, spherocytes on film.
- **Tumour markers:** CA 19-9 (pancreaticobiliary; not specific, raised in cholestasis), AFP (hepatocellular carcinoma).

### 27.12 Emergencies in jaundice

#### Acute cholangitis

- **Charcot triad:** fever, jaundice, RUQ pain; **Reynolds pentad:** + hypotension, confusion. Medical and surgical emergency.
- Management: resuscitation, **blood cultures, IV broad-spectrum antibiotics** (covering enteric Gram-negative bacilli, anaerobes, enterococci), analgesia, and **urgent biliary drainage** (ERCP with sphincterotomy and stone extraction or stent; percutaneous transhepatic drainage if ERCP is not feasible). Severity grading by the Tokyo Guidelines.

#### Acute liver failure (ALF)

- **Definition:** rapid deterioration of liver function with **coagulopathy (INR ≥1.5) and hepatic encephalopathy** in a patient without pre-existing liver disease, within 26 weeks (hyperacute <7 days; acute 7–28 days; subacute >28 days).
- **Causes:** paracetamol (most common in many countries), viral hepatitis (A, B, E), drug-induced (idiosyncratic), autoimmune hepatitis, Wilson disease, ischaemic hepatitis, acute fatty liver of pregnancy and HELLP, *Amanita* mushroom poisoning, Budd–Chiari syndrome, malignant infiltration.
- **Management:** early referral to a transplant centre; **N-acetylcysteine** (for paracetamol and also beneficial in non-paracetamol ALF); intensive care; glucose monitoring (hypoglycaemia), correct electrolytes; manage cerebral oedema (head-up position, hypertonic saline, mannitol, avoid sedation if possible); treat infection; **avoid** routine correction of INR, as it is used for prognosis; **King's College criteria** guide listing for transplantation.
- **Warning signs:** rising INR, encephalopathy, hypoglycaemia, metabolic acidosis, renal failure.

#### Haemolytic crisis

- Jaundice with pallor, dark urine (haemoglobinuria), splenomegaly, anaemia and raised reticulocytes: treat the cause (steroids for autoimmune haemolysis, exchange transfusion in severe sickle crisis, stop offending drugs, supportive transfusion).

#### Others

- **Decompensated cirrhosis:** jaundice with ascites, hepatic encephalopathy, variceal bleeding, spontaneous bacterial peritonitis, hepatorenal syndrome, hepatocellular carcinoma.
- **Pancreatitis** with biliary obstruction.
- **Sepsis-associated cholestasis.**
- **Obstetric emergencies:** acute fatty liver of pregnancy, HELLP, severe intrahepatic cholestasis of pregnancy (stillbirth risk): urgent obstetric and hepatology care.

### 27.13 Special conditions

- **Gilbert syndrome:** benign, common (UGT1A1 promoter variation); mild fluctuating unconjugated hyperbilirubinaemia (usually <70 µmol/L), triggered by fasting, intercurrent illness, exercise or stress; normal liver tests otherwise and no haemolysis. Diagnosis by exclusion; no treatment.
- **Drug-induced liver injury (DILI):** a diagnosis of exclusion; temporal association, typical pattern; withdraw the suspected drug. Amoxicillin-clavulanate and flucloxacillin cause cholestatic injury, sometimes appearing weeks after the course finishes.
- **Intrahepatic cholestasis of pregnancy:** third-trimester generalised itching (especially palms and soles), raised serum bile acids, mildly raised transaminases; ursodeoxycholic acid; plan early delivery (e.g. 35–37 weeks, depending on bile acid levels) because of the risk of stillbirth.
- **Primary biliary cholangitis (PBC):** middle-aged woman, fatigue, itch, raised ALP, AMA positive; ursodeoxycholic acid; obeticholic acid or fibrates in non-responders.
- **Primary sclerosing cholangitis (PSC):** younger men, IBD (ulcerative colitis); cholestatic enzymes; MRCP with beading; risk of cholangiocarcinoma and colon cancer.
- **Wilson disease:** age <40 with liver disease, neuropsychiatric features, Coombs-negative haemolysis, low caeruloplasmin, Kayser–Fleischer rings.
- **Pancreatic cancer and cholangiocarcinoma:** painless progressive jaundice, weight loss, pruritus, palpable gallbladder; investigate urgently.
- **Neonatal jaundice:** see Chapter 58 (physiological vs pathological; jaundice in the first 24 hours or beyond 2 weeks requires urgent evaluation; biliary atresia in prolonged conjugated hyperbilirubinaemia).

### 27.14 Worked cases

#### Case 1: Variceal haemorrhage

*A 54-year-old man with alcohol-related cirrhosis vomits a large volume of fresh blood and collapses. HR 128, BP 84/50, spider naevi, ascites, asterixis. Hb 82 g/L, platelets 70, INR 1.9.*

**Reasoning.** Haematemesis in cirrhosis with portal hypertension: **variceal bleeding** until proved otherwise. This is a life-threatening emergency.

**Action.** Airway protection (consider intubation before endoscopy), two large-bore lines, **restrictive transfusion** (target Hb 70–80), IV **terlipressin** and **ceftriaxone**, urgent endoscopy within 12 hours with **band ligation**, PPI afterwards, lactulose, thiamine, careful fluids; if bleeding is uncontrolled, balloon tamponade/stent as a bridge to **TIPS**. Do not give FFP to "correct" the INR.

#### Case 2: NSAID-related ulcer

*A 70-year-old woman with osteoarthritis on regular ibuprofen and low-dose aspirin has two episodes of melaena and dizziness. HR 106, BP 106/62, Hb 74 g/L, urea 16 mmol/L, creatinine 90 µmol/L. Glasgow-Blatchford score 13.*

**Reasoning.** Melaena, raised urea:creatinine ratio, anaemia and NSAID/aspirin use: **upper GI bleed from peptic ulcer.** High GBS: admit and urgent endoscopy.

**Action.** Resuscitate, transfuse if Hb <70 (or per local cardiac threshold), IV PPI, endoscopy within 24 hours with therapy for a Forrest Ib duodenal ulcer, high-dose PPI infusion post-procedure, *H. pylori* testing and eradication, stop NSAIDs; decide on aspirin (resume early if for secondary prevention), long-term PPI.

#### Case 3: Anticoagulated patient with melaena

*A 78-year-old man on warfarin for atrial fibrillation (INR 5.4) presents with melaena and light-headedness. HR 110, BP 98/60.*

**Action.** Resuscitate, stop warfarin, **PCC plus IV vitamin K** for major bleeding, endoscopy once stabilised; restart anticoagulation after haemostasis once the risk of rebleeding is reassessed (early restart is often appropriate, typically within 7 days, depending on thrombotic risk); consider alternatives (DOAC, left atrial appendage occlusion).

#### Case 4: Lower GI bleeding in an older patient

*A 76-year-old man has sudden painless passage of a large volume of dark red blood. HR 104, BP 118/70. Rectal examination: maroon blood, no mass. Hb 108 g/L. Oakland score is high.*

**Reasoning.** Painless, large-volume haematochezia in an older patient: **diverticular bleeding** most likely; angiodysplasia and colitis are alternatives. Exclude a brisk upper source if unstable.

**Action.** Admit, resuscitate, stop anticoagulants/NSAIDs, colonoscopy within 24 hours after preparation; CT angiography with embolisation if ongoing instability. Most diverticular bleeds stop spontaneously.

#### Case 5: Painless jaundice

*A 66-year-old man has had 3 weeks of progressive jaundice, dark urine, pale stool, itch, 7 kg weight loss, and a new diagnosis of diabetes. There is no pain. Examination: deep jaundice, scratch marks, palpable non-tender gallbladder. Bilirubin 280 µmol/L (conjugated), ALP 640 U/L, ALT 90.*

**Reasoning.** Progressive painless obstructive jaundice with weight loss, new diabetes and a palpable gallbladder (Courvoisier law): **pancreatic head cancer** (or ampullary tumour/cholangiocarcinoma).

**Action.** Ultrasound (dilated intra- and extrahepatic ducts), then CT pancreas protocol, EUS with biopsy, CA 19-9 (as baseline), MDT; biliary decompression (ERCP and stent) for symptoms or before chemotherapy or surgery; treat itch; nutritional support.

#### Case 6: Viral hepatitis

*A 24-year-old woman returned from a trip to India, has 5 days of anorexia, nausea, malaise and right upper quadrant discomfort, then dark urine and jaundice. ALT 2,100 U/L, ALP 180, bilirubin 120 µmol/L, INR 1.2. HAV IgM positive.*

**Reasoning.** Hepatocellular pattern after travel with prodrome: **acute hepatitis A**, usually self-limited. Check INR, glucose, mental status to detect acute liver failure.

**Action.** Supportive care; avoid alcohol and hepatotoxic drugs; monitor INR and encephalopathy; notify public health; vaccinate and give immunoglobulin to close contacts; exclude other hepatitis viruses.

#### Case 7: Haemolytic jaundice

*A 32-year-old woman has 2 weeks of fatigue, pallor and mild jaundice, with dark urine but pale-yellow stool; no itch. Examination: splenomegaly. Hb 72 g/L, reticulocytes 12%, bilirubin 68 µmol/L (mostly unconjugated), LDH high, haptoglobin undetectable, direct antiglobulin test positive; ALT and ALP normal.*

**Reasoning.** Pre-hepatic jaundice with unconjugated hyperbilirubinaemia, anaemia, reticulocytosis, raised LDH and low haptoglobin, DAT positive: **warm autoimmune haemolytic anaemia.**

**Action.** Prednisolone; look for underlying cause (SLE, lymphoma, CLL, drugs, infections); folate supplementation, transfuse cautiously if necessary; second-line therapy (rituximab) if refractory.

#### Case 8: Drug-induced cholestasis

*A 59-year-old man develops itching and jaundice 3 weeks after a course of amoxicillin-clavulanate for a chest infection (completed 10 days ago). He is otherwise well. ALP 520, ALT 140, bilirubin 90 (conjugated). Ultrasound normal, hepatitis serology negative.*

**Reasoning.** Cholestatic pattern after amoxicillin-clavulanate (typical latency up to weeks after finishing), normal biliary imaging, exclusion of other causes: **drug-induced cholestatic liver injury.**

**Action.** Avoid the drug in future (document the reaction), symptomatic treatment of itch (cholestyramine, rifampicin, naltrexone in selected cases), ursodeoxycholic acid may help; monitor LFTs; most recover within weeks to months; refer if persistent.

#### Case 9: Paracetamol-induced acute liver failure

*A 22-year-old woman presents 36 hours after an intentional overdose of paracetamol; she is drowsy, vomiting, jaundiced. INR 3.4, ALT 6,500, creatinine 180, pH 7.28, lactate 5.2, glucose 2.6 mmol/L.*

**Reasoning.** Paracetamol-induced acute liver failure with encephalopathy, coagulopathy, acidosis and AKI: severe; meets criteria for transplant-centre referral.

**Action.** Immediately start **IV N-acetylcysteine** (regardless of time since ingestion when there is liver injury), correct hypoglycaemia, contact the liver unit, ICU care, monitor INR, lactate, creatinine, mental state; psychiatric assessment after recovery; King's College criteria for listing (arterial pH <7.3 after resuscitation, or INR >6.5 with creatinine >300 µmol/L and grade 3–4 encephalopathy).

#### Case 10: Itch in pregnancy

*A 32-year-old woman at 34 weeks has severe generalised itching, worst on palms and soles at night, with no rash, and mild jaundice. Blood: ALT 120, bile acids 45 µmol/L, blood pressure normal, urinalysis normal.*

**Reasoning.** Third-trimester pruritus with raised bile acids: **intrahepatic cholestasis of pregnancy.** Exclude pre-eclampsia, HELLP, hepatitis, and biliary disease.

**Action.** Ursodeoxycholic acid, weekly monitoring of LFTs and bile acids, plan delivery at about 35–37 weeks depending on bile acid levels, counsel regarding stillbirth risk, check postpartum LFTs.

### 27.15 Common pitfalls

- **Being reassured by a normal initial haemoglobin** in acute bleeding.
- **Missing tachycardia blunting** in older patients and those on beta-blockers.
- **Over-transfusing** (especially in variceal bleeding) or failing to transfuse in massive haemorrhage.
- **Giving tranexamic acid** routinely.
- **Delaying endoscopy** in a stable patient beyond 24 hours, or performing it in an unresuscitated patient.
- **Failing to give antibiotics and vasoactive drugs** in suspected variceal bleeding.
- **Assuming haematochezia is always lower GI.**
- **Missing aorto-enteric fistula** in a patient with an aortic graft.
- **Treating iron-deficiency anaemia with iron without investigating the source.**
- **Attributing all jaundice to alcohol or gallstones** without ultrasound.
- **Missing haemolysis** (jaundice with anaemia and reticulocytosis) and Gilbert syndrome (avoid unnecessary investigations).
- **Overlooking drugs, herbal supplements and paracetamol.**
- **Delaying referral in acute liver failure** (rapid deterioration; transplant window).
- **Performing diagnostic ERCP** for simple investigation, instead of MRCP.
- **Failing to consider cholangitis** in a febrile jaundiced patient.

### 27.16 Key points

- In GI bleeding, resuscitate first: ABC, IV access, crystalloid, restrictive transfusion (Hb threshold ~70 g/L), correct coagulopathy appropriately, and involve gastroenterology early.
- Use the Glasgow-Blatchford score to find low-risk upper GI bleeds suitable for outpatient care; endoscopy within 24 hours for others.
- Suspected variceal bleeding: vasoactive drug, antibiotics, endoscopy within 12 hours with band ligation; TIPS for failure.
- Haematochezia may be from an upper source if the patient is unstable.
- Iron-deficiency anaemia in adults needs GI investigation.
- Classify jaundice by bilirubin type and liver test pattern: pre-hepatic (unconjugated), hepatocellular, cholestatic/obstructive.
- Ultrasound first, then MRCP/CT/EUS; avoid diagnostic ERCP.
- Emergencies: cholangitis (drainage and antibiotics), acute liver failure (NAC, early transplant referral), haemolytic crisis.
- Ask about drugs, alcohol, travel, pregnancy, and family history in every jaundiced patient.

### 27.17 Self-assessment

1. What features suggest an upper GI source in a patient presenting with melaena or haematochezia?
   *Answer: Melaena itself, raised urea relative to creatinine, history of haematemesis, NSAID or ulcer history, liver disease; haematochezia with shock may also indicate a brisk upper source.*

2. What haemoglobin threshold and target are recommended for most patients with acute upper GI bleeding?
   *Answer: Transfuse at Hb <70 g/L, aiming for 70–90 g/L (higher thresholds in significant cardiovascular disease); avoid over-transfusion in variceal bleeding.*

3. List the key components of initial management of variceal bleeding.
   *Answer: Resuscitation with restrictive transfusion, airway protection, vasoactive drug (terlipressin/octreotide), antibiotic prophylaxis (ceftriaxone), endoscopy within 12 hours with band ligation, TIPS or balloon tamponade for refractory bleeding.*

4. How does conjugated hyperbilirubinaemia differ from unconjugated in terms of urine colour?
   *Answer: Conjugated bilirubin is water-soluble and appears in urine (dark urine); unconjugated bilirubin is albumin-bound and not excreted in urine (acholuric jaundice).*

5. What is the R factor and what ranges define hepatocellular and cholestatic patterns?
   *Answer: R = (ALT/ULN) ÷ (ALP/ULN); R >5 hepatocellular, R <2 cholestatic, 2–5 mixed.*

6. What is the definition of acute liver failure, and what is the first drug to give in suspected paracetamol-related ALF?
   *Answer: Coagulopathy (INR ≥1.5) and hepatic encephalopathy within 26 weeks of illness onset in a patient without pre-existing liver disease; IV N-acetylcysteine.*
