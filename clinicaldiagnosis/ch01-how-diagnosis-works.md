# Part I: Foundations of Clinical Reasoning

## Chapter 1. How Diagnosis Works

### Learning objectives

After studying this chapter you should be able to:

1. Describe what a diagnosis is *for*, and why a "correct label" is only part of the goal.
2. Contrast pattern recognition (fast, intuitive) with hypothetico-deductive reasoning (slow, analytic), and say when each is appropriate.
3. Explain how clinicians build and use *illness scripts*.
4. Convert between probability and odds, and update a probability with a likelihood ratio.
5. Explain why the same test result means different things in different patients and settings, using prevalence.
6. Hold a working diagnosis with appropriate confidence and revise it when new data arrive.

---

### 1.1 What is a diagnosis?

A diagnosis is a **working explanation of a patient's problem that is good enough to guide action**. Notice the three parts of that definition.

- *Working*: a diagnosis is provisional. It is the best current hypothesis, and it should change when the evidence changes.
- *Explanation*: it links symptoms, signs and results into one coherent story, ideally with a mechanism.
- *Guide action*: its value lies in what it makes you do (treat, test, reassure, refer, wait) and what it lets you tell the patient about the future (prognosis).

This has a practical consequence. You do not always need a precise label to act well. A patient with fever, hypotension and a raised respiratory rate needs fluids, cultures and antibiotics within the hour; the exact organism and source can wait. Conversely, a precise label that changes nothing (say, an incidental finding of uncertain meaning) can cause harm through anxiety, further testing and overtreatment.

Four functions of diagnosis are worth keeping in mind:

| Function | Question it answers | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Classification | What kind of problem is this? | "This is a heart failure syndrome." |
| Explanation | Why is it happening? | "Caused by ischaemic cardiomyopathy." |
| Prognosis | What will happen? | "Likely to worsen without treatment; good response expected to ACE inhibitor." |
| Decision | What should we do now? | "Admit, give diuretic, check renal function." |

A useful clinician moves between these levels. Early in an encounter you often work at the level of *syndrome or danger* ("an acutely unwell patient with chest pain"), and only later at the level of *specific disease*.

---

### 1.2 Two ways of thinking

Research on clinical reasoning consistently finds that experienced clinicians use two overlapping modes, often described as **System 1** and **System 2** thinking.

**Pattern recognition (System 1).** You see a patient and the diagnosis "jumps out". A child with a barking cough and stridor: croup. A man with crushing central chest pain, sweating and grey skin: acute myocardial infarction until proven otherwise. This mode is fast, effortless, and usually right in familiar situations. It works because experience has stored thousands of *illness scripts* (see 1.3).

**Hypothetico-deductive reasoning (System 2).** You generate a short list of hypotheses from early cues, then deliberately seek the history, signs and tests that distinguish them. This mode is slow, effortful and explicit. It is what you use when the pattern is unfamiliar, when the first impression does not fit, or when the stakes are high.

Neither mode is "better". Their failure modes differ:

- Pattern recognition fails through *bias*: you recognise a pattern that is not really there, or you fail to notice that a cue does not fit (Chapter 2).
- Analytic reasoning fails through *overload and delay*: it is slow, tiring, and can generate endless possibilities without a way to rank them.

Skilled clinicians **switch**. They accept the quick impression as a hypothesis, then ask: "What else could this be? What doesn't fit? What must I not miss?" This single habit, stepping back from the first impression, is the cheapest and most powerful safeguard in diagnosis.

#### When to slow down

Deliberately switch to analytic mode when:

- the presentation is atypical, or the patient is at the extremes of age;
- the first diagnosis does not explain *all* the findings;
- the patient has been seen several times for the same problem;
- the patient is not responding to treatment as expected;
- the cost of being wrong is high (chest pain, headache with neurological features, abdominal pain in pregnancy, fever in an immunocompromised person);
- you feel unusually certain very early in the encounter.

### 1.3 Illness scripts

An **illness script** is a mental summary of a disease: who gets it (epidemiology), how it develops (time course), what it does (pathophysiology), and what it looks like (symptoms, signs, results). Expert diagnosis is largely the matching of a patient to a stored script.

Consider a script for **acute appendicitis**:

- *Who*: most common in older children and young adults; can occur at any age.
- *Time course*: pain over hours to a day or two, often starting around the umbilicus, then moving to the right iliac fossa.
- *Features*: anorexia, nausea, low-grade fever, localised tenderness, guarding; pain on coughing or moving.
- *Typical results*: raised white cell count and CRP (not always).
- *Complications*: perforation, abscess, peritonitis.

Compare it with **ureteric colic**: sudden severe loin-to-groin pain that comes in waves, the patient writhing and unable to lie still, haematuria on dipstick. The *restlessness* versus the *stillness* of peritonitis is a classic script difference.

How scripts are built and used:

- **Beginners** memorise lists of features and use them as checklists.
- **Experts** hold richer, more flexible scripts including *variants* (the elderly patient with silent appendicitis), *mimics* and *exceptions*.
- You build scripts by **seeing many patients and getting feedback**. A script not corrected by outcomes can be confidently wrong.

**Practical tip.** After each patient, write a one-line summary using *semantic qualifiers*: pairs of opposites that sharpen the picture. For example: "acute vs chronic", "localised vs diffuse", "constant vs intermittent", "febrile vs afebrile". "A 62-year-old man with an *acute*, *severe*, *tearing* chest pain radiating to the back, with *unequal arm blood pressures*" is a very different problem from "chronic, intermittent, exertional chest tightness relieved by rest."

### 1.4 The hypothetico-deductive cycle

The deliberate mode follows a loop:

1. **Gather initial cues**: age, sex, chief complaint, general appearance, vital signs.
2. **Generate hypotheses** early (within the first minute or two). Aim for three to five, not thirty.
3. **Refine** with targeted history and examination: each question should aim to raise or lower specific hypotheses.
4. **Test** only when the result could change management (Chapter 4).
5. **Reassess**: does one hypothesis now explain everything? If so, act on it. If not, return to step 2.

Example of efficient questioning. A 45-year-old woman presents with breathlessness.

| Hypothesis | Question or finding that discriminates |
|---|---|
| Asthma | Wheeze, nocturnal cough, triggers, personal or family atopy |
| Heart failure | Orthopnoea, ankle swelling, raised JVP, crackles |
| Pulmonary embolism | Sudden onset, pleuritic pain, recent immobilisation, calf swelling |
| Anaemia | Pallor, heavy menstrual bleeding, fatigue |
| Anxiety / hyperventilation | Tingling around the mouth, normal oxygen saturation, situational trigger (diagnosis of exclusion) |

Notice that every question is *purposeful*. Asking about leg swelling is not a ritual; it moves the probabilities of PE and heart failure.

### 1.5 Probability: the language of diagnostic uncertainty

Diagnosis is rarely certain. It is the progressive narrowing of uncertainty, and the honest way to describe this is with probabilities.

#### Pre-test and post-test probability

- **Pre-test probability**: the chance of the disease *before* you know the test result, based on the patient's age, sex, symptoms, setting and risk factors.
- **Post-test probability**: the chance of disease *after* the result.

A test does not give you "the answer". It moves a probability up or down. How far depends on both the test and where you started.

#### Odds and likelihood ratios

Probabilities do not combine easily. **Odds** do.

- Odds = probability / (1 − probability)
- Probability = odds / (1 + odds)

A **likelihood ratio (LR)** tells you how much a finding changes the odds:

- LR+ = sensitivity / (1 − specificity)
- LR− = (1 − sensitivity) / specificity

The updating rule is simple:

> **Post-test odds = pre-test odds × likelihood ratio**

Rough guide to the strength of an LR:

| LR+ | LR− | Effect on probability |
|---|---|---|
| > 10 | < 0.1 | Large, often conclusive |
| 5–10 | 0.1–0.2 | Moderate |
| 2–5 | 0.2–0.5 | Small but sometimes useful |
| 1–2 | 0.5–1 | Minimal |
| 1 | 1 | None |

**Worked example.** A 40-year-old man has a pre-test probability of 10% for a condition. A finding with LR+ = 5 is present.

1. Pre-test odds = 0.10 / 0.90 = 0.111
2. Post-test odds = 0.111 × 5 = 0.556
3. Post-test probability = 0.556 / 1.556 = **0.357 (about 36%)**

A positive finding with a "good" LR of 5 still leaves him *more likely not to have the disease than to have it*. This surprises many learners. The starting point matters.

If the same finding were present in a patient with a pre-test probability of 50% (odds = 1), the post-test odds would be 5 and the probability **83%**.

#### Clinical findings have likelihood ratios too

The same arithmetic applies to history and examination, not only to laboratory tests. Examples of the *kind* of finding that is genuinely informative: a very high-risk feature (focal neurological deficit in a patient with headache), a very specific sign (a pulsatile expansile mass in the abdomen), or a very sensitive feature (absence of a cough and fever makes pneumonia much less likely). A symptom with LRs near 1 (for example, "feeling tired" in many conditions) adds almost nothing, and gathering it is not diagnostic work.

### 1.6 Prevalence: why context changes everything

The **prevalence** of a disease in the group you are looking at determines the pre-test probability. The same symptom therefore carries different meaning in different places.

Chest pain in:

- a 25-year-old athlete in primary care: almost always musculoskeletal or benign;
- a 65-year-old diabetic smoker in an emergency department: a substantial chance of acute coronary syndrome.

#### The effect on predictive value

Suppose a test has **sensitivity 90% and specificity 90%**. Apply it to 10,000 people at two different prevalences.

**Prevalence 1% (low)**

| | Disease | No disease | Total |
|---|---|---|---|
| Test positive | 90 | 990 | 1,080 |
| Test negative | 10 | 8,910 | 8,920 |
| Total | 100 | 9,900 | 10,000 |

Positive predictive value (PPV) = 90 / 1,080 = **8.3%**. Most positives are false.

**Prevalence 50% (high)**

| | Disease | No disease | Total |
|---|---|---|---|
| Test positive | 4,500 | 500 | 5,000 |
| Test negative | 500 | 4,500 | 5,000 |
| Total | 5,000 | 5,000 | 10,000 |

PPV = 4,500 / 5,000 = **90%**.

*The same test, with the same sensitivity and specificity, is a poor confirmer in a low-prevalence setting and an excellent one in a high-prevalence setting.* This is why screening healthy populations generates many false positives, and why a positive result must always be interpreted in context.

> **Rule of thumb:** Common things are common. When hoofbeats are heard, think horses before zebras. But *write down* the zebra that would be fatal if missed (Chapter 3).

### 1.7 The role of time

Time is a diagnostic tool, and often the most underused. Three uses:

1. **Natural history.** Many self-limiting illnesses (viral upper respiratory infections, most non-specific abdominal pain) resolve without a precise diagnosis. Reassessment after a defined interval is a legitimate strategy when the danger is low.
2. **Response to treatment.** Improvement after a bronchodilator supports asthma; failure to improve makes you revisit the diagnosis. Be careful: some conditions improve with treatment of the wrong diagnosis, and some serious conditions fluctuate.
3. **Evolution.** Appendicitis may look vague at hour 6 and obvious at hour 18. Meningococcal disease may begin with fever and a normal-looking skin. Repeated examination catches evolving disease.

Always **state the plan for time**: "If the pain has not settled in 24 hours, or if you develop vomiting, fever or worsening pain, return immediately." This is safety-netting (Chapter 73), and it turns uncertainty into a managed risk.

### 1.8 Working diagnoses and uncertainty

You will often act before you are sure. The honest way to do this is with a **working diagnosis** and a clear list of alternatives.

A good working diagnosis statement has four parts:

1. *The leading diagnosis* and your confidence ("probably").
2. *The main alternatives*, especially the dangerous ones.
3. *What would change your mind.*
4. *What you are doing about it now.*

Example: "This is most likely a viral gastroenteritis (leading). I have excluded signs of peritonitis on examination today, but appendicitis is the diagnosis I cannot yet rule out (alternative). If the pain localises to the right lower abdomen, or if he becomes more unwell, I'll reconsider (what would change my mind). For now, oral fluids, and review in 12 hours (plan)."

Tolerating uncertainty is a clinical skill. Over-certainty closes the mind too early. Over-hedging ("it could be anything") paralyses action. The aim is *calibration*: when you say you are 80% sure, you should be right about 80% of the time.

### 1.9 Worked cases

#### Case 1: Pattern recognition done right

A 3-year-old attends at 2 a.m. with a barking cough, hoarse voice and inspiratory stridor at rest, no drooling, fully immunised, temperature 37.8 °C. He is alert and drinking.

*Reasoning.* The script for **viral croup** fits closely: age 6 months to 3 years, URTI prodrome, barking cough, stridor. Pattern recognition is appropriate and likely correct. The analytic check takes seconds: "What dangerous alternatives share some features?" Epiglottitis (toxic child, drooling, muffled voice, no cough), bacterial tracheitis (high fever, toxic, poor response to treatment), foreign-body aspiration (sudden choking episode). None fits, so you treat with corticosteroid, observe, and give safety-net advice.

*Lesson.* Fast recognition is excellent when the pattern is classic, and a brief "what else?" check costs almost nothing.

#### Case 2: When the pattern doesn't fit

A 68-year-old man with known COPD is brought in with worsening breathlessness for 2 days. His wheeze is no worse than usual, he has no productive cough or fever, and his oxygen saturation is 89% on air (his baseline is 93%). Pulse is 112 and regular. His left calf is mildly swollen; he returned from a long-haul flight 9 days ago.

*Reasoning.* The pattern "COPD patient with breathlessness = exacerbation" is strong but **does not explain everything**: no change in sputum, no fever, a tachycardia, and a swollen calf after a flight. Several features point to **pulmonary embolism**. The analytic system is triggered because *the first diagnosis fails to explain all the findings*.

*Action.* Estimate PE probability with a validated tool (Chapter 70), and arrange imaging (CT pulmonary angiography) according to the result. Treat the COPD component in parallel.

*Lesson.* A prior label is a hypothesis, not a fact. The old diagnosis (COPD) can make you blind to the new one.

#### Case 3: Prevalence in action

Two patients each present with sudden, severe chest pain and each has a negative first troponin 6 hours after onset.

- **Patient A**: 24-year-old, non-smoker, no family history, pain worse on inspiration and lying flat, a pericardial friction rub, recent viral illness.
- **Patient B**: 63-year-old man, diabetic, smoker, pain central and pressure-like, radiating to the jaw, sweaty during pain.

*Reasoning.* For A, the pre-test probability of acute coronary syndrome is very low and a script for **pericarditis** fits. A negative troponin (a test with a very low LR− in this time window) nearly rules out myocardial infarction. For B, the pre-test probability is high; a single negative troponin lowers it but does not eliminate it, and you proceed with serial testing, ECG monitoring and risk stratification. The *same* negative result leads to discharge in one and to continued evaluation in the other.

*Lesson.* The test result is not interpreted alone. Pre-test probability is half of the equation.

#### Case 4: Likelihood ratios at the bedside

A 55-year-old woman has a 5-day cough. Your pre-test probability of community-acquired pneumonia is about 5%. You find a respiratory rate of 26, fever, and focal crackles. Informally, you judge that this cluster makes pneumonia substantially more likely, perhaps pushing the probability to 40–50%. A chest X-ray is now reasonable because the result could change management: a clear film in a patient with this cluster would still leave doubt, but a consolidation would confirm.

*Compare*: a 25-year-old with 2 days of dry cough, no fever, a normal chest examination and normal vital signs has a pre-test probability under 1–2%. A chest X-ray here is more likely to find an irrelevant abnormality than to change care.

*Lesson.* Findings from the bedside move probabilities. Tests are most valuable when probability is in the middle zone.

### 1.10 Common pitfalls

- **Treating the label as the goal.** The goal is a good decision and a good outcome.
- **Ignoring base rates.** Rare diagnoses are tempting because they are interesting.
- **Confusing sensitivity with PPV.** "The test is 95% sensitive" does not mean "95% of positives are diseased."
- **Failing to update.** Clinging to the first diagnosis despite contradictory data.
- **Collecting data without a question.** Ordering a panel of tests "to see what turns up" generates noise (Chapter 4).
- **Pretending to certainty.** Patients cope with uncertainty better when you explain it honestly.

---

### 1.11 Key points

- Diagnosis is a working explanation that guides action; it is provisional.
- Experienced clinicians use fast pattern recognition *and* slow analytic reasoning, and switch between them deliberately.
- Illness scripts are built from experience and refined with feedback.
- Probabilities update with evidence: **post-test odds = pre-test odds × LR**.
- Prevalence determines pre-test probability; the same result means different things in different settings.
- Use time, reassessment and safety-netting as diagnostic tools.
- State a leading diagnosis, the dangerous alternatives, and what would change your mind.

### 1.12 Self-assessment

1. A test has sensitivity 80% and specificity 95%. Calculate LR+ and LR−.
   *Answer: LR+ = 0.80 / 0.05 = 16; LR− = 0.20 / 0.95 ≈ 0.21.*

2. A patient's pre-test probability is 20%. A finding with LR+ = 4 is present. What is the post-test probability?
   *Answer: Pre-test odds 0.25; post-test odds 1.0; probability 50%.*

3. Why does a positive screening test in a low-risk population often turn out to be a false positive?
   *Answer: Low prevalence means that even with good specificity the number of false positives can outnumber true positives, giving a low PPV.*

4. Name three situations in which you should deliberately switch from pattern recognition to analytic reasoning.
   *Answer (any three): atypical presentation; first diagnosis doesn't explain all findings; repeated visits; no response to treatment; high-stakes presentation; unusual certainty very early.*

5. In the COPD case (Case 2), which features did *not* fit the working label of exacerbation?
   *Answer: No change in sputum or fever; tachycardia; unilateral calf swelling; recent long-haul flight; desaturation out of proportion.*
