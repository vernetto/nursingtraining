## Chapter 29. Back and Joint Pain

### Learning objectives

After studying this chapter you should be able to:

1. Classify low back pain into non-specific, radicular, and specific (serious) causes, and screen for red flags.
2. Perform a focused back examination, including neurological testing of the lower limbs and straight-leg raise, and recognise cauda equina syndrome and spinal cord compression.
3. Decide when imaging and blood tests are indicated and when they are not, and manage non-specific back pain evidence-based.
4. Distinguish inflammatory from mechanical back pain and recognise axial spondyloarthritis.
5. Take a joint-pain history that separates articular from non-articular pain, inflammatory from mechanical, and monoarticular from polyarticular presentations.
6. Recognise the hot swollen joint as septic arthritis until proven otherwise, and use synovial fluid analysis to differentiate septic, crystal and inflammatory arthritis.
7. Recognise the common patterns of polyarthritis (rheumatoid, osteoarthritis, psoriatic, viral, SLE, polymyalgia rheumatica) and the limping child.

# Part A: Back Pain

### 29.1 The scale of the problem

Back pain is one of the most common reasons for consultation and a leading cause of disability worldwide. About 80% of people experience it at some point. Most episodes are **non-specific (mechanical)** and recover within weeks, but recurrence is common, and a minority become chronic.

The diagnostic task is to **identify the small proportion (<5%) with serious pathology** (cauda equina syndrome, spinal cord compression, infection, malignancy, fracture, aortic aneurysm, and inflammatory spondyloarthritis) while avoiding **unnecessary imaging and medicalisation** of the benign majority. Imaging often shows age-related findings (disc bulges, degeneration) in asymptomatic people, so a "positive" scan is frequently incidental and may lead to unnecessary treatment (Chapter 4).

#### Classification

| Category | Proportion | Description |
|---|---|---|
| **Non-specific (mechanical) low back pain** | ≈ 90% | Pain from muscles, ligaments, facet joints, discs without a specific identifiable pathology; varies with posture and movement |
| **Radicular pain / radiculopathy (sciatica)** | ≈ 5% | Nerve root irritation or compression, usually by disc herniation; leg pain greater than back pain, with dermatomal distribution |
| **Neurogenic claudication (spinal stenosis)** | Common in older adults | Leg pain and heaviness with walking or standing, relieved by sitting or flexing forward |
| **Specific serious causes** | <1–5% | Fracture, infection, malignancy, cauda equina, aneurysm |
| **Inflammatory back pain (axial SpA)** | ≈ 5% (chronic back pain) | Age <40, insidious onset, morning stiffness, improves with exercise |
| **Referred pain** | Variable | From abdominal, pelvic, renal, vascular, or hip pathology |

**By duration:** acute (<6 weeks), subacute (6–12 weeks), chronic (>12 weeks).

### 29.2 History in back pain

#### The pain

- **Onset:** sudden (lifting, trauma: strain, disc, fracture) or insidious (inflammatory, malignancy, infection, degeneration).
- **Location:** central spine, paraspinal, buttock, radiation to the leg (above or below the knee, dermatomal), groin, abdomen.
- **Character:** mechanical (worse with activity and movement, better with rest), inflammatory (worse with rest, better with movement), neurogenic (burning, tingling, numbness), constant unremitting night pain (malignancy, infection).
- **Radiation:** pain to the foot with numbness or tingling in a dermatomal pattern suggests radiculopathy; pain to the buttock and posterior thigh above the knee is common in non-specific back pain.
- **Aggravating factors:** bending forward and sitting (disc), extension and standing/walking (stenosis, facet), coughing and straining (disc, raised pressure), lying flat (malignancy, infection, abdominal aneurysm).
- **Relieving factors:** rest, position change, sitting (stenosis), movement and exercise (inflammatory), NSAIDs (inflammatory back pain responds well).
- **Time pattern:** morning stiffness duration (>30 minutes: inflammatory), night pain, progression.

#### Red flags (screen every patient)

| Red flag | Concern |
|---|---|
| **Saddle anaesthesia (perineal numbness), urinary retention or incontinence, faecal incontinence, bilateral leg weakness or sciatica, sexual dysfunction** | **Cauda equina syndrome** (emergency) |
| **Progressive or severe neurological deficit** (foot drop, leg weakness, gait disturbance), band-like trunk sensory change, upper motor neuron signs | **Spinal cord or nerve root compression** (emergency) |
| **Fever, chills, recent infection, intravenous drug use, immunosuppression, diabetes, recent spinal procedure or surgery, indwelling catheter** | **Spinal infection** (vertebral osteomyelitis, discitis, epidural abscess) |
| **History of cancer, unexplained weight loss, age >50, pain at night or at rest, pain not relieved by rest, failure to improve after 4–6 weeks** | **Malignancy** (metastasis, myeloma, lymphoma, primary tumours) |
| **Significant trauma (or minor trauma in older/osteoporotic patients), prolonged corticosteroid use, osteoporosis, age >70** | **Vertebral fracture** |
| **Age <20 or >55 with new-onset pain** | Increased risk of serious pathology |
| **Inflammatory features: age <40, insidious onset, >3 months, morning stiffness >30 minutes, improved by exercise but not rest, night pain (second half of the night), alternating buttock pain** | **Axial spondyloarthritis** |
| **Abdominal pain, pulsatile abdominal mass, vascular risk factors, pain unrelated to movement** | **Abdominal aortic aneurysm or dissection** |
| **Pain not influenced by movement or position, renal colic features, urinary symptoms** | Renal or genitourinary cause |
| **Pain of thoracic spine, constitutional symptoms, previous IV access** | Infection, malignancy, aortic disease |
| **Severe unremitting pain; structural deformity** | Fracture, tumour |
| **Hypercalcaemia symptoms, anaemia, renal impairment, recurrent infections** | **Myeloma** |

Other history: **psychosocial "yellow flags"** (belief that pain is harmful or severe, fear-avoidance, low mood, catastrophising, work dissatisfaction, compensation claims, poor social support) predict chronicity and should be assessed (STarT Back tool). Also past history (cancer, osteoporosis, previous back problems), medications (steroids, anticoagulants), occupation, activity, previous treatments, patient expectations.

### 29.3 Examination of the back

1. **General:** distress, posture, fever, pallor, weight loss, ability to rise from a chair and walk.
2. **Inspection (standing):** spinal curvature (scoliosis, kyphosis, loss of lumbar lordosis), pelvic tilt, muscle wasting, skin lesions (café-au-lait, hairy patch, dimple: spina bifida occulta; herpes zoster), scars.
3. **Palpation:** spinous process tenderness (vertebral fracture, infection, tumour), paraspinal muscle spasm, sacroiliac joints, sciatic notch, trochanteric bursa.
4. **Movement:** flexion, extension, lateral flexion, rotation. Note pain and restriction. **Schober's test** (modified): two points 10 cm above and 5 cm below the lumbosacral junction; on forward flexion the distance should increase by at least 5 cm (reduced in ankylosing spondylitis).
5. **Gait and walking on heels (L4–L5) and toes (S1)**; Trendelenburg sign (hip abductor weakness: L5).
6. **Neurological examination of the lower limbs (essential if leg symptoms):**

| Root | Motor | Sensory | Reflex |
|---|---|---|---|
| **L2–L3** | Hip flexion | Anterior/medial thigh | – |
| **L4** | Knee extension, ankle dorsiflexion | Medial lower leg | Knee jerk |
| **L5** | Great toe dorsiflexion (extensor hallucis longus), ankle dorsiflexion, hip abduction | Dorsum of foot, lateral lower leg, first web space | – (medial hamstring) |
| **S1** | Plantar flexion, eversion, hamstrings | Lateral foot and sole | Ankle jerk |
| **S2–S4** | Anal sphincter, bladder | Perineum/saddle | Anal reflex, bulbocavernosus |

   - Test tone, power (MRC grading 0–5), sensation (light touch, pinprick, proprioception, vibration), reflexes (knee, ankle, plantar response, clonus).
   - **Upper motor neuron signs** (brisk reflexes, clonus, extensor plantar responses) indicate lesions above the conus (cord compression).
7. **Nerve stretch tests:**
   - **Straight-leg raise (SLR):** supine patient, raise the straight leg; pain reproduced down the leg (not just the back or hamstring tightness) at **30–70°** suggests L5 or S1 radiculopathy; sensitive (about 90%) but not very specific for disc herniation. **Crossed SLR** (raising the unaffected leg reproduces symptoms in the affected leg) is highly specific (about 90%) but less sensitive. **Slump test** is an alternative.
   - **Femoral stretch test** (prone, knee flexion with hip extension): L2–L4 radiculopathy.
8. **Hips:** examine the hip joint (internal rotation, FABER test); hip pathology can mimic back or buttock pain.
9. **Vascular and abdominal examination:** femoral pulses, abdominal aorta for pulsatile mass, abdominal tenderness.
10. **Sacroiliac and other tests:** SI joint compression, distraction, Gaenslen, Patrick (FABER); pain on SI provocation tests suggests SI joint disease (spondyloarthritis or mechanical).
11. **If cauda equina is suspected:** **perianal sensation (S2–S4 saddle area),** **anal tone and voluntary contraction** (rectal examination), **bulbocavernosus reflex,** post-void residual bladder volume (bladder scan).
12. **Examine other systems** if indicated: breast, prostate, thyroid, lung, lymph nodes (metastatic primary).

### 29.4 Specific conditions

#### Non-specific mechanical low back pain

- Back (and buttock) pain varying with movement and posture; no neurological deficit; no red flags; normal general examination.
- **Good prognosis:** most patients improve substantially within 2–6 weeks, though many have recurrences.
- **Management:** reassure, advise to **stay active** and return to normal activities as soon as possible (avoid bed rest), **simple analgesia** (paracetamol has little effect on back pain; **NSAIDs** short-term), heat, **exercise therapy**, manual therapy (spinal manipulation, massage) and **physiotherapy** in selected patients, **psychosocial interventions** for risk of chronicity (cognitive behavioural therapy, pain education), return-to-work planning. **Opioids** are generally not recommended (limited benefit, risk of harm); **muscle relaxants** (short-term) and **antidepressants** (duloxetine for chronic) in selected patients. **Avoid** routine imaging, injections, and surgery for non-specific pain.
- **Safety-netting:** explain that serious causes are rare, and advise to return urgently if leg weakness, numbness, difficulty passing urine, or numbness around the genitals develop.

#### Radiculopathy (sciatica) from disc herniation

- **L5/S1 and L4/L5 discs** (95%); leg pain more than back pain, below the knee, with paraesthesia or numbness in the dermatome, positive SLR or crossed SLR, possible weakness or reflex change.
- **Natural history:** most improve within 6–12 weeks, often the herniation shrinks.
- **Management:** analgesia (NSAIDs; neuropathic agents such as gabapentin or amitriptyline have modest benefit), stay active, physiotherapy, **epidural steroid injection** for short-term relief in selected patients, **surgical discectomy** for severe or persistent radiculopathy (>6–12 weeks) with concordant MRI findings, or **progressive motor deficit,** or **cauda equina syndrome (emergency).**
- **MRI** only if: red flags, progressive neurological deficit, or considering surgery or injection after failed conservative management.

#### Lumbar spinal stenosis

- Older adults; **neurogenic claudication:** bilateral buttock and leg pain, heaviness or weakness with walking or standing, **relieved by sitting or leaning forward** (the "shopping trolley sign"), often with preserved pulses (distinguishing it from vascular claudication, which is relieved by standing still and associated with absent pulses).
- Cause: degenerative narrowing of the central canal and foramina.
- **MRI** confirms; treatment: exercise, physiotherapy, analgesia, epidural injections, surgical decompression for severe symptoms.

#### Cauda equina syndrome (CES)

- **Emergency:** compression of the lumbosacral nerve roots below the conus (usually a large central disc herniation; also tumour, haematoma, abscess, trauma).
- **Features:** low back pain, **bilateral or alternating sciatica,** **saddle (perineal) anaesthesia or paraesthesia,** **bladder dysfunction (difficulty initiating micturition, retention with overflow, loss of sensation of bladder fullness, painless retention),** **bowel dysfunction (faecal incontinence, loss of sensation),** **sexual dysfunction (erectile failure, loss of genital sensation),** **bilateral leg weakness, reduced anal tone,** absent ankle reflexes.
- **Management:** **immediate MRI** (the same day) and urgent neurosurgical decompression; outcome depends on the timing: surgery before bladder involvement (incomplete CES, CES-I) gives much better results than after retention (CES-R).
- **Low threshold:** any patient with back pain and bladder, bowel or saddle symptoms needs urgent assessment (rectal examination, bladder scan), with a high index of suspicion because early symptoms can be subtle.

#### Spinal cord compression (including metastatic)

- **Malignant epidural spinal cord compression (MSCC):** back pain (often thoracic, worse lying down, night pain), progressive leg weakness, sensory level, sphincter dysfunction; known cancer (breast, prostate, lung, myeloma, renal).
- **Emergency:** same-day **whole-spine MRI,** high-dose dexamethasone, urgent oncology/neurosurgery for radiotherapy or surgical decompression.

#### Spinal infection (vertebral osteomyelitis, discitis, epidural abscess)

- **Risk factors:** IV drug use, diabetes, immunosuppression, recent spinal surgery or injection, bacteraemia (endocarditis, urinary infection), indwelling catheters, TB (Pott disease).
- **Features:** back pain (severe, constant, localised tenderness), fever (may be absent), raised ESR/CRP (nearly universal), progressive neurological deficit (epidural abscess: back pain, fever, neurological deficit triad; classic but present in a minority at first presentation).
- **Investigation:** **MRI with gadolinium of the whole spine,** blood cultures (before antibiotics), CT-guided biopsy; ESR/CRP.
- **Treatment:** prolonged antibiotics, surgery for neurological deficit, abscess or instability.

#### Vertebral compression fracture

- Older adults with osteoporosis, steroids, minor trauma; sudden-onset severe back pain, localised spinal tenderness, loss of height, kyphosis.
- X-ray (lateral), MRI if unclear (also to differentiate osteoporotic from malignant fracture); treat pain, bone health (bisphosphonate, calcium, vitamin D), consider vertebroplasty in selected patients, exclude myeloma and metastases when atypical.

#### Malignancy

- **Metastases** (breast, prostate, lung, kidney, thyroid) and **myeloma**: persistent, progressive, non-mechanical pain, nocturnal and unrelieved by rest; weight loss; raised ESR, ALP, calcium; X-ray lytic or sclerotic lesions; bone scan, MRI, CT.
- **Myeloma:** age >50, anaemia, hypercalcaemia, renal impairment, bone pain, infections; **serum protein electrophoresis,** free light chains, urine Bence-Jones protein.

#### Axial spondyloarthritis (including ankylosing spondylitis)

- Young (<45), chronic (>3 months) back pain with **inflammatory features;** associated with HLA-B27 (present in 80–90% of AS), peripheral arthritis, enthesitis (heel pain), dactylitis, uveitis (anterior, acute, painful red eye), psoriasis, IBD.
- **Examination:** reduced lumbar flexion (Schober), chest expansion <2.5 cm, sacroiliac tenderness, occiput-to-wall distance.
- **Investigation:** CRP, HLA-B27, **MRI of sacroiliac joints** (bone marrow oedema in active inflammation, earlier than radiographic changes) and X-ray (sacroiliitis, syndesmophytes).
- **Treatment:** NSAIDs (continuous), exercise/physiotherapy, biologic agents (TNF inhibitors, IL-17 inhibitors) for refractory disease; screen for uveitis.

#### Referred pain and non-spinal causes

- **Abdominal aortic aneurysm:** older man, vascular risk, abdominal pulsation, constant pain unrelated to movement; leak/rupture is an emergency.
- **Aortic dissection:** tearing back pain (Chapter 22).
- **Renal disease:** colic, pyelonephritis (fever, loin tenderness, urinary symptoms).
- **Pancreatitis, peptic ulcer, cholecystitis:** upper back pain.
- **Pelvic and gynaecological:** endometriosis, pelvic inflammatory disease, ectopic pregnancy.
- **Hip osteoarthritis:** groin and buttock pain, restricted internal rotation.
- **Sacroiliac joint dysfunction, piriformis syndrome.**
- **Herpes zoster:** unilateral dermatomal pain before the rash.
- **Psychogenic and functional back pain.**

### 29.5 Investigations for back pain

- **No imaging** for non-specific acute low back pain without red flags: imaging does not improve outcomes, exposes the patient to radiation, and often reveals incidental findings.
- **X-ray** (lumbar spine) has limited value; use in suspected fracture (trauma, osteoporosis, age >70, steroids), spondylolisthesis, deformity, or suspected spondyloarthritis, ideally combined with MRI.
- **MRI** is the investigation of choice for: **suspected cauda equina, cord compression, infection, tumour, or progressive neurological deficit** (urgent); radiculopathy persisting beyond 6 weeks if surgery or injection is considered; suspected axial SpA; unexplained severe pain.
- **CT** when MRI is contraindicated (pacemaker), or for bony anatomy (fractures, bone tumours).
- **Bone scan, PET-CT** for suspected metastases/occult infection.
- **Blood tests:**
  - **ESR, CRP, FBC:** infection, malignancy, inflammatory disease.
  - **Calcium, ALP, phosphate, renal function:** myeloma, metastases, Paget disease, metabolic bone disease.
  - **Serum protein electrophoresis and free light chains, urine Bence-Jones protein:** myeloma.
  - **PSA** (men with suspected prostate metastases), tumour markers where appropriate.
  - **HLA-B27** (selected), **blood cultures** (fever), **vitamin D**, **PTH**, **ferritin**.
- **Bladder scan (post-void residual)** if bladder involvement is suspected.
- **Nerve conduction studies** if the diagnosis of radiculopathy is unclear or neuropathy is suspected.
- **DEXA** for osteoporosis risk assessment.

# Part B: Joint Pain

### 29.6 A structured approach to joint pain

Joint complaints are common and varied. A systematic approach, in the order below, makes the differential manageable.

#### Step 1: Is it really the joint? (articular vs non-articular vs referred)

| Feature | Articular (joint) | Periarticular (tendon, bursa, ligament) | Referred or neuropathic |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Pain on movement** | Both **active and passive** movement painful and restricted in all directions | **Active** movement painful; passive movement painless; pain on specific movements or resisted contraction | Not related to joint movement; neurological features |
| **Swelling** | Diffuse joint swelling (effusion, synovitis), joint-line tenderness | Localised swelling and tenderness | Absent |
| **Examples** | Arthritis | Tendinitis, bursitis, epicondylitis | Radiculopathy, nerve entrapment, referred hip pain to the knee |

#### Step 2: Inflammatory or mechanical?

| Feature | Inflammatory | Mechanical (degenerative) |
|---|---|---|
| **Morning stiffness** | **>30–60 minutes** | <30 minutes |
| **Effect of rest** | Worse (stiffness and "gelling") | Better |
| **Effect of activity** | Better with use | Worse with use |
| **Night pain** | Common (second half of the night) | With movement or position |
| **Swelling** | Soft-tissue swelling, warmth, effusion | Bony enlargement, crepitus, less warmth |
| **Systemic features** | Fatigue, fever, weight loss, anaemia of chronic disease | Rare |
| **Blood tests** | Raised ESR/CRP | Normal |

#### Step 3: How many joints, and which?

- **Monoarthritis** (one joint): infection, crystals, trauma/haemarthrosis, mechanical, early inflammatory arthritis. **Must exclude septic arthritis.**
- **Oligoarthritis** (2–4 joints): reactive arthritis, spondyloarthritis, psoriatic arthritis, gout, juvenile idiopathic arthritis, Lyme disease.
- **Polyarthritis** (≥5 joints): rheumatoid arthritis, SLE, psoriatic arthritis, viral arthritis, osteoarthritis (generalised), crystal arthritis (polyarticular gout), sarcoidosis, vasculitis, paraneoplastic.
- **Pattern:** symmetric small-joint (RA); asymmetric large lower-limb (spondyloarthritis); DIP joints (psoriatic, OA); first MTP (gout); first CMC, DIP, PIP, knees, hips (OA); proximal girdle pain (PMR).

#### Step 4: Extra-articular features

Fever, rash (malar, psoriasis, erythema nodosum, erythema migrans, livedo, purpura), nail changes (pitting, onycholysis), eye disease (red eye: uveitis, scleritis; dry eye), mouth ulcers, Raynaud phenomenon, urethritis or conjunctivitis (reactive arthritis), diarrhoea (IBD, reactive), genital ulcers, lung disease, kidney disease, neurological symptoms, weight loss, drug exposure, tick bites, travel, sexual history, recent infection.

### 29.7 The hot swollen joint: septic arthritis

**Septic arthritis is an emergency:** joint destruction occurs within days, and mortality is substantial (up to 10–15%), especially in older adults and patients with comorbidities.

#### Features

- Acute monoarthritis (knee most common, then hip, shoulder, ankle, wrist), **hot, red, swollen, very painful joint with marked restriction of movement,** inability to bear weight; fever (absent in a significant minority, especially older or immunosuppressed patients).
- **Risk factors:** pre-existing joint disease (RA, OA, **prosthetic joint**), diabetes, immunosuppression, age >80, skin infection, IV drug use, recent joint surgery or injection, bacteraemia, sexually active young adults (gonococcal), sickle cell disease, HIV.
- **Organisms:** *Staphylococcus aureus* (most common), streptococci, Gram-negative bacilli (older, IV drug users), *Neisseria gonorrhoeae* (young sexually active adults: migratory polyarthralgia, tenosynovitis, vesiculopustular skin lesions), *Kingella kingae* (young children), *Borrelia* (Lyme), mycobacteria and fungi (chronic monoarthritis).

#### Management

1. **Aspirate the joint urgently, before antibiotics** (unless the delay is significant in a septic patient), under sterile technique, and send for: **cell count and differential, Gram stain and culture, crystal analysis (polarised light microscopy),** and PCR where available.
2. **Blood cultures** (positive in ~50%), FBC, CRP/ESR, U&E.
3. **Start empirical IV antibiotics** (covering *S. aureus*, including MRSA if risk, plus streptococci and Gram-negatives as per local protocols; add gonococcal cover if relevant) after cultures; adjust per results; usually 2–4 weeks IV then oral (total duration 4–6 weeks for native joints in many protocols).
4. **Drainage:** repeated aspiration or arthroscopic/open washout; urgent orthopaedic involvement, especially for the hip, shoulder, prosthetic joints and children.
5. **Imaging:** X-ray (baseline), ultrasound (effusion, guide aspiration); MRI for osteomyelitis; do not delay treatment for imaging.
6. **Analgesia, rest, joint splinting.**

#### Synovial fluid analysis: interpretation

| Feature | Normal | Non-inflammatory (OA, trauma) | Inflammatory (RA, gout, spondyloarthritis) | Septic |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Appearance** | Clear, pale yellow | Clear, yellow | Cloudy, yellow | Turbid, purulent |
| **Viscosity** | High | High | Low | Variable (low) |
| **WBC count (/µL)** | <200 | 200–2,000 | 2,000–50,000 | **>50,000** (often >100,000; but values of 20,000–50,000 may be seen in early infection or in an infected prosthesis) |
| **Neutrophils (%)** | <25% | <25% | >50% | >75% (often >90%) |
| **Gram stain / culture** | Negative | Negative | Negative | Positive in ~50–70% (Gram stain) |
| **Crystals** | None | None | Gout: needle-shaped, strongly negatively birefringent; CPPD: rhomboid, weakly positive | May coexist with crystals |

**Crystals do not exclude infection:** septic arthritis and gout or pseudogout can coexist. A high WBC count in a patient with known crystal arthritis still needs culture and antibiotics until infection is excluded.

#### Prosthetic joint infection

- Early (<3 months), delayed (3–24 months), late (>24 months). Pain, swelling, fever, wound drainage or sinus tract, loosening on X-ray. Synovial fluid WBC thresholds are lower (>1,100–3,000/µL and >60–70% neutrophils, depending on the timing). Management by orthopaedic and infectious disease specialists (debridement, implant retention or exchange, prolonged antibiotics).

### 29.8 Crystal arthritis

#### Gout

- **Acute monoarthritis** (first metatarsophalangeal joint in ~50%; also ankle, knee, midfoot, wrist, fingers), with onset over hours (often at night), intense pain (the bedclothes cannot be tolerated), erythema, warmth, swelling, and sometimes fever; resolves in 1–2 weeks without treatment.
- **Risk factors:** male sex, older age, post-menopausal women, hyperuricaemia, chronic kidney disease, obesity, alcohol (beer, spirits), purine-rich diet, fructose, diuretics (thiazides, loop), low-dose aspirin, ciclosporin, pyrazinamide, metabolic syndrome, hypertension, family history; triggers: acute illness, surgery, dehydration, starting urate-lowering drugs.
- **Diagnosis:** **joint aspiration with demonstration of monosodium urate crystals** (needle-shaped, negative birefringence under polarised light) is the gold standard. **Serum urate may be normal or low during an acute attack** (acute-phase decrease), so a normal urate does not exclude gout; check between attacks. Dual-energy CT and ultrasound (double-contour sign) are helpful.
- **Chronic tophaceous gout:** tophi (ears, olecranon bursae, fingers, Achilles), erosions on X-ray ("punched-out" with overhanging edges), urate nephropathy and stones.
- **Acute treatment:** NSAIDs (full dose, if no contraindication), colchicine (low-dose regimen, adjust for renal function and interacting drugs), or **corticosteroids** (oral, intra-articular, or intramuscular); IL-1 inhibitors in refractory cases. Treat **early.**
- **Long-term:** urate-lowering therapy (allopurinol first-line, titrated to serum urate target <360 µmol/L (6 mg/dL), or <300 µmol/L (5 mg/dL) in tophaceous disease; febuxostat; benzbromarone, pegloticase) started in patients with recurrent attacks, tophi, CKD, or stones; **prophylaxis** against flares (low-dose colchicine or NSAID for 3–6 months) when starting urate-lowering treatment; lifestyle and treat comorbidities.

#### Calcium pyrophosphate deposition disease (CPPD, pseudogout)

- Acute monoarthritis (knee, wrist most common) in older adults, often with **chondrocalcinosis** on X-ray; may be associated with haemochromatosis, hyperparathyroidism, hypomagnesaemia, hypophosphatasia, or post-surgery.
- Synovial fluid: **rhomboid or rod-shaped, weakly positively birefringent crystals.**
- Treatment: NSAIDs, colchicine, intra-articular or oral steroids; screen for secondary causes if young.

### 29.9 Other causes of acute monoarthritis

- **Traumatic haemarthrosis:** bloody effusion after injury; fracture, ligament rupture (cruciate), meniscal injury. In a patient on anticoagulants or with a bleeding disorder (haemophilia), spontaneous haemarthrosis.
- **Reactive arthritis:** asymmetric oligoarthritis of lower-limb joints 1–4 weeks after urogenital (*Chlamydia*) or gastrointestinal (*Salmonella*, *Shigella*, *Campylobacter*, *Yersinia*) infection; may have urethritis, conjunctivitis, enthesitis, mouth ulcers, circinate balanitis, keratoderma blennorrhagicum; HLA-B27 association. Treat with NSAIDs, steroid injections, and sulfasalazine if persistent.
- **Lyme arthritis:** intermittent or persistent monoarthritis (knee) after tick exposure, in endemic areas; erythema migrans history, positive serology.
- **Osteoarthritis flare:** hot but modest effusion; crystals may coexist.
- **Early rheumatoid or psoriatic arthritis** presenting as monoarthritis.
- **Haemoglobinopathy, avascular necrosis, tumours (pigmented villonodular synovitis), foreign-body synovitis.**

### 29.10 Polyarthritis and chronic joint pain

#### Osteoarthritis (OA)

- The commonest joint disease; **age >45**, pain with use, relieved by rest, short morning stiffness (<30 minutes), bony enlargement, crepitus, reduced range of movement, variable swelling.
- **Distribution:** DIP joints (Heberden's nodes), PIP joints (Bouchard's nodes), **first carpometacarpal joint** (squaring of the thumb base), knees, hips, first MTP joint, cervical and lumbar spine. **Wrist, MCP joints and elbows are usually spared** (their involvement should prompt consideration of haemochromatosis, CPPD, or other causes).
- **Risk factors:** age, obesity, previous injury, occupational loading, genetics, joint deformity.
- **Diagnosis:** clinical in people ≥45 with typical features; X-ray (joint space narrowing, osteophytes, subchondral sclerosis and cysts) is not required for diagnosis and correlates poorly with symptoms.
- **Management:** education, weight loss, **exercise** (strengthening and aerobic), physiotherapy, footwear and walking aids, **topical NSAIDs** (knee), oral NSAIDs (shortest effective duration, caution), paracetamol (limited benefit), intra-articular steroid injection (short-term relief), duloxetine; **joint replacement** for severe disease. Avoid opioids and glucosamine/chondroitin (weak evidence).

#### Rheumatoid arthritis (RA)

- **Symmetric polyarthritis** of small joints (MCP, PIP, wrists, MTP) with **morning stiffness >1 hour,** fatigue, swelling (boggy synovitis), squeeze-test tenderness across the MCP/MTP joints; elbows, shoulders, knees, ankles; spares DIP joints and thoracolumbar spine; onset over weeks to months; female predominance (3:1), age 30–60.
- **Extra-articular:** rheumatoid nodules, scleritis/episcleritis, interstitial lung disease, pleural effusions, pericarditis, vasculitis, Felty syndrome (splenomegaly and neutropenia), anaemia of chronic disease, amyloidosis, osteoporosis, cardiovascular disease.
- **Investigations:** **anti-CCP antibodies** (highly specific, ~95%), **rheumatoid factor** (less specific; present in other conditions), ESR/CRP, FBC, X-ray of hands and feet (erosions develop within the first 1–2 years), **ultrasound/MRI** for early synovitis.
- **Classification:** ACR/EULAR 2010 criteria (joint involvement, serology, acute-phase reactants, duration).
- **Management:** **early referral** (window of opportunity); **disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs (DMARDs)**, methotrexate first-line, with folic acid, plus hydroxychloroquine, sulfasalazine, leflunomide; short-term glucocorticoids for flares; biologic (TNF, IL-6, abatacept, rituximab) and targeted synthetic (JAK inhibitors) agents for inadequate response; treat to target (remission); manage cardiovascular risk, bone health, screening for infections.

#### Psoriatic arthritis

- Psoriasis (skin or nail changes: pitting, onycholysis) with asymmetric oligoarthritis, **DIP involvement,** dactylitis ("sausage digit"), enthesitis, spondylitis, arthritis mutilans; may precede skin disease.
- Treat with NSAIDs, DMARDs (methotrexate, leflunomide), biologics.

#### Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE)

- Young women: arthralgia and arthritis (non-erosive), malar rash, photosensitivity, oral ulcers, serositis, renal disease (proteinuria, haematuria), neuropsychiatric disease, cytopenias, Raynaud phenomenon.
- **Tests:** ANA (sensitive), anti-dsDNA, anti-Sm, complement (C3, C4), antiphospholipid antibodies, urinalysis, FBC.

#### Viral and other infectious arthritis

- **Parvovirus B19** (symmetric small-joint polyarthritis in adults, often with a rash in children: "slapped cheek"), **hepatitis B and C**, **rubella**, **chikungunya** (severe acute polyarthralgia with fever and rash, can persist for months), **dengue**, **Ross River virus**, **HIV**, **EBV**, **mumps**, **Lyme disease**, **post-streptococcal reactive arthritis** and **acute rheumatic fever** (migratory polyarthritis, carditis, chorea, erythema marginatum, subcutaneous nodules; Jones criteria; preceding sore throat).
- Usually self-limiting; confirm with serology/PCR; exclude septic arthritis in monoarticular presentation.

#### Polymyalgia rheumatica (PMR)

- Age >50 (rarely <50), **bilateral shoulder and hip girdle pain and stiffness** (difficulty raising the arms, combing hair, rising from a chair or bed), **morning stiffness >45 minutes,** raised ESR/CRP, malaise, low-grade fever, weight loss; rapid response to low-dose prednisolone (12.5–25 mg daily; relief within days).
- **Associated with giant cell arteritis** in ~15–20% (screen for headache, scalp tenderness, jaw claudication, visual symptoms).
- Differential: late-onset RA, inflammatory myopathy, hypothyroidism, statin myopathy, malignancy, osteoarthritis, fibromyalgia.
- Management: gradual steroid taper over 1–2 years or more; monitor for relapse, osteoporosis prophylaxis; methotrexate or tocilizumab as steroid-sparing agents.

#### Fibromyalgia

- Chronic widespread pain (>3 months), fatigue, unrefreshing sleep, cognitive difficulties ("fibro-fog"), non-articular tenderness at multiple sites; normal examination of joints, normal inflammatory markers and normal X-rays; often associated with IBS, headaches, depression, anxiety; middle-aged women.
- **Diagnosis:** clinical (e.g. the 2016 ACR criteria: widespread pain index and symptom severity scale), after exclusion of other conditions (hypothyroidism, vitamin D deficiency, PMR, inflammatory arthritis, sleep apnoea).
- **Management:** education, graded **aerobic exercise,** sleep hygiene, CBT, pain-modulating drugs (low-dose amitriptyline, duloxetine, pregabalin); avoid opioids and unneeded investigations.

#### Other

- **Vasculitis** (granulomatosis with polyangiitis, polyarteritis nodosa, IgA vasculitis), **sarcoidosis** (ankle arthritis with erythema nodosum: Löfgren syndrome), **haemochromatosis arthropathy** (2nd and 3rd MCP joints, hook osteophytes), **paraneoplastic** rheumatic syndromes, **hypothyroidism**, **hyperparathyroidism**, **hypermobility syndromes** (Beighton score), **drug-induced arthralgia** (statins, aromatase inhibitors, quinolones, immune checkpoint inhibitors).
- **Still disease (adult onset):** high spiking fevers, evanescent salmon-coloured rash, arthritis, sore throat, hyperferritinaemia.

### 29.11 The limping child and paediatric joint pain

A child who limps or refuses to walk needs careful assessment; the causes vary by age.

| Age | Common/important causes |
|---|---|
| **Toddler (1–3 years)** | Septic arthritis, osteomyelitis, toddler's fracture, transient synovitis, juvenile idiopathic arthritis, non-accidental injury |
| **Child (3–10 years)** | **Transient synovitis of the hip** (most common; follows viral illness; self-limiting), **Perthes disease** (avascular necrosis of the femoral head; age 4–8, insidious limp, knee or hip pain), septic arthritis/osteomyelitis, JIA, leukaemia, trauma |
| **Adolescent (>10 years)** | **Slipped capital femoral epiphysis** (SCFE: obese adolescent, hip or knee pain, limited internal rotation, externally rotated gait, emergency), overuse injuries (Osgood–Schlatter), JIA, trauma |

**Distinguishing septic arthritis from transient synovitis (hip):** the **Kocher criteria** (non-weight-bearing on the affected side, ESR >40 mm/h, fever >38.5 °C, WBC >12 × 10⁹/L) give predicted probabilities of septic arthritis rising from about 3% with one criterion to more than 90% with all four; the criteria need local validation. An irritable hip with fever and inability to bear weight needs urgent ultrasound, blood tests, and aspiration if septic arthritis is possible.

**Always** examine the hip in a child with knee pain (referred pain), the spine, and consider non-accidental injury, leukaemia (night pain, anaemia, bruising, hepatosplenomegaly, bone pain), and malignant bone tumours (Ewing sarcoma, osteosarcoma).

**Juvenile idiopathic arthritis:** arthritis lasting ≥6 weeks in a child <16 years, after exclusion of other causes; subtypes include oligoarticular (risk of chronic anterior uveitis: ophthalmological screening), polyarticular, systemic (fever, rash, serositis), enthesitis-related.

### 29.12 Investigations for joint pain

| Test | Use |
|---|---|
| **FBC, ESR, CRP** | Inflammation, infection; anaemia of chronic disease; neutropenia (Felty) |
| **Serum urate** | Gout (may be normal during an attack); check between attacks |
| **Rheumatoid factor, anti-CCP** | Rheumatoid arthritis (anti-CCP more specific) |
| **ANA, anti-dsDNA, ENA, complement** | SLE, connective tissue disease |
| **HLA-B27** | Spondyloarthritis (supportive, not diagnostic) |
| **ASO, throat swab** | Post-streptococcal arthritis, rheumatic fever |
| **Serology** (Lyme, parvovirus, hepatitis, HIV, chikungunya), **chlamydia/gonorrhoea PCR** | Infective and reactive arthritis |
| **Blood cultures** | Septic arthritis |
| **Joint aspiration and synovial fluid analysis** | Cell count, Gram stain, culture, crystals; essential in monoarthritis |
| **Plain X-ray** | Early disease may be normal; later: erosions (RA), joint space narrowing, osteophytes (OA), chondrocalcinosis (CPPD), punched-out erosions (gout), periostitis, sacroiliitis |
| **Ultrasound** | Effusion, synovitis, erosions, tophi, double contour sign, guide aspiration |
| **MRI** | Early inflammatory disease, osteomyelitis, avascular necrosis, spondyloarthritis, cartilage and ligament lesions |
| **Bone scan** | Multifocal pathology, infection, malignancy |
| **Calcium, phosphate, ALP, TSH, vitamin D, ferritin/transferrin saturation** | Metabolic and endocrine mimics, haemochromatosis |
| **Urinalysis** | Renal involvement (SLE, vasculitis) |

**Interpreting autoantibodies:** ANA is sensitive but not specific (low titres in 10–30% of healthy people, especially older); rheumatoid factor is positive in infections, other autoimmune diseases, and healthy elderly. **Do not order "arthritis screens" in undifferentiated joint pain**; order tests guided by the pre-test probability from the history and examination (Chapter 4).

### 29.13 Worked cases

#### Case 1: Non-specific low back pain

*A 38-year-old office worker develops low back pain after gardening, with pain across the lower back and buttock that varies with movement and eases with walking. No leg symptoms, no neurological deficit, no red flags, afebrile. Examination: paraspinal tenderness and limited flexion; SLR negative.*

**Reasoning.** **Non-specific mechanical low back pain;** no imaging needed.

**Action.** Explain the benign course, advise to stay active, short-course NSAID, heat, simple exercises, work adjustments, safety-net advice (leg weakness, numbness, bladder or bowel symptoms), review in 2 weeks if not improving; discuss psychosocial risk factors.

#### Case 2: Cauda equina syndrome

*A 44-year-old man has had severe low back pain with sciatica on both sides for 2 days, and since this morning cannot pass urine properly and has numbness when wiping himself. Examination: reduced perianal sensation, reduced anal tone, absent ankle jerks, mild bilateral foot weakness. Bladder scan: 800 mL retention.*

**Reasoning.** Bilateral sciatica, saddle anaesthesia, urinary retention, reduced anal tone: **cauda equina syndrome** (CES-R).

**Action.** **Emergency MRI** and immediate neurosurgical referral for decompression; catheterise; document the neurological state; the sooner, the better the chance of bladder recovery.

#### Case 3: Epidural abscess

*A 52-year-old man with poorly controlled diabetes has severe thoracolumbar back pain for 5 days, fever 38.6 °C, and since yesterday weakness in both legs. Spine percussion tenderness. WBC 19, CRP 220. Blood cultures later grow* S. aureus.

**Reasoning.** Back pain, fever, neurological deficit and risk factor: **spinal epidural abscess.**

**Action.** Blood cultures, **urgent MRI of the whole spine with contrast,** IV antibiotics (including MRSA cover), neurosurgical decompression and drainage if neurological deficit; look for the source (endocarditis, skin, urinary).

#### Case 4: Back pain and cancer

*A 66-year-old man has had 3 months of progressive thoracic back pain, worse at night and lying flat, with 7 kg weight loss and new urinary hesitancy. Percussion tenderness at T8, brisk leg reflexes, mild leg weakness. PSA is very high.*

**Reasoning.** Progressive night pain, weight loss, upper motor neuron signs: **metastatic spinal cord compression** from prostate cancer.

**Action.** Same-day whole-spine MRI, high-dose dexamethasone, urgent oncology and neurosurgical input for radiotherapy or surgical decompression, androgen deprivation therapy, analgesia.

#### Case 5: Inflammatory back pain

*A 27-year-old man has had 9 months of insidious low back and buttock pain, worse at night and in the early morning with stiffness for 90 minutes, relieved by exercise and by ibuprofen but not by rest. He had a painful red eye last year. Chest expansion is 2 cm; Schober test is reduced; sacroiliac joints are tender. CRP is raised.*

**Reasoning.** Inflammatory back pain criteria met, plus anterior uveitis: **axial spondyloarthritis (ankylosing spondylitis).** MRI of the sacroiliac joints shows bone marrow oedema; HLA-B27 positive.

**Action.** Rheumatology referral; regular NSAIDs, exercise programme, smoking cessation; TNF or IL-17 inhibitor if inadequate response; monitor for uveitis, IBD and cardiovascular risk.

#### Case 6: Hot knee

*A 72-year-old woman with rheumatoid arthritis on methotrexate has had a hot, swollen, painful right knee for 2 days, and feels unwell with fever 38.2 °C. She can barely bear weight. Aspiration: turbid fluid, WBC 85,000/µL, 94% neutrophils; Gram stain shows Gram-positive cocci in clusters. No crystals.*

**Reasoning.** Acute monoarthritis with fever, immunosuppression and a typical synovial fluid picture: **septic arthritis** (*S. aureus*).

**Action.** Blood cultures, IV antibiotics (flucloxacillin or per local protocol; add vancomycin if MRSA risk), orthopaedic washout, hold methotrexate, analgesia, monitor for sepsis and bone involvement.

#### Case 7: Gout

*A 58-year-old man with hypertension on a thiazide, after a weekend of beer and red meat, wakes at 3 a.m. with severe pain, redness and swelling in the first MTP joint of the right foot; he cannot bear the weight of a sheet. Temperature 37.6 °C. Serum urate is 480 µmol/L. Aspiration of the joint shows needle-shaped negatively birefringent crystals; no organisms.*

**Reasoning.** Classic **acute gout.**

**Action.** NSAID (e.g. naproxen) or colchicine low-dose or prednisolone short course; avoid starting allopurinol during the acute flare (start after resolution, with prophylaxis); lifestyle, review thiazide, consider losartan; arrange urate-lowering therapy if recurrent attacks, CKD, or tophi.

#### Case 8: Rheumatoid arthritis

*A 45-year-old woman has 3 months of symmetric swelling and pain in MCP and PIP joints and wrists, with morning stiffness for 2 hours, fatigue, and difficulty opening jars. Squeeze test positive across MCPs. ESR 52, CRP 40. Anti-CCP and rheumatoid factor positive. X-rays: periarticular osteopenia, no erosions yet.*

**Reasoning.** Early **rheumatoid arthritis,** seropositive, high risk of erosive disease.

**Action.** Urgent rheumatology referral; start methotrexate (with folic acid) plus short-term glucocorticoid bridging within weeks; treat to target; screen for hepatitis B/C, TB, vaccinate; assess cardiovascular risk.

#### Case 9: Polymyalgia rheumatica

*A 74-year-old woman has had 4 weeks of aching and stiffness around both shoulders and hips, worse in the morning (90 minutes), difficulty combing her hair and rising from chairs. No headache or visual symptoms. Muscle strength is normal but movement is painful. ESR 78, CRP 45. CK normal.*

**Reasoning.** **Polymyalgia rheumatica.**

**Action.** Prednisolone 15 mg daily with dramatic response in days, slow taper over 12–24 months, screen for GCA at each visit, bone protection and glucose monitoring; reconsider the diagnosis if response is incomplete.

#### Case 10: The limping child

*A 7-year-old boy has 3 days of a limp and right knee pain after a viral illness, mild fever 37.8 °C, and can bear weight with discomfort. Hip movement is limited in internal rotation; ESR 20, WBC 9; ultrasound shows a small hip effusion.*

**Reasoning.** **Transient synovitis of the hip** (Kocher score 0–1; no fever >38.5, weight-bearing, ESR <40, normal WBC).

**Action.** Rest, NSAIDs, review within 24–48 hours; if fever, non-weight-bearing, rising inflammatory markers or no improvement: urgent aspiration for septic arthritis; consider Perthes disease if persistent (follow-up X-ray after 4–6 weeks).

#### Case 11: Widespread pain

*A 41-year-old woman has had 2 years of widespread aching pain, fatigue, poor sleep and low mood. Examination shows widespread tenderness but no synovitis; ESR, CRP, TSH, vitamin D, CK, and FBC are normal.*

**Reasoning.** **Fibromyalgia** (after exclusion of other conditions).

**Action.** Positive diagnosis with explanation, graded aerobic exercise, sleep hygiene, CBT, consider duloxetine or amitriptyline, avoid opioids, set realistic goals and follow-up.

### 29.14 Common pitfalls

- **Ordering MRI for non-specific back pain** and acting on incidental findings.
- **Missing red flags** or failing to ask about bladder, bowel and saddle sensation.
- **Not examining the legs neurologically** or the abdomen and hips in back pain.
- **Failing to perform a rectal examination** when cauda equina is suspected; relying on bladder symptoms alone.
- **Delaying MRI** in suspected cauda equina, infection or cord compression.
- **Prescribing prolonged bed rest or opioids** for non-specific back pain.
- **Missing axial spondyloarthritis** in a young person because the X-ray is normal.
- **Starting antibiotics before aspirating** a septic joint (when feasible), or failing to aspirate a hot joint.
- **Excluding infection because crystals are seen.**
- **Relying on a normal serum urate** to rule out gout during an attack.
- **Ordering an "autoimmune screen"** for non-specific joint pain.
- **Missing PMR/GCA** in older patients with girdle pain and headache.
- **Missing septic arthritis in children and immunosuppressed adults** (fever may be absent).
- **Missing SCFE** in an obese adolescent with knee pain.
- **Failing to treat inflammatory arthritis early** (the window of opportunity).
- **Labelling widespread pain as fibromyalgia** without a basic exclusion work-up.

### 29.15 Key points

- Most back pain is non-specific and self-limiting; screen for red flags (cauda equina, cord compression, infection, malignancy, fracture, aneurysm, axial SpA) in every patient.
- Do not image non-specific back pain; use MRI promptly for suspected cauda equina, cord compression, infection, or progressive deficit.
- Cauda equina syndrome is a surgical emergency: saddle anaesthesia, bladder and bowel dysfunction, bilateral leg symptoms; do a rectal examination and bladder scan.
- Inflammatory back pain (young, insidious, morning stiffness, improves with exercise) suggests axial spondyloarthritis.
- In joint pain, decide: articular or not, inflammatory or mechanical, mono- or polyarticular, and look for extra-articular clues.
- A hot swollen joint is septic arthritis until proven otherwise: aspirate (cell count, Gram stain, culture, crystals), cultures, IV antibiotics, drainage.
- Crystals do not exclude infection; urate may be normal in acute gout.
- Early referral and DMARDs for suspected RA; PMR responds dramatically to low-dose steroids; screen for GCA.
- In children, differentiate septic arthritis from transient synovitis (Kocher criteria), and remember SCFE and Perthes.

### 29.16 Self-assessment

1. List the features of cauda equina syndrome and the investigation and timing required.
   *Answer: Back pain, bilateral sciatica, saddle anaesthesia, urinary retention/incontinence, faecal incontinence, sexual dysfunction, bilateral leg weakness, reduced anal tone; emergency MRI the same day and urgent decompression.*

2. How do inflammatory and mechanical back pain differ?
   *Answer: Inflammatory: age <40, insidious onset, >3 months, morning stiffness >30 minutes, improvement with exercise not rest, night pain; mechanical: worse with activity, better with rest, short stiffness.*

3. What does synovial fluid WBC >50,000/µL with 95% neutrophils suggest, and what are the immediate steps?
   *Answer: Septic arthritis; Gram stain and culture, blood cultures, IV antibiotics, urgent drainage/washout and orthopaedic review.*

4. Why may serum urate be normal during an acute gout attack, and how is gout diagnosed?
   *Answer: Urate may fall during acute inflammation; diagnosis is by identifying needle-shaped, negatively birefringent monosodium urate crystals in synovial fluid.*

5. Which joints are typically spared in osteoarthritis, and what does involvement suggest?
   *Answer: Wrists, MCP joints, elbows, shoulders are usually spared; involvement suggests CPPD, haemochromatosis, inflammatory arthritis or another secondary cause.*

6. State the Kocher criteria and their use.
   *Answer: Non-weight-bearing, ESR >40 mm/h, fever >38.5 °C, WBC >12 × 10⁹/L; the number present predicts the probability of septic arthritis in the child with an irritable hip.*
