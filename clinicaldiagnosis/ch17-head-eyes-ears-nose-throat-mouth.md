## Chapter 17. Head, Eyes, Ears, Nose, Throat and Mouth

### Learning objectives

After studying this chapter you should be able to:

1. Examine the head, scalp, face and neck, including the thyroid gland and salivary glands.
2. Assess visual acuity, visual fields, pupils and eye movements, and perform fundoscopy.
3. Differentiate the causes of a red eye and recognise the sight-threatening conditions.
4. Examine the ear with an otoscope, and use the Rinne and Weber tests to separate conductive from sensorineural hearing loss.
5. Examine the nose, oral cavity, tonsils and pharynx.
6. Recognise the emergencies of this region: sudden visual loss, acute angle-closure glaucoma, giant cell arteritis, orbital cellulitis, airway obstruction, epiglottitis, peritonsillar abscess, deep neck infection, epistaxis.
7. Recognise the signs of thyroid disease on examination.

---

### 17.1 Why this region matters

The head and neck contain the organs of sight, hearing, smell, taste, speech and swallowing, and also the entry to the airway and gastrointestinal tract. Simple examination yields diagnostic information about many systemic conditions: anaemia (conjunctivae), jaundice (sclerae), thyroid and endocrine disease (face, eyes, neck), hypertension and diabetes (retina), raised intracranial pressure (disc swelling), vasculitis (temporal arteries), and malignancy (nodes, oral lesions). Several conditions can cause **permanent loss of vision, hearing or airway** within hours if missed.

Equipment: a **pen torch**, **ophthalmoscope**, **otoscope**, **tongue depressors**, a **Snellen chart** (or smartphone-based equivalent), a **tuning fork** (512 Hz for hearing; 128 Hz for neurological tests), gloves, and a good light.

---

### 17.2 The head, face and scalp

#### Inspection

- **Shape and symmetry** of the head and face; swelling; deformity.
- **Skin:** rashes (malar, seborrhoeic), scars, lesions (basal cell carcinoma on sun-exposed skin), herpes zoster in the ophthalmic division (vesicles on the forehead and nose tip: **Hutchinson sign** suggests ocular involvement).
- **Hair:** distribution, loss (patchy: alopecia areata; diffuse: telogen effluvium, thyroid disease, iron deficiency; scarring alopecia).
- **Facial expression and movement:** asymmetry (facial nerve palsy), ptosis, mask-like face, myopathic face.
- **Facies of systemic disease:** see Chapter 10 (acromegaly, Cushing, thyroid, SLE, etc).

#### Palpation

- **Scalp and skull:** tenderness, lumps, depressions (fracture), Battle sign (bruising behind the ear: basal skull fracture).
- **Temporal arteries:** prominent, tender, nodular, pulseless in **giant cell arteritis (GCA).** Ask about new headache, scalp tenderness, jaw claudication, visual symptoms, constitutional symptoms and proximal muscle stiffness. A normal temporal artery examination does not exclude GCA.
- **Sinuses:** frontal and maxillary tenderness on percussion (limited value).
- **Temporomandibular joint:** palpate in front of the tragus as the patient opens and closes the mouth; clicking, deviation, crepitus, tenderness.
- **Parotid and submandibular glands:** swelling, tenderness, stones (palpable in the floor of the mouth), mass.

### 17.3 The eyes

#### History essentials

Loss of vision (**sudden vs gradual; one eye vs both; painful vs painless; complete vs partial**), flashes or floaters, a curtain or shadow, double vision, pain, redness, discharge, photophobia, halos around lights, headache, trauma, contact lens use, previous eye disease, family history (glaucoma), systemic illness (diabetes, hypertension, autoimmune disease), medications.

#### External inspection

- **Lids and lashes:** ptosis, lid retraction (thyroid eye disease), swelling, erythema, styes (hordeolum), chalazion, blepharitis, ectropion/entropion, xanthelasma.
- **Position and prominence:** **proptosis (exophthalmos)** from thyroid eye disease (most common), orbital tumour, cavernous sinus disease, orbital cellulitis. Look from above and behind.
- **Conjunctiva and sclera:** pallor (lower lid), jaundice (sclera), redness, subconjunctival haemorrhage (benign, often after coughing or spontaneous; with trauma consider more serious injury), chemosis (swelling), discharge.
- **Cornea:** clarity, lesions, arcus senilis (benign over 60; abnormal before 45), ulcers (use fluorescein and a blue light).
- **Iris, pupil, lens:** cataract (cloudy lens), shape and symmetry of the pupils, hyphaema or hypopyon (blood or pus in the anterior chamber).

#### Visual acuity

Test each eye separately with the patient's glasses or a pinhole, using a **Snellen chart at 6 metres.** Record the smallest line read (e.g. 6/9: the patient reads at 6 m what a normal eye reads at 9 m). If the patient cannot read the top letter, test counting fingers, hand movements, and perception of light. **A new reduction in acuity not corrected by pinhole implies pathology** (not simple refractive error). Visual acuity is a vital sign for the eye: always record it in any eye complaint.

#### Visual fields

Confrontation testing: sit facing the patient at arm's length, cover one eye each, and compare the patient's field with your own, using a finger or a red pin in each of the four quadrants (Chapter 15). Look for central scotoma (macular disease, optic neuritis), hemianopia, quadrantanopia.

| Defect | Site of lesion |
|---|---|
| Monocular blindness | Eye, optic nerve |
| Bitemporal hemianopia | Optic chiasm (pituitary tumour, craniopharyngioma, meningioma) |
| Homonymous hemianopia | Contralateral optic tract, radiation, occipital cortex (stroke, tumour) |
| Superior quadrantanopia ("pie in the sky") | Temporal lobe (Meyer loop) |
| Inferior quadrantanopia | Parietal lobe |
| Homonymous hemianopia with macular sparing | Occipital cortex (posterior cerebral artery stroke) |
| Central scotoma | Macular disease, optic neuritis |
| Tunnel vision | Glaucoma, retinitis pigmentosa |

#### Pupils

- Size, shape, symmetry; **direct and consensual light reflex**; the **swinging torch test** for a **relative afferent pupillary defect (RAPD, Marcus Gunn pupil):** when the light swings from the normal eye to the affected one, the pupils dilate rather than constrict (optic nerve disease: optic neuritis, ischaemic optic neuropathy, severe retinal disease).
- **Accommodation reflex:** constriction on focusing on a near object.
- **Abnormal pupils:**

| Finding | Causes |
|---|---|
| **Dilated, fixed pupil** with ptosis and eye "down and out" | Third nerve palsy (compression: posterior communicating artery aneurysm, uncal herniation: **emergency**) |
| **Small pupil, ptosis, anhidrosis** (Horner syndrome) | Sympathetic lesion: Pancoast tumour, carotid dissection, brainstem stroke (lateral medullary), cluster headache |
| **Bilateral small pinpoint pupils** | Opioids, pontine haemorrhage, organophosphates |
| **Bilateral dilated pupils** | Sympathomimetics (cocaine, amphetamines), anticholinergics, hypoxic brain injury, deep coma |
| **Argyll Robertson pupils** | Small, irregular, accommodate but do not react to light (neurosyphilis; also diabetes) |
| **Holmes–Adie pupil** | Tonically dilated, slow reaction, absent knee jerks |
| **Irregular pupil** | Trauma, iritis (posterior synechiae), surgery |
| **Mid-dilated fixed oval pupil** with a painful red eye | **Acute angle-closure glaucoma** |

#### Eye movements

Ask the patient to follow a target in an "H" pattern without moving the head; look for restricted movement, nystagmus, diplopia (Chapter 15 for nerve palsies). **Thyroid eye disease** causes restrictive ophthalmopathy (typically limited upgaze and abduction).

#### Fundoscopy (ophthalmoscopy)

Darken the room; dilate pupils only when safe (not in suspected angle closure or when neurological pupil monitoring is needed). Use your right eye for the patient's right eye. Find the **red reflex** (absent in cataract, vitreous haemorrhage, retinoblastoma in children), then follow a vessel to the **optic disc**.

Assess systematically:

| Structure | Normal | Abnormal findings |
|---|---|---|
| **Optic disc** | Pink, sharp margins, cup:disc ratio ~0.3 | **Papilloedema** (swollen disc: blurred margins, obscured vessels, loss of spontaneous venous pulsation, haemorrhages) in raised ICP; optic atrophy (pale disc); cupping (glaucoma); optic neuritis; new vessels |
| **Vessels** | Arteries narrower than veins (2:3); smooth | Narrowing, "silver wiring," AV nipping (hypertension); venous beading, tortuosity |
| **Retina (background)** | Even orange-red | Haemorrhages (dot and blot, flame-shaped), microaneurysms, hard exudates, cotton-wool spots, drusen, pigmentation, retinal detachment, new vessel formation |
| **Macula** | Dark, with central foveal reflex, 2 disc diameters temporal to the disc | Drusen, haemorrhage, oedema (age-related macular degeneration, diabetes) |

- **Diabetic retinopathy:** background (microaneurysms, dot-blot haemorrhages, hard exudates), pre-proliferative (cotton-wool spots, venous beading), **proliferative (neovascularisation, vitreous haemorrhage),** maculopathy. Screening is part of diabetic care.
- **Hypertensive retinopathy:** arteriolar narrowing, AV nipping, flame haemorrhages, cotton-wool spots, hard exudates; **papilloedema** in malignant hypertension.
- **Roth spots** (retinal haemorrhages with a pale centre): infective endocarditis.
- **Retinal artery occlusion:** pale retina with a "cherry-red" macular spot; **retinal vein occlusion:** "blood and thunder" haemorrhages.

#### The red eye

The key is to identify the **sight-threatening** causes by asking: **Is vision reduced? Is there pain? Is there photophobia? Is the pupil abnormal? Is the cornea clear? Is the redness diffuse or around the cornea (ciliary flush)?**

| Condition | Pain | Vision | Pupil | Discharge | Other clues |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Conjunctivitis (viral)** | Gritty, mild | Normal | Normal | Watery | Diffuse redness, often bilateral, preauricular node, URTI |
| **Conjunctivitis (bacterial)** | Gritty | Normal | Normal | Purulent, lids stuck on waking | Diffuse redness |
| **Allergic conjunctivitis** | Itch | Normal | Normal | Watery | Chemosis, atopy, seasonal |
| **Subconjunctival haemorrhage** | None | Normal | Normal | None | Bright red patch, sharply defined |
| **Episcleritis** | Mild | Normal | Normal | None | Sectoral redness, blanches with phenylephrine |
| **Scleritis** | Severe, boring, wakes at night | May be reduced | Normal | None | Deep violaceous redness; association with rheumatoid, vasculitis |
| **Keratitis / corneal ulcer / abrasion** | Moderate–severe, foreign-body sensation | Reduced | Normal or small | Watery/purulent | Fluorescein staining; contact lens wearers (risk of Pseudomonas and Acanthamoeba); HSV dendritic ulcer |
| **Anterior uveitis (iritis)** | Moderate, photophobia | Reduced | Small, irregular | None | Ciliary flush, cells in the anterior chamber, hypopyon; HLA-B27 diseases, sarcoid, IBD |
| **Acute angle-closure glaucoma** | **Severe, with headache, nausea, vomiting** | **Markedly reduced, halos around lights** | **Mid-dilated, fixed, oval** | None | Hazy ("steamy") cornea, hard eye, red; older, hypermetropic patients; dark room or dilating drops as triggers |
| **Endophthalmitis** | Severe | Markedly reduced | – | Hypopyon | After surgery or trauma |
| **Orbital cellulitis** | Pain on eye movement | May be reduced | RAPD late | – | Proptosis, restricted movements, fever; urgent imaging and IV antibiotics |

**Red flags requiring same-day ophthalmology:** reduced visual acuity, severe pain, photophobia, corneal opacity, pupil abnormality, history of trauma (especially penetrating or chemical), contact lens wearer with pain, hypopyon, proptosis or ophthalmoplegia, recent intraocular surgery. **Chemical injury: irrigate immediately before further assessment.**

#### Sudden visual loss

An emergency. Ask: one eye or both? Painless or painful? Associated features?

| Cause | Features |
|---|---|
| **Central retinal artery occlusion** | Painless sudden loss, pale retina with cherry-red spot, RAPD; **embolic source search (carotid, heart); GCA must be excluded** |
| **Giant cell arteritis (arteritic anterior ischaemic optic neuropathy)** | Age >50, headache, scalp tenderness, jaw claudication, PMR, high ESR/CRP; pale swollen disc; **urgent high-dose steroids** to protect the other eye |
| **Retinal detachment** | Flashes, new floaters, a shadow or "curtain" moving across the vision; surgical emergency |
| **Vitreous haemorrhage** | Sudden "cobwebs" or red haze; diabetes |
| **Central retinal vein occlusion** | Painless, variable loss, "blood and thunder" fundus |
| **Acute angle-closure glaucoma** | Pain, halos, red eye, hazy cornea |
| **Optic neuritis** | Young adult, pain on eye movement, reduced colour vision, RAPD; multiple sclerosis association |
| **Transient monocular vision loss (amaurosis fugax)** | "Curtain" descending over one eye, lasting minutes; carotid or cardiac embolism; treat as TIA |
| **Stroke (occipital)** | Homonymous hemianopia; other neurological signs |
| **Migraine aura** | Positive visual phenomena (zig-zag, scintillating), spreads over 20–30 minutes |

#### Double vision (diplopia)

- **Monocular** (persists when one eye is covered): refractive, cataract, corneal problem.
- **Binocular** (resolves on covering either eye): ocular motility problem; cranial nerve III, IV, VI palsies, myasthenia gravis, thyroid eye disease, internuclear ophthalmoplegia (multiple sclerosis), brainstem stroke, orbital disease.
- **New binocular diplopia with headache, pupil involvement, or ptosis** (third nerve palsy) is an emergency.

### 17.4 The ears

#### History

Hearing loss (unilateral vs bilateral, sudden vs gradual), tinnitus, vertigo, otalgia, otorrhoea, itching, previous ear disease or surgery, noise exposure, drugs (aminoglycosides, loop diuretics, cisplatin, aspirin in high doses), family history.

#### Inspection and palpation

- **Pinna:** deformity, haematoma ("cauliflower ear"), skin lesions (squamous cell carcinoma, basal cell carcinoma), tophi, keloid.
- **Surrounding:** scars (postauricular), swelling over the mastoid (mastoiditis: protrusion of the pinna, tenderness, erythema; especially in children).
- **Palpate:** tragus (pain on pressing the tragus or pulling the pinna suggests otitis externa), mastoid tenderness, pre-auricular and post-auricular lymph nodes.

#### Otoscopy

1. Explain; hold the otoscope like a pen, steadying your hand against the patient's face.
2. **Straighten the canal:** pull the pinna up and back (adults; down and back in young children).
3. Insert the speculum gently into the outer third of the canal.
4. Look at the **canal** (wax, foreign body, swelling, erythema, discharge, polyp, exostoses) and the **tympanic membrane (TM).**

| Normal TM | Pearly grey, translucent, with a cone of light at 5 o'clock (right ear) or 7 o'clock (left), the handle of malleus visible, mobile on pneumatic otoscopy |
|---|---|

| Finding | Interpretation |
|---|---|
| **Red, bulging TM, loss of landmarks, ± perforation with otorrhoea** | **Acute otitis media** |
| **Dull, retracted TM, air bubbles or amber fluid level** | **Otitis media with effusion (glue ear)** |
| **Swollen, erythematous canal, discharge, painful tragal movement** | **Otitis externa** |
| **White chalky patches (tympanosclerosis)** | Old scarring |
| **Perforation** | Trauma, infection; central (safe) or marginal/attic (unsafe, cholesteatoma) |
| **Attic retraction pocket with white keratin debris** | **Cholesteatoma** (destructive, requires ENT referral) |
| **Wax (cerumen)** | Common cause of conductive hearing loss |
| **Foreign body** | Children (insects, beads); live insect: drown with oil before removal |
| **Blood behind the TM; Battle sign; CSF otorrhoea** | **Basal skull fracture** |
| **Blue/red tympanic mass** | Glomus tumour |
| **Vesicles in the ear, facial palsy** | **Ramsay Hunt syndrome (herpes zoster oticus)** |
| **Bullae on the TM** | Bullous myringitis |

#### Hearing tests (tuning fork, 512 Hz)

- **Rinne test:** strike the fork and hold it against the mastoid process (bone conduction, BC), then place it beside the ear canal (air conduction, AC). Ask which sounds louder. Normally **AC > BC (Rinne positive)**. In **conductive** hearing loss, **BC > AC (Rinne negative)** on the affected side. In sensorineural loss, AC > BC (but both reduced).
- **Weber test:** place the vibrating fork on the vertex or midline of the forehead; ask where it is heard. Normally heard centrally. In **conductive loss**, it lateralises to the **affected (deaf) ear.** In **sensorineural loss**, it lateralises to the **normal (better) ear.**

| | Rinne | Weber |
|---|---|---|
| **Normal** | AC > BC both sides | Midline |
| **Conductive loss (R ear)** | BC > AC on right | Louder in the right ear |
| **Sensorineural loss (R ear)** | AC > BC on right (reduced) | Louder in the left ear |

Use **whispered voice** at 60 cm for a crude screening of hearing, formal **pure-tone audiometry** and tympanometry for confirmation.

#### Patterns of hearing loss

| Type | Causes |
|---|---|
| **Conductive** | Wax, otitis externa, otitis media with effusion, perforation, otosclerosis, ossicular disruption, cholesteatoma, tumour |
| **Sensorineural** | Presbycusis (age-related), noise-induced, ototoxic drugs, Ménière disease, acoustic neuroma (vestibular schwannoma), meningitis, viral labyrinthitis, congenital, trauma |
| **Sudden sensorineural hearing loss** (over <72 hours) | **Otologic emergency:** urgent audiometry and steroids (within days); exclude retrocochlear lesions (MRI) |
| **Unilateral sensorineural or asymmetric loss with tinnitus** | **Vestibular schwannoma** until excluded (MRI) |

**Vertigo** is discussed in Chapters 15 and 24. Dix–Hallpike manoeuvre diagnoses benign paroxysmal positional vertigo; **HINTS** examination (head impulse, nystagmus, test of skew) distinguishes peripheral from central causes in acute continuous vertigo.

### 17.5 The nose and sinuses

#### Inspection and palpation

- External deformity, swelling, erythema, scars, alar collapse.
- **Patency:** occlude each nostril in turn and ask the patient to breathe in.
- **Anterior rhinoscopy** (otoscope with a large speculum or nasal speculum, with the head tilted back): mucosal colour (pink normal; pale boggy in allergic rhinitis; erythematous in infection), **septum** (deviation, perforation, septal haematoma after trauma: boggy, needs urgent drainage), **turbinates** (swollen), **polyps** (pale, grape-like, mobile: asthma, aspirin sensitivity, cystic fibrosis, chronic rhinosinusitis), discharge (clear, mucopurulent, bloody), crusting, foreign body (unilateral purulent foul-smelling discharge in a child), ulcers (granulomatosis with polyangiitis, cocaine, tuberculosis, syphilis).
- **Sinuses:** tenderness over frontal and maxillary sinuses.

#### Epistaxis

Most arise from **Little's area (Kiesselbach plexus)** on the anterior septum. Management: sit upright leaning forward, **pinch the soft part of the nose for 10–15 minutes**, ice, then cautery or packing. Consider coagulopathy, anticoagulants, hypertension, trauma, tumour, hereditary haemorrhagic telangiectasia, and posterior bleeds (profuse, passing into the throat). **Unilateral nasal obstruction with bloodstained discharge in an adult** warrants investigation for tumour.

**Red flags:** unilateral nasal obstruction, bloody discharge, facial swelling or numbness, diplopia (sinonasal malignancy); periorbital swelling and visual change with sinusitis (orbital complication); persistent clear rhinorrhoea after head injury (CSF leak: halo sign); septal haematoma.

### 17.6 The mouth and throat

#### Preparation

Wear gloves, use a good light and tongue depressors, and explain. Remove dentures.

#### Systematic examination

1. **Lips:** colour, ulcers, cracks (angular cheilitis: iron, B vitamin deficiency; Candida), swelling (angioedema), herpes labialis, perioral pigmentation (Peutz–Jeghers), telangiectasia (hereditary haemorrhagic telangiectasia), cancer (lower lip).
2. **Buccal mucosa and gums:** hydration, pallor, ulcers (aphthous; Crohn disease; Behçet; carcinoma), white plaques (Candida: can be scraped off; leukoplakia: cannot be scraped, premalignant; lichen planus: lacy pattern), gum hypertrophy (phenytoin, leukaemia), bleeding gums (vitamin C deficiency, thrombocytopenia), gingivitis, periodontal disease, abscess, **Koplik spots** (measles), pigmentation (Addison disease).
3. **Teeth:** caries, loss, abscess.
4. **Tongue:** inspect the dorsum, sides and underside. Look for: **smooth, red tongue** (glossitis: B12, folate, iron deficiency), **coating** (smoking, dehydration, infection), **hairy leukoplakia** (lateral borders: HIV, EBV), **fasciculations** (motor neurone disease), **deviation** on protrusion (hypoglossal nerve palsy: toward the weak side), **macroglossia** (acromegaly, amyloid, hypothyroidism), **ulcers** (squamous cell carcinoma: persistent painless ulcer, induration, lateral border), **geographic tongue** (benign).
5. **Floor of the mouth:** salivary stones, swelling, **Ludwig's angina** (bilateral submandibular and sublingual cellulitis, raised tongue, trismus, drooling, airway risk: emergency).
6. **Palate:** colour, perforation, high arched (Marfan), cleft, petechiae, torus palatinus (benign), uvula position (deviates away from a peritonsillar abscess), palatal movement ("ah"; unilateral weakness: vagus nerve).
7. **Tonsils and pharynx:** press the tongue and ask the patient to say "ah." Note size (grade 0–4), erythema, exudate, ulcers, asymmetry, pseudomembrane (diphtheria: grey adherent), vesicles (herpangina, hand-foot-mouth disease), petechiae on the palate (infectious mononucleosis, streptococcal pharyngitis).

#### Sore throat: patterns

| Diagnosis | Features |
|---|---|
| **Viral pharyngitis** | Gradual onset, cough, rhinorrhoea, hoarseness, conjunctivitis; most common |
| **Group A streptococcal pharyngitis** | Sudden onset, fever, tonsillar exudate, tender anterior cervical nodes, no cough; scored using **Centor/McIsaac** criteria (Chapter 70) |
| **Infectious mononucleosis (EBV)** | Adolescents, severe sore throat, marked tonsillar exudate, posterior cervical and generalised lymphadenopathy, splenomegaly, fatigue |
| **Peritonsillar abscess (quinsy)** | Severe unilateral pain, trismus, "hot potato" voice, uvula deviated, unilateral tonsillar bulge; needs drainage |
| **Retropharyngeal or parapharyngeal abscess** | Child, fever, neck stiffness, drooling, stridor; CT; emergency |
| **Epiglottitis** | Rapid onset, severe sore throat with minimal throat findings, drooling, muffled voice, stridor, tripod posture; **do not examine the throat; secure airway** (ENT, anaesthetist) |
| **Diphtheria** | Grey membrane, bull neck, travel/unvaccinated |
| **Candidiasis** | Inhaled steroids, antibiotics, immunosuppression, HIV |
| **Gonococcal pharyngitis, syphilis, HIV seroconversion** | Sexual history |
| **Malignancy** | Persistent unilateral sore throat, otalgia, dysphagia, neck node, weight loss, smoking and alcohol |

#### Hoarseness

Persistent hoarseness for more than 3 weeks in an adult (especially smoker or heavy drinker) requires **laryngoscopy** to exclude laryngeal cancer. Other causes: viral laryngitis, voice misuse, vocal cord nodules or polyps, reflux, hypothyroidism, recurrent laryngeal nerve palsy (lung cancer, thyroid surgery, aortic aneurysm).

#### Stridor and airway

**Stridor** (harsh inspiratory noise) signals upper airway obstruction and is an emergency: call senior help, position the patient comfortably, give high-flow oxygen, avoid agitating the patient, and prepare for airway intervention. Causes: foreign body, croup, epiglottitis, anaphylaxis, angioedema, tumour, trauma, retropharyngeal abscess, vocal cord palsy.

### 17.7 The neck and thyroid gland

#### Inspection

Scars (thyroidectomy: collar incision), swelling, visible pulsation, lymph nodes, skin changes, posture.

Ask the patient to **swallow a sip of water** and watch the neck: the thyroid and thyroglossal cysts move upward with swallowing; lymph nodes do not. Ask the patient to **protrude the tongue**: a thyroglossal cyst moves up.

#### Palpation of the thyroid

Stand **behind** the patient with fingers over the gland (below the cricoid cartilage), neck slightly flexed. Ask the patient to swallow. Assess: size, shape, consistency, nodularity, tenderness, mobility, retrosternal extension (cannot get below it).

| Finding | Possible meaning |
|---|---|
| **Diffuse smooth enlargement** | Graves' disease, Hashimoto thyroiditis, iodine deficiency (endemic goitre), physiological goitre |
| **Multinodular goitre** | Common, benign; may be toxic |
| **Solitary nodule** | Cyst, adenoma, carcinoma (hard, fixed, with hoarseness or nodes), thyroiditis |
| **Tender thyroid** | Subacute (de Quervain) thyroiditis |
| **Hard, fixed, irregular, with nodes or vocal cord palsy** | Thyroid cancer; anaplastic carcinoma |
| **Retrosternal extension** | Positive **Pemberton sign** (raising both arms causes facial congestion, stridor, venous distension) |

**Auscultate** for a bruit (Graves' disease: increased vascularity).

#### Assessing thyroid status

| | Hyperthyroidism | Hypothyroidism |
|---|---|---|
| **General** | Weight loss despite appetite, heat intolerance, anxiety, irritability | Weight gain, cold intolerance, lethargy, depression |
| **Pulse** | Tachycardia, atrial fibrillation, bounding | Bradycardia |
| **Skin** | Warm, moist, fine hair, palmar erythema, pretibial myxoedema (Graves' disease), onycholysis | Dry, cold, coarse, pale, yellowish; hair loss; peaches-and-cream puffiness |
| **Hands** | Fine tremor, sweaty palms, acropachy | Carpal tunnel syndrome, cool dry hands |
| **Eyes** | Lid retraction, **lid lag** on following a downward-moving finger, **exophthalmos, chemosis, ophthalmoplegia** (Graves' ophthalmopathy specifically) | Periorbital puffiness, loss of lateral third of the eyebrows |
| **Neuromuscular** | Proximal myopathy, **brisk reflexes** | **Slow-relaxing reflexes**, ataxia, deafness |
| **Voice** | Normal | Hoarse, deep |
| **Heart** | Systolic flow murmur, heart failure (late) | Pericardial effusion, bradycardia |
| **Mood** | Anxious, restless | Depressed, slow thinking; myxoedema psychosis (rare) |

**Thyroid storm** and **myxoedema coma** are emergencies (Chapter 48).

#### Salivary glands and other neck masses

- **Parotid:** in front of and below the ear; swelling from mumps, bacterial parotitis, stone, Sjögren syndrome, sarcoid, tumour (a hard, fixed mass with facial nerve weakness suggests malignancy).
- **Submandibular:** bimanual palpation with one finger in the floor of the mouth; stones, sialadenitis, tumour.
- **Lymph nodes:** see Chapter 10 (supraclavicular nodes: thoracic or abdominal malignancy; persistent hard nodes: head and neck cancer, lymphoma; tender nodes: infection; TB).
- **Midline neck mass:** thyroglossal cyst (moves with tongue protrusion), thyroid nodule, dermoid cyst.
- **Lateral neck mass:** branchial cyst (anterior to sternocleidomastoid, upper third), cystic hygroma, carotid body tumour (pulsatile, moves side to side but not up and down), lymph node.

**Head and neck cancer red flags:** unexplained neck lump persisting >3 weeks in an adult; persistent painless oral ulcer or white/red patch (>3 weeks); unexplained hoarseness; persistent unilateral sore throat or otalgia; dysphagia; unexplained oral bleeding; unilateral nasal blockage; risk factors (tobacco, alcohol, HPV, betel nut).

### 17.8 Worked cases

#### Case 1: A painful red eye in an elderly woman

*A 72-year-old woman wakes with a severe right eye ache, headache, nausea and vomiting. She sees halos around lights. She had been to the cinema the previous evening. The right eye is red, the cornea is hazy ("steamy"), the pupil is mid-dilated, oval and fixed, and the eye feels hard on gentle palpation through the lid. Vision: counting fingers.*

**Reasoning.** Pain, halos, nausea, hazy cornea, fixed mid-dilated oval pupil, raised intraocular pressure: **acute angle-closure glaucoma.** It occurs in older hypermetropic eyes with shallow anterior chambers; dim light or dilating drops can trigger it. Irreversible optic nerve damage may occur within hours.

**Action.** Emergency ophthalmology referral; lying supine, IV acetazolamide, topical beta-blocker, pilocarpine (once the pressure begins to fall), analgesia and antiemetic; definitive laser iridotomy. Avoid mydriatic drugs.

#### Case 2: Sudden visual loss and headache

*A 76-year-old woman has had a new right temporal headache for 3 weeks, jaw ache when chewing, and morning stiffness in her shoulders, with a recent weight loss. This morning she noticed painless loss of vision in her right eye. Examination: right temporal artery thickened and tender, pulseless. Right pupil: RAPD. Fundus: pale swollen optic disc.*

**Reasoning.** Age >50, new headache, jaw claudication, polymyalgia symptoms, tender temporal artery and visual loss with a pale swollen disc: **giant cell arteritis with arteritic anterior ischaemic optic neuropathy.** The other eye is at high risk within days.

**Action.** Same-day **high-dose corticosteroids** (e.g. IV methylprednisolone or oral prednisolone according to local protocol) *without waiting for biopsy,* urgent ESR/CRP and platelet count, and temporal artery biopsy or ultrasound within days, with ophthalmology and rheumatology referral. Add gastric and bone protection.

#### Case 3: Flashes and a curtain

*A 58-year-old high myope has had flashes of light and many new floaters for 3 days, followed this morning by a dark shadow in the upper-outer part of the vision of his left eye, "like a curtain."*

**Reasoning.** Flashes (vitreous traction), new floaters and a progressive field defect: **rhegmatogenous retinal detachment.** The field defect is opposite to the location of the detachment (superior-temporal shadow indicates inferior-nasal detachment). Risk factors: myopia, previous cataract surgery, trauma, family history.

**Action.** Same-day ophthalmology referral; avoid delay, since macular detachment reduces the outcome. Surgery (laser retinopexy, pneumatic retinopexy, vitrectomy or scleral buckle).

#### Case 4: Conductive or sensorineural?

*A 45-year-old man has noticed muffled hearing in his left ear for 3 weeks after a cold. He has no pain or vertigo. Otoscopy: the left TM is dull and retracted, with a visible amber air–fluid level. Rinne: BC > AC in the left ear. Weber: lateralises to the left.*

**Reasoning.** Conductive hearing loss on the left (negative Rinne on the left, Weber to the affected ear). Otoscopy points to **otitis media with effusion** following an upper respiratory tract infection (eustachian tube dysfunction). **A unilateral effusion in an adult** deserves a look at the **postnasal space** (nasopharyngeal carcinoma) if it persists beyond 6 to 8 weeks, particularly in people of southern Chinese or North African descent and in smokers.

**Management.** Watchful waiting, decongestants and autoinflation; audiology and ENT review if persistent.

#### Case 5: A sore throat that is not "just a sore throat"

*A 22-year-old man has had 4 days of worsening right-sided sore throat, now unable to open his mouth fully and with a muffled "hot potato" voice, drooling, and fever of 39 °C. The right tonsil is swollen and displaced medially, the uvula is pushed to the left, and there is tender right-sided cervical lymphadenopathy.*

**Reasoning.** Unilateral severe pain, trismus, muffled voice, uvular deviation: **peritonsillar abscess (quinsy).** Complications: airway obstruction, extension into the parapharyngeal space, sepsis.

**Action.** Assess the airway, IV fluids, analgesia, IV antibiotics, ENT referral for needle aspiration or incision and drainage; consider CT if there is suspicion of deep neck extension or if the airway is compromised.

#### Case 6: A goitre and tremor

*A 32-year-old woman has lost 7 kg over 3 months despite a good appetite, has palpitations, sweating, and hand tremor. She has lid retraction, lid lag, mild proptosis, a smooth diffuse goitre with an audible bruit, a pulse of 112 (regular), fine tremor, brisk reflexes, and non-pitting swelling over the shins.*

**Reasoning.** Thyrotoxic symptoms and signs with a diffuse goitre with bruit, **ophthalmopathy and pretibial myxoedema:** **Graves' disease.** Eye signs (proptosis, restricted movement) and pretibial myxoedema are specific to Graves' disease and do not occur in other causes of thyrotoxicosis.

**Next steps.** TSH, free T4, free T3, TSH-receptor antibodies; ECG for AF; treatment with beta-blocker for symptom control and antithyroid drugs (or radioiodine or surgery); ophthalmology review for eye disease; counsel on smoking cessation (worsens eye disease) and contraception (pregnancy implications).

### 17.9 Common pitfalls

- **Not recording visual acuity** in any eye complaint.
- **Treating a "red eye" with steroid drops** before excluding herpetic keratitis (steroids worsen dendritic ulcers).
- **Giving mydriatic drops** to a patient with a shallow anterior chamber (angle closure).
- **Missing giant cell arteritis** because the ESR is "only" moderately raised or the temporal arteries are normal.
- **Delaying steroids for biopsy** in suspected GCA.
- **Mistaking floaters and flashes for a harmless finding** without a dilated retinal examination.
- **Looking at the throat of a child with suspected epiglottitis.**
- **Not checking the airway** in deep neck infection and Ludwig angina.
- **Assuming unilateral hearing loss** is wax without otoscopy and tuning fork tests, or ignoring asymmetric sensorineural hearing loss (vestibular schwannoma).
- **Overlooking persistent hoarseness** (more than 3 weeks) or a unilateral neck lump in an adult smoker.
- **Missing a septal haematoma** after nasal trauma.
- **Performing fundoscopy without dilatation** when not needed, or dilating when neurological pupil assessment is needed.
- **Forgetting the dental and oral cancer screen.**

---

### 17.10 Key points

- Record visual acuity first in any eye problem; check pupils (RAPD), fields, movements and fundus.
- A red eye with reduced vision, pain, photophobia or an abnormal pupil needs urgent ophthalmic assessment; acute angle-closure glaucoma and corneal ulcers are sight-threatening.
- Sudden visual loss is an emergency: retinal detachment, central retinal artery occlusion, giant cell arteritis, vitreous haemorrhage, angle closure.
- Use Rinne and Weber to separate conductive from sensorineural loss; investigate asymmetric or sudden sensorineural loss urgently.
- Examine the mouth and throat systematically; do not examine the throat of a child with suspected epiglottitis.
- Examine the thyroid from behind during swallowing, and look for eye and skin signs to characterise thyroid status.
- Persistent hoarseness, a persistent neck lump, or a non-healing oral ulcer requires specialist assessment for malignancy.

### 17.11 Self-assessment

1. Describe the Rinne and Weber findings in a right conductive hearing loss.
   *Answer: Rinne negative on the right (BC > AC); Weber lateralises to the right (affected) ear.*

2. List the features of acute angle-closure glaucoma on examination.
   *Answer: Painful red eye, hazy cornea, mid-dilated fixed oval pupil, hard eye, reduced vision, halos, nausea and vomiting.*

3. A 70-year-old has sudden painless vision loss in one eye, a new temporal headache and jaw pain. What is the diagnosis, and what should you do before biopsy?
   *Answer: Giant cell arteritis; start high-dose corticosteroids immediately and arrange urgent ESR/CRP and biopsy or ultrasound.*

4. Which eye signs are specific to Graves' disease, as opposed to thyrotoxicosis in general?
   *Answer: Proptosis (exophthalmos), ophthalmoplegia, chemosis (and pretibial myxoedema, thyroid acropachy); lid lag and lid retraction occur in any thyrotoxicosis.*

5. What does a relative afferent pupillary defect indicate?
   *Answer: Optic nerve or severe retinal disease on the affected side (the pupils dilate when the light swings from the normal to the affected eye).*

6. A 55-year-old smoker has had hoarseness for 6 weeks. What is the next step?
   *Answer: Laryngoscopy (urgent ENT referral) to exclude laryngeal cancer.*
