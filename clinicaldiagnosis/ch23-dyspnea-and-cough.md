## Chapter 23. Dyspnea and Cough

### Learning objectives

After studying this chapter you should be able to:

1. Define dyspnea, characterise it with the right questions, and grade its severity.
2. Build a differential for acute, subacute and chronic dyspnea using pathophysiological and anatomical frameworks.
3. Recognise the breathless patient who needs immediate resuscitation and the features of respiratory failure.
4. Discriminate cardiac from pulmonary from other causes of dyspnea using history, examination and first-line tests (oximetry, ECG, chest X-ray, natriuretic peptides, spirometry, D-dimer).
5. Classify cough by duration and character, and recognise the common and dangerous causes of acute, subacute and chronic cough.
6. Apply a stepwise approach to chronic cough, including the "big three" (upper airway cough syndrome, asthma, reflux) and the red flags for lung cancer, TB and interstitial disease.
7. Recognise haemoptysis and manage it by severity and cause.

---

### 23.1 Dyspnea: what it is

**Dyspnea** (breathlessness, shortness of breath) is the subjective experience of uncomfortable breathing. It is a **symptom**, not a sign: a patient may report severe dyspnea with normal oxygen levels (anxiety, anaemia, deconditioning), and a patient may have profound hypoxaemia with little dyspnea (carbon monoxide poisoning, silent hypoxia). It is distinct from **tachypnoea** (a fast respiratory rate), **hyperpnoea** (increased depth), and **hypoxia** (low tissue oxygen).

Dyspnea arises from a mismatch between the **respiratory drive** (which responds to chemoreceptors, mechanoreceptors and cortical signals) and the **mechanical response** of the respiratory system. Three broad mechanisms:

- **Increased work of breathing** (airway obstruction, stiff lungs, chest wall restriction, respiratory muscle weakness).
- **Increased ventilatory drive** (hypoxia, acidosis, anaemia, pulmonary receptor stimulation, anxiety, pregnancy, drugs such as salicylate).
- **Impaired gas exchange or circulation** (V/Q mismatch, shunt, diffusion limitation, low cardiac output).

#### Why a careful approach is needed

Dyspnea is common, and the differential is wide. A few causes (heart failure, COPD, asthma, pneumonia, anaemia, deconditioning, obesity) account for most cases. But several life-threatening conditions (PE, tension pneumothorax, acute coronary syndrome, anaphylaxis, severe asthma, upper airway obstruction, cardiac tamponade, severe sepsis) present with dyspnea and require immediate action. About a third of patients with chronic dyspnea have more than one contributing cause (for example, heart failure and COPD and obesity), so one explanation does not close the case.

### 23.2 The unstable breathless patient

Before taking a detailed history, assess the patient with **ABCDE** (Chapter 37). Features that mandate immediate resuscitation:

- **Airway:** stridor, drooling, inability to speak, swollen face or tongue, tracheal tug.
- **Breathing:** respiratory rate >30 or <8, SpO₂ <90% despite oxygen, use of accessory muscles, paradoxical movement, cyanosis, silent chest, new unilateral absent breath sounds with hypotension (tension pneumothorax).
- **Circulation:** hypotension, tachycardia >130, cool mottled peripheries, raised JVP, signs of tamponade.
- **Disability:** drowsiness, confusion, agitation, flapping tremor (CO₂ narcosis), glucose abnormalities.
- **Exposure:** rash and urticaria (anaphylaxis), fever, sepsis, injuries.

Give oxygen to hypoxaemic patients (with a lower target of 88–92% for those at risk of hypercapnic failure), get IV access, monitor, and treat the cause while diagnosing it. Obtain a **bedside ECG, glucose, SpO₂, blood gas, and chest X-ray** as soon as practicable. Use bedside ultrasound (lung and heart) if available (Chapter 67).

### 23.3 Characterising dyspnea: the history

#### Time course

| Onset | Typical causes |
|---|---|
| **Seconds to minutes** | Pulmonary embolism, pneumothorax, anaphylaxis, foreign-body aspiration, acute pulmonary oedema (flash), arrhythmia, acute valvular rupture, tamponade, acute aortic regurgitation |
| **Hours to days** | Asthma exacerbation, pneumonia, COPD exacerbation, heart failure decompensation, pleural effusion, ARDS, metabolic acidosis (DKA), anaemia from acute bleeding, pericardial effusion |
| **Weeks to months** | Interstitial lung disease, malignancy (lung, pleura), chronic heart failure, anaemia, pulmonary hypertension, thyroid disease, neuromuscular disease, heart valve disease |
| **Years** | COPD, obesity, deconditioning, interstitial fibrosis, chronic thromboembolic disease, kyphoscoliosis |
| **Intermittent / variable** | Asthma, angina equivalent, paroxysmal arrhythmia, vocal cord dysfunction, panic attacks, hypersensitivity pneumonitis |

#### Exertional pattern and severity

Quantify using a standard scale such as the **modified Medical Research Council (mMRC) dyspnoea scale:**

| Grade | Description |
|---|---|
| 0 | Breathless only with strenuous exercise |
| 1 | Breathless when hurrying on the level or walking up a slight hill |
| 2 | Walks slower than people of the same age because of breathlessness, or has to stop for breath when walking at own pace on the level |
| 3 | Stops for breath after walking about 100 metres or after a few minutes on the level |
| 4 | Too breathless to leave the house, or breathless when dressing |

For heart failure, use the **NYHA class** (I–IV). Ask for concrete examples: "How many stairs can you climb? How far can you walk before stopping, and what makes you stop (breath, legs, chest pain)?"

#### Specific questions that discriminate

| Question | What it suggests |
|---|---|
| **Orthopnoea** (breathless lying flat; number of pillows) | Heart failure (also massive obesity, diaphragmatic paralysis, severe COPD, ascites) |
| **Paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnoea** | Heart failure |
| **Platypnoea** (worse upright, better lying) | Hepatopulmonary syndrome, intracardiac shunt (patent foramen ovale), pulmonary AV malformations |
| **Trepopnoea** (worse lying on one side) | Unilateral lung disease, effusion, heart failure with large ventricle |
| **Wheeze, nocturnal cough, triggers (cold air, allergens, exercise, aspirin)** | Asthma |
| **Productive cough, smoking, winter exacerbations** | COPD / chronic bronchitis |
| **Sudden onset with pleuritic pain, leg swelling, recent immobilisation** | Pulmonary embolism |
| **Sudden sharp unilateral pain, tall thin young man** | Pneumothorax |
| **Chest pain with exertion** | Angina, aortic stenosis, pulmonary hypertension |
| **Ankle swelling, weight gain** | Heart failure, nephrotic syndrome, cirrhosis |
| **Fever, rigors, productive cough** | Pneumonia |
| **Haemoptysis, weight loss** | Lung cancer, TB, bronchiectasis, PE |
| **Dry cough, progressive exertional dyspnoea, clubbing** | Interstitial lung disease |
| **Pallor, fatigue, heavy periods or bleeding** | Anaemia |
| **Palpitations** | Arrhythmia (AF), anxiety, thyrotoxicosis |
| **Tingling around the mouth and fingers, sighing breathing, relation to stress** | Hyperventilation, panic |
| **Wheeze/stridor after eating, stings or drugs; hives** | Anaphylaxis |
| **Hoarseness, noisy breathing, dysphagia** | Upper airway obstruction (goitre, tumour, foreign body, vocal cord paralysis) |
| **Weakness, double vision, drooping eyelids, variability through the day** | Myasthenia gravis, neuromuscular respiratory failure |
| **Tinnitus, vomiting, hyperventilation after aspirin overdose** | Salicylate poisoning |

#### Background and exposures

- **Smoking history** (pack-years), occupational history (asbestos, silica, coal, isocyanates, flour, wood dust, farming), pets and birds, mould exposure, environmental dusts, hobbies.
- **Medications:** amiodarone, methotrexate, nitrofurantoin, bleomycin (lung toxicity); beta-blockers (bronchospasm, fatigue); NSAIDs and aspirin (asthma); ACE inhibitors (cough); chemotherapy (cardiotoxicity).
- **Past history:** heart disease, hypertension, diabetes, thromboembolism, malignancy, radiotherapy to the chest, connective tissue disease, TB, HIV.
- **Family history:** asthma, atopy, alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency, cystic fibrosis, pulmonary fibrosis, thrombophilia, cardiomyopathy.
- **Travel and exposure to infection:** TB, histoplasmosis, COVID-19, influenza.
- **Pregnancy:** physiological dyspnea of pregnancy; consider PE, peripartum cardiomyopathy.

### 23.4 Examination of the breathless patient

Combine the systems examinations (Chapters 12–13).

- **General:** posture (tripod, orthopnoeic), speech (sentences, words), colour, cyanosis, pallor, sweating, anxiety, distress, obesity.
- **Hands:** clubbing, tar staining, tremor, flap, peripheral cyanosis, temperature, rheumatoid hands, sclerodactyly.
- **Vital signs:** RR (count for a full minute), HR and rhythm, BP, SpO₂ (at rest and on exertion), temperature, peak flow.
- **Neck:** JVP, tracheal position, lymph nodes, accessory muscle use.
- **Chest:** inspection, expansion, percussion, auscultation (crackles, wheeze, bronchial breathing, reduced air entry, pleural rub).
- **Heart:** apex position, murmurs, gallop rhythms, loud P₂, RV heave.
- **Abdomen:** hepatomegaly, ascites.
- **Legs:** oedema, calf swelling or tenderness, varicose veins.
- **Others:** skin (rash, scleroderma), joints, thyroid, muscles (neuromuscular weakness).

**A pattern-based approach to the bedside:**

| Findings | Diagnosis to consider |
|---|---|
| Wheeze, prolonged expiration, hyperinflation, ± crackles | Asthma, COPD, heart failure ("cardiac asthma") |
| Raised JVP, basal crackles, S3, oedema, cardiomegaly | Heart failure |
| Focal crackles, bronchial breathing, dullness | Pneumonia |
| Stony dull base, reduced breath sounds | Pleural effusion |
| Hyper-resonance, absent breath sounds, tracheal deviation | Pneumothorax / tension pneumothorax |
| Fine basal crackles, clubbing | Pulmonary fibrosis |
| Normal chest, tachycardia, hypoxia, unilateral leg swelling | PE |
| Silent chest, exhaustion | Life-threatening asthma |
| Stridor, drooling | Upper airway obstruction, epiglottitis, anaphylaxis |
| Normal chest, deep sighing breathing, normal SpO₂ | Metabolic acidosis, hyperventilation, anxiety, anaemia, thyrotoxicosis |
| Pallor, flow murmur | Anaemia |
| Weakness, ptosis, poor cough, low vital capacity | Neuromuscular disease |

### 23.5 Investigations

Choose according to the leading hypotheses (Chapter 4).

#### First tier (most patients)

- **Pulse oximetry** (at rest and, if resting is normal, on exertion).
- **ECG:** arrhythmias, ischaemia, right heart strain, left ventricular hypertrophy, low voltage (effusion, COPD).
- **Chest X-ray:** consolidation, effusion, pneumothorax, hyperinflation, interstitial markings, cardiomegaly, pulmonary oedema, mediastinal widening, masses.
- **Full blood count** (anaemia, polycythaemia, infection, eosinophilia).
- **U&E, glucose, liver function, thyroid function, CRP.**
- **Natriuretic peptide (BNP or NT-proBNP)**: useful for ruling out heart failure when low. A normal BNP (<100 pg/mL) or NT-proBNP (<300 pg/mL in acute dyspnoea, with age-adjusted thresholds for rule-in) makes heart failure unlikely. High values are not specific: renal failure, atrial fibrillation, pulmonary embolism, sepsis and age raise levels.
- **Arterial or venous blood gas:** pH, PaO₂, PaCO₂, bicarbonate, lactate.
- **Peak expiratory flow and spirometry** (obstructive vs restrictive pattern).

#### Second tier according to the findings

- **Troponin** (cardiac ischaemia, PE, myocarditis).
- **D-dimer** when PE is suspected and pre-test probability is low or moderate (Chapter 70); age-adjusted cut-offs.
- **CT pulmonary angiography (CTPA)** for suspected PE (or V/Q scan).
- **Echocardiography:** LV and RV function, valves, pulmonary pressures, pericardial effusion.
- **High-resolution CT chest:** interstitial lung disease, bronchiectasis, emphysema, small lung cancers.
- **Full pulmonary function tests** (lung volumes, DLCO), **bronchodilator reversibility**, **FeNO**, **eosinophils** for asthma.
- **Cardiopulmonary exercise test** for unexplained dyspnoea.
- **Sleep study** for suspected sleep apnoea.
- **Bronchoscopy** for suspected malignancy or infection.
- **Pleural aspiration** for effusions.
- **Autoimmune serology, precipitins** for suspected interstitial disease.
- **Neuromuscular tests:** vital capacity (forced vital capacity lying and sitting), maximal inspiratory pressure, nerve conduction.

#### Matching test to presentation

| Presentation | Key tests |
|---|---|
| Acute dyspnoea, wheeze, known asthma | Peak flow, oximetry, chest X-ray if severe or atypical, blood gas if severe |
| Acute dyspnoea, orthopnoea, oedema | ECG, chest X-ray, BNP, troponin, echo, U&E |
| Acute pleuritic pain, hypoxia, risk factors | Wells/PERC, D-dimer, CTPA |
| Fever, productive cough | Chest X-ray, CRP, cultures, severity score (CURB-65) |
| Chronic exertional dyspnoea, smoker | Spirometry, chest X-ray, ECG, FBC, echo |
| Chronic dry cough, clubbing, crackles | HRCT, spirometry with DLCO, autoimmune screen |
| Unexplained chronic dyspnoea | Stepwise: oximetry (exertional), CXR, ECG, FBC, TSH, spirometry, BNP, echo, then CPET/CT |

### 23.6 Common causes of dyspnea

#### Respiratory

- **Asthma:** variable, episodic, wheeze, nocturnal symptoms, triggers, atopy; reversible airflow obstruction.
- **COPD:** smoker, chronic cough and sputum, progressive exertional dyspnoea, exacerbations, FEV₁/FVC <0.7 post-bronchodilator.
- **Pneumonia:** acute; fever; productive cough; focal signs.
- **Pulmonary embolism:** sudden or subacute dyspnoea, pleuritic pain, risk factors.
- **Pneumothorax:** sudden onset.
- **Pleural effusion:** progressive; dull base.
- **Interstitial lung disease:** progressive; dry cough; fine crackles; clubbing.
- **Lung cancer or lymphangitic carcinomatosis:** weight loss, haemoptysis, nodes.
- **Bronchiectasis:** chronic productive cough, recurrent infections.
- **Pulmonary hypertension:** exertional dyspnoea, syncope, loud P₂.
- **ARDS, aspiration, anaphylaxis, upper airway obstruction.**
- **Sleep apnoea and obesity hypoventilation.**

#### Cardiac

- **Left heart failure** (HFrEF and HFpEF): orthopnoea, PND, oedema, S3, raised JVP.
- **Valvular disease:** aortic stenosis, mitral stenosis, regurgitation.
- **Arrhythmias:** AF with rapid rate, ventricular tachycardia.
- **Ischaemic heart disease:** angina equivalent.
- **Cardiomyopathy, myocarditis.**
- **Pericardial effusion, constrictive pericarditis.**
- **Congenital heart disease:** intracardiac shunts, Eisenmenger syndrome.

#### Others

- **Anaemia** (acute or chronic).
- **Metabolic acidosis:** DKA, renal failure, lactic acidosis, salicylates, toxic alcohols.
- **Thyrotoxicosis and hypothyroidism.**
- **Neuromuscular:** myasthenia gravis, Guillain–Barré, motor neurone disease, muscular dystrophy, phrenic nerve palsy.
- **Chest wall:** kyphoscoliosis, rib fractures, ankylosing spondylitis.
- **Abdominal:** ascites, pregnancy, obesity, large masses.
- **Psychogenic:** anxiety, panic disorder, hyperventilation, vocal cord dysfunction.
- **Drug-related:** beta-blockers, amiodarone, nitrofurantoin, methotrexate, bleomycin, NSAIDs.
- **Deconditioning:** a diagnosis of exclusion in the sedentary or post-illness patient.
- **Poisoning:** carbon monoxide, cyanide, methaemoglobinaemia.

#### Unexplained chronic dyspnea

When initial tests are unrevealing, think of:

- **Asthma** with normal spirometry (variable airway obstruction; use bronchial provocation or peak flow variability).
- **Heart failure with preserved ejection fraction** (diastolic dysfunction; BNP, echo, exercise testing).
- **Pulmonary vascular disease** (chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension; V/Q scan).
- **Early interstitial lung disease** (HRCT).
- **Anaemia and thyroid disease.**
- **Obesity and deconditioning.**
- **Dysfunctional breathing and vocal cord dysfunction.**
- **Neuromuscular weakness** (diaphragm).
- **Multiple small causes.**

A **cardiopulmonary exercise test (CPET)** can differentiate cardiac, pulmonary, vascular, muscular and deconditioning contributions.

### 23.7 Cough: definitions and approach

**Cough** is a protective reflex to clear the airways, and the most common symptom for which people seek medical advice. It may arise from the airways, lungs, pleura, upper airway, oesophagus, ear (Arnold's nerve), or the central nervous system.

#### Classification by duration

| Type | Duration | Typical causes |
|---|---|---|
| **Acute** | <3 weeks | Viral upper respiratory infection, acute bronchitis, pneumonia, influenza, COVID-19, pertussis, exacerbations of asthma or COPD, aspiration, foreign body, inhaled irritants, pulmonary embolism, acute heart failure |
| **Subacute** | 3–8 weeks | Post-infectious cough (the most common), pertussis, post-viral bronchial hyperreactivity, sinusitis |
| **Chronic** | >8 weeks | Upper airway cough syndrome (UACS), asthma and cough-variant asthma, non-asthmatic eosinophilic bronchitis, GORD, ACE inhibitor, COPD/chronic bronchitis, bronchiectasis, lung cancer, interstitial lung disease, heart failure, TB, tobacco smoking, chronic aspiration, obstructive sleep apnoea, refractory (unexplained) chronic cough |

(Duration categories vary slightly by guideline; some define chronic as >8 weeks in adults and >4 weeks in children.)

#### The history

- **Duration and tempo:** onset related to an infection or exposure; progressive or episodic.
- **Character:** dry vs productive; barking (croup, tracheomalacia, psychogenic), brassy (tracheal compression), whooping (pertussis), paroxysmal with post-tussive vomiting (pertussis), nocturnal (asthma, GORD, heart failure), positional.
- **Sputum:** amount, colour, consistency, blood. Mucoid (viral, chronic bronchitis), purulent (infection, bronchiectasis), rusty (pneumococcal), frothy pink (pulmonary oedema), foul (anaerobes, abscess), copious daily (bronchiectasis), black (smoke inhalation, coal).
- **Triggers:** cold air, exercise, allergens, laughing, talking, meals, lying flat, strong odours, medications.
- **Associated features:** wheeze, dyspnoea, chest pain, fever, night sweats, weight loss, hoarseness, dysphagia, heartburn, nasal symptoms (post-nasal drip, throat clearing), rhinitis, sinusitis, snoring.
- **Exposures:** smoking (active and passive), vaping, occupational dusts, mould, birds, TB contacts, travel.
- **Drugs:** ACE inhibitors (dry cough in about 5–20%, may start weeks or months after initiation), beta-blockers, amiodarone, nitrofurantoin, methotrexate, inhaled corticosteroids (hoarseness and cough), dry-powder inhalers.
- **Past history:** asthma, atopy, reflux, sinus disease, heart failure, cancer, TB, immunosuppression.

#### Red flags for serious disease

- **Haemoptysis**
- **Age >45–50 with smoking history and new/changed cough**
- **Weight loss, night sweats, fever**
- **Hoarseness lasting >3 weeks**
- **Dysphagia or choking on swallowing**
- **Clubbing, abnormal chest X-ray, abnormal examination**
- **Dyspnoea at rest or progressive**
- **Recurrent pneumonia**
- **Immunosuppression, HIV, TB exposure**
- **Prior cancer**
- **Cough in a child with sudden onset (foreign body)**

Red flags require a **chest X-ray** (and often CT), whatever the cough type.

### 23.8 Approach to chronic cough

Adults with chronic cough, a normal examination, a normal chest X-ray, who are non-smokers and not taking ACE inhibitors have most commonly one or more of three causes (the "big three"):

1. **Upper airway cough syndrome (UACS; "post-nasal drip syndrome"):** throat clearing, sensation of mucus dripping, nasal congestion, cobblestoning of the pharynx, sinus disease. Trial: intranasal corticosteroid and antihistamine/decongestant (sedating first-generation antihistamine in some guidelines), treatment of allergy and sinusitis.
2. **Asthma (including cough-variant asthma) and eosinophilic bronchitis:** often cough is the only symptom; spirometry may be normal; look for variable airflow obstruction, airway hyperresponsiveness, raised FeNO or sputum eosinophils. Trial: inhaled corticosteroid (± bronchodilator).
3. **Gastro-oesophageal reflux disease (GORD):** cough may occur without heartburn; may require PPI trial for 8–12 weeks and lifestyle measures.

**Stepwise approach:**

1. Take a detailed history and examine (ENT, chest, heart).
2. **Stop ACE inhibitors** (cough usually resolves within 1–4 weeks after stopping; it can take longer) and stop smoking.
3. **Chest X-ray** to exclude significant pathology.
4. **Spirometry with bronchodilator reversibility**; consider FeNO and peak flow variability.
5. **Treat empirically** for the most likely cause(s) based on the history, and **assess response** over weeks (include adherence and technique).
6. If unresponsive: **HRCT chest**, consider **bronchoscopy** (airway tumour, foreign body, endobronchial disease), **ENT evaluation** (laryngoscopy), **24-hour oesophageal pH/impedance monitoring**, assessment for aspiration/swallowing disorders, obstructive sleep apnoea.
7. Consider **refractory chronic cough (cough hypersensitivity syndrome):** speech and language therapy, neuromodulators (e.g. low-dose gabapentin, pregabalin, amitriptyline) or newer P2X3 antagonists where approved.

### 23.9 Causes of cough with distinctive features

| Cause | Distinctive features |
|---|---|
| **Viral URTI / acute bronchitis** | Cough with coryza, sore throat; lasts up to 3 weeks; usually self-limiting; antibiotics not indicated for uncomplicated cases |
| **Post-infectious cough** | Follows a viral illness; dry or minimal sputum; 3–8 weeks; resolves spontaneously |
| **Pertussis (whooping cough)** | Paroxysmal cough, whoop (not always), post-tussive vomiting, lasts weeks to months; in adults often only persistent cough; infants at risk of apnoea; PCR/culture |
| **Pneumonia** | Fever, pleuritic pain, dyspnoea, focal signs |
| **TB** | Cough >2–3 weeks, haemoptysis, weight loss, night sweats, risk factors |
| **COPD / chronic bronchitis** | Smoker, productive morning cough |
| **Bronchiectasis** | Copious purulent sputum, recurrent infections, haemoptysis, clubbing, crackles |
| **Lung cancer** | New or changed cough, haemoptysis, hoarseness, weight loss, clubbing |
| **ILD** | Dry cough, progressive dyspnoea, clubbing, fine crackles |
| **Heart failure** | Nocturnal cough, orthopnoea, frothy sputum |
| **ACE inhibitor** | Dry, tickling, irritating cough |
| **Aspiration** | Cough with eating or drinking, neurological disease, dysphagia |
| **Foreign body (child)** | Sudden choking episode, persistent cough, localised wheeze |
| **Cystic fibrosis** | Chronic productive cough, failure to thrive, steatorrhoea, nasal polyps |
| **Psychogenic / habit cough** | Honking or barking, disappears during sleep and distraction |
| **Laryngeal or tracheal disease** | Stridor, hoarseness, brassy cough |
| **Eosinophilic granulomatosis, drug reactions** | Eosinophilia, systemic features |

### 23.10 Haemoptysis

**Haemoptysis** is the coughing up of blood from the lower respiratory tract. It must be distinguished from **haematemesis** (dark, acidic, mixed with food, from the stomach) and **pseudohaemoptysis** (bleeding from the nose, mouth or pharynx).

#### Severity

- **Mild:** streaks of blood in sputum.
- **Moderate:** up to ~100–200 mL/24 h.
- **Massive (life-threatening):** typically >200–600 mL in 24 hours (definitions vary), or any volume that impairs gas exchange or haemodynamics. Most deaths result from asphyxiation, not exsanguination.

#### Common causes

| Cause | Typical clues |
|---|---|
| **Bronchitis, respiratory infection** | Acute illness, mild streaks |
| **Bronchiectasis** | Chronic sputum, recurrent infection |
| **Lung cancer (primary or metastatic)** | Smoker, weight loss, persistent or recurrent bleed |
| **TB (active or old cavity, aspergilloma)** | Weight loss, night sweats, cavity on imaging |
| **Pulmonary embolism / infarction** | Pleuritic pain, risk factors |
| **Mitral stenosis, pulmonary oedema** | Frothy pink sputum; murmur |
| **Pneumonia, lung abscess** | Fever, productive cough |
| **Vasculitis and Goodpasture syndrome** | Haematuria, renal failure, haemoptysis; positive ANCA/anti-GBM |
| **Anticoagulation, coagulopathy** | Drug history |
| **Trauma, foreign body, bronchial artery bleeding, arteriovenous malformations (Osler–Weber–Rendu)** | History, imaging |
| **Cryptogenic** | No cause found (~20–30% of non-massive haemoptysis) |

#### Approach

1. **Stabilise** if significant: airway protection, position bleeding side down (if known), oxygen, IV access, cross-match, correct coagulopathy, tranexamic acid (nebulised or IV) in some settings.
2. **Chest X-ray** (and CT, ideally CT angiography, for localisation and cause), **bronchoscopy** (rigid for massive haemoptysis; flexible for localisation).
3. **Treatment:** bronchial artery embolisation, bronchoscopic measures, surgery for refractory bleeding, treatment of the underlying cause.
4. **Any haemoptysis in a smoker over 40** warrants urgent investigation (CXR and CT, bronchoscopy).

### 23.11 Worked cases

#### Case 1: Acute pulmonary oedema

*A 72-year-old man with a previous myocardial infarction wakes at 3 a.m. gasping for air, sitting bolt upright at an open window, coughing up pink frothy sputum. RR 34, HR 118, BP 190/105, SpO₂ 84% on air. Bilateral coarse crackles to the mid-zones, S3 gallop, sweaty and anxious.*

**Reasoning.** Sudden severe dyspnoea, frothy pink sputum, crackles, S3, hypertension and ischaemic history: **acute cardiogenic pulmonary oedema**, probably with a hypertensive and/or ischaemic trigger.

**Action.** Sit up, high-flow oxygen/CPAP, IV loop diuretic, IV nitrate (BP permitting), ECG (STEMI?), troponin, chest X-ray, BNP, echocardiogram, treat precipitant (ischaemia, arrhythmia, hypertensive crisis, non-adherence, valvular failure).

#### Case 2: A smoker with progressive breathlessness

*A 66-year-old man (45 pack-years) has had 3 years of progressive breathlessness on walking uphill, morning cough with white sputum, and two winter chest infections. He is mMRC 3. Examination: barrel chest, reduced cricosternal distance, prolonged expiration, quiet breath sounds, no clubbing. Spirometry post-bronchodilator: FEV₁/FVC 0.52, FEV₁ 48% predicted, minimal reversibility.*

**Reasoning.** Persistent airflow limitation (FEV₁/FVC <0.7 post-bronchodilator) in a smoker with chronic symptoms: **COPD** (GOLD grade 2–3). Look for comorbidities: heart failure, lung cancer, osteoporosis, depression.

**Action.** Smoking cessation (the only intervention that alters decline), inhaled bronchodilators, pulmonary rehabilitation, vaccination, assess for exacerbation risk, oxygen assessment if hypoxaemic, review inhaler technique, screen for alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency in appropriate cases.

#### Case 3: Dyspnoea and cough in a young woman

*A 24-year-old woman has episodic cough and chest tightness at night and after exercise for 6 months, worse in spring. She has eczema and hay fever. Chest examination between episodes is normal; spirometry is normal. Peak flow diary shows morning dips of 25% below evening values.*

**Reasoning.** Atopy, nocturnal and exercise-related symptoms, variable peak flow: **asthma** (a normal spirometry in an asymptomatic phase does not exclude it).

**Action.** Confirm variability (peak flow diary, reversibility, FeNO, challenge test if needed), start inhaled corticosteroid (± reliever), identify triggers, provide an action plan.

#### Case 4: Pleuritic pain and dyspnoea in a cancer patient

*A 58-year-old woman with ovarian cancer on chemotherapy has had 2 days of worsening breathlessness and left-sided pleuritic pain. HR 118, RR 28, SpO₂ 90%, BP 105/68. Chest clear. Left calf swollen. Troponin mildly raised, D-dimer elevated, ECG shows sinus tachycardia with T-wave inversion in V1–V3.*

**Reasoning.** Cancer, unilateral leg swelling, tachycardia, hypoxia, ECG evidence of right heart strain: **pulmonary embolism**, possibly intermediate-high risk.

**Action.** Start therapeutic anticoagulation (LMWH), CTPA, echocardiography (RV function), consider reperfusion therapy if she deteriorates, screen for contraindications and bleeding risk (platelets, brain metastases).

#### Case 5: Chronic cough that is not what it seems

*A 55-year-old man, an ex-smoker, has had a dry cough for 4 months and says his lisinopril was started 6 months ago. He has no sputum, breathlessness or weight loss. Chest X-ray and spirometry are normal.*

**Reasoning.** ACE inhibitor-induced cough is the most likely cause (dry, persistent, onset weeks to months after starting). Chest X-ray and spirometry are normal, reducing the likelihood of other causes.

**Action.** Stop lisinopril and switch to an ARB; review in 4–6 weeks (cough may take up to a month or longer to fade); if it persists, investigate for UACS, asthma and GORD, and reconsider imaging.

#### Case 6: Cough with a red flag

*A 63-year-old woman, a 30-pack-year smoker, has had a new persistent cough for 10 weeks with two episodes of blood-streaked sputum, a 5 kg weight loss and hoarseness. Chest X-ray shows a left hilar mass.*

**Reasoning.** New cough, haemoptysis, weight loss, hoarseness (recurrent laryngeal nerve involvement suggesting mediastinal disease) and a hilar mass: **lung cancer** until proved otherwise.

**Action.** Urgent CT chest/upper abdomen with contrast, bronchoscopy and biopsy or image-guided biopsy, staging (PET/CT, brain imaging as indicated), multidisciplinary team referral under the suspected lung cancer pathway.

#### Case 7: Dyspnoea in a woman with normal tests

*A 32-year-old woman has episodes of "air hunger" with frequent sighing, tingling around the mouth and fingers, chest tightness and dizziness, often triggered by stress. Examination, ECG, chest X-ray, spirometry, blood tests (including TSH, FBC) are normal. SpO₂ is 99% during an episode.*

**Reasoning.** Features of **dysfunctional breathing/hyperventilation syndrome** (with possible panic disorder). This is a positive diagnosis when typical, but exclude organic disease first, including anaemia, thyroid disease, asthma, and PE if risk factors exist.

**Action.** Explain the mechanism (hyperventilation lowers CO₂, causing tingling and dizziness), breathing retraining with a physiotherapist, address anxiety (CBT), and provide follow-up.

### 23.12 Common pitfalls

- **Treating dyspnoea as "anxiety"** without excluding organic causes.
- **Assuming a single cause** in older, multimorbid patients.
- **Relying on SpO₂ alone:** normal oxygen saturation does not exclude significant disease (anaemia, acidosis, early PE, asthma between attacks).
- **Giving high-flow oxygen to patients with chronic hypercapnia.**
- **Misinterpreting a "quiet chest"** in asthma.
- **Over-reliance on BNP** (raised in renal failure, AF, PE, sepsis, age).
- **Missing PE** because the chest is clear.
- **Missing upper airway obstruction** when wheeze is attributed to asthma.
- **Not asking about ACE inhibitors, smoking, occupation and exposures** in chronic cough.
- **Empirical treatment without follow-up** for chronic cough (check response and adherence).
- **Ignoring red flags** (haemoptysis, weight loss, hoarseness, clubbing) in cough.
- **Delaying chest imaging** in a smoker with persistent cough.
- **Prescribing antibiotics for acute bronchitis** without evidence of bacterial infection.

---

### 23.13 Key points

- Dyspnoea is a subjective symptom; quantify it (mMRC, NYHA) and link it to exertion, posture and time course.
- In acute dyspnoea, start with ABCDE and look for the immediate threats: airway obstruction, tension pneumothorax, severe asthma, PE, pulmonary oedema, anaphylaxis.
- Use history and examination to separate cardiac, respiratory, haematological, metabolic, neuromuscular and functional causes; confirm with oximetry, ECG, chest X-ray, BNP, spirometry and targeted imaging.
- Multiple causes are common; unexplained chronic dyspnoea needs a stepwise work-up, ending with CPET if necessary.
- Classify cough as acute, subacute or chronic; most acute cough is viral and self-limiting.
- Chronic cough: stop ACE inhibitors and smoking, check chest X-ray, treat the big three (UACS, asthma/eosinophilic bronchitis, GORD) sequentially, and refer when red flags are present.
- Haemoptysis in a smoker or with weight loss needs urgent imaging; massive haemoptysis is an airway emergency.

### 23.14 Self-assessment

1. Define orthopnoea and PND, and name the commonest cause.
   *Answer: Orthopnoea is breathlessness on lying flat, relieved by sitting up; PND is waking at night gasping for breath; heart failure is the commonest cause.*

2. What is the BNP value below which acute heart failure is unlikely?
   *Answer: BNP <100 pg/mL (or NT-proBNP <300 pg/mL), with consideration of age and renal function.*

3. List the three most common causes of chronic cough in non-smokers with normal chest X-rays and no ACE inhibitor.
   *Answer: Upper airway cough syndrome, asthma (including cough-variant asthma and eosinophilic bronchitis), and gastro-oesophageal reflux disease.*

4. A patient with COPD has a PaCO₂ of 7.5 kPa on high-flow oxygen. What is the likely issue and what should you do?
   *Answer: Oxygen-induced hypercapnia; reduce to controlled low-flow oxygen aiming for SpO₂ 88–92%, consider non-invasive ventilation.*

5. Name five red flags in a patient with cough.
   *Answer (any five): haemoptysis; weight loss; night sweats; hoarseness; clubbing; age >45 with smoking; dyspnoea at rest; abnormal chest X-ray; dysphagia; immunosuppression; recurrent pneumonia.*

6. When should a cough attributed to an ACE inhibitor be expected to resolve after stopping the drug?
   *Answer: Usually within 1–4 weeks, but sometimes it takes longer.*
