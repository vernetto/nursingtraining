# Part IV: Symptom-Based Diagnosis (Presenting Complaints)

## Chapter 21. Fever

### Learning objectives

After studying this chapter you should be able to:

1. Define fever, distinguish it from hyperthermia, and explain why the distinction matters.
2. Take a focused history and perform a focused examination to identify the source of an acute fever.
3. Recognise the febrile patient who is dangerously ill (sepsis, meningitis, neutropenic fever, malaria) and act without delay.
4. Build a structured differential for prolonged, recurrent and unexplained fever, including infection, malignancy, inflammatory disease and drugs.
5. Use travel, exposure, age and immune status to refine probabilities.
6. Choose investigations in a stepwise, hypothesis-driven manner, and avoid the "shotgun" work-up.
7. Apply the approach to special groups: infants, older adults, post-operative patients and the immunocompromised.

---

### 21.1 What fever is, and what it is not

**Fever** is a regulated rise in the hypothalamic temperature set-point, usually triggered by pyrogens (exogenous, such as bacterial products, or endogenous, such as interleukin-1, interleukin-6 and TNF-α released by immune cells). The body then generates and conserves heat (shivering, vasoconstriction) until the new set-point is reached.

**Hyperthermia** is a rise in body temperature that is *not* due to a change in set-point: heat production or heat gain overwhelms heat loss. The distinction matters because antipyretics (paracetamol, NSAIDs) work on the set-point and are ineffective in hyperthermia, which requires physical cooling and removal of the cause.

| | Fever | Hyperthermia |
|---|---|---|
| Mechanism | Raised set-point | Failure of heat dissipation or excess heat production |
| Skin | Often cool and vasoconstricted in the rise phase, then warm and flushed | Hot, may be dry (heat stroke) or sweaty |
| Response to antipyretics | Yes | No |
| Examples | Infection, inflammation, malignancy, drugs | Heat stroke, neuroleptic malignant syndrome, serotonin syndrome, malignant hyperthermia, thyroid storm, anticholinergic toxicity, stimulant or salicylate toxicity |
| Danger | Usually the cause | Temperatures above about 41 °C cause direct organ injury |

#### Definitions in practice

- **Normal body temperature** varies by individual, site of measurement and time of day (lowest early morning, highest late afternoon, with a variation of about 0.5–1 °C). The traditional 37.0 °C is an average; the normal range is wider and tends to be somewhat lower in older adults.
- **Fever** is usually taken as a core temperature of **38.0 °C or higher** (or ≥37.8 °C by mouth in some definitions). Older adults and some immunosuppressed patients may mount a blunted response, so a **rise of about 1.1 °C above baseline** or a temperature of ≥37.8 °C on repeated readings can be significant.
- **Measurement site:** core measures (rectal, pulmonary artery, bladder catheter) are most accurate; oral and tympanic measurements are convenient but can be affected by technique and recent drinks; axillary and forehead (temporal artery or skin infrared) readings can be falsely low, and should not be used to rule out fever in an unwell patient.
- **Subjective fever** ("I felt hot and shivery") deserves attention but should be confirmed with a thermometer.
- **Hypothermia** (<35 °C) in an unwell patient can signal sepsis and carries a worse prognosis than fever.

#### Is fever harmful?

Moderate fever is part of an adaptive host response and enhances some immune functions. It increases metabolic demand and fluid loss, which matters in patients with heart, lung or brain disease. It is **not** the same as the severity of the underlying illness: a patient with a temperature of 39.5 °C with a viral illness may be far less ill than a patient with a temperature of 36.2 °C and septic shock. Treat the patient (comfort, hydration, the cause), not the number.

### 21.2 Three questions to ask every febrile patient

1. **Is this patient dangerously ill now?** (Sepsis, meningitis, immunosuppression, severe malaria, toxic shock, necrotising infection.)
2. **Where is the source?** (Focal symptoms and signs; exposures; devices; recent procedures.)
3. **How long has it lasted, and what is the pattern?** (Acute <2 weeks; prolonged; recurrent; unexplained.)

Everything in this chapter is a refinement of those three questions.

### 21.3 Acute fever: the first assessment

#### Immediate triage: look for danger

A febrile patient needs urgent attention if any of the following are present:

- **Haemodynamic compromise:** systolic BP <100 mmHg (or a fall of >40 mmHg from baseline), heart rate >120, capillary refill >3 seconds, mottled skin, reduced urine output, raised lactate.
- **Altered mental state:** confusion, drowsiness, irritability, new agitation.
- **Respiratory compromise:** respiratory rate ≥25–30, SpO₂ <92%, increased work of breathing.
- **Non-blanching rash** (petechiae, purpura).
- **Meningism:** neck stiffness, photophobia, headache, positive Kernig or Brudzinski signs (these signs are specific but insensitive; their absence does not exclude meningitis).
- **Immunosuppression:** neutropenia (ANC <0.5 × 10⁹/L), chemotherapy, high-dose steroids, transplant, HIV with low CD4 count, asplenia, biologic therapy.
- **Recent travel to a malaria-endemic area.**
- **Extremes of age** (neonate, infant, frail older person).
- **Pregnancy or the puerperium.**
- **Severe pain out of proportion to findings** (necrotising soft-tissue infection, mesenteric ischaemia).
- **Injecting drug use, indwelling lines, prosthetic material, recent surgery.**

For patients with possible sepsis, follow the local sepsis pathway: assess with an early warning score (NEWS2 or similar), take blood cultures and lactate, give **antibiotics within the first hour** if shock or high suspicion, and give fluids (Chapters 38–39).

#### The focused history

Ask about:

- **Onset and course:** abrupt (bacterial, viral, malaria) or insidious; rigors (chills with shaking suggest bacteraemia, malaria, pyelonephritis, abscess, cholangitis, but are not specific); sweats; pattern.
- **Localising symptoms by system:**
  - *Head and neck:* headache, sore throat, ear pain, sinus pain, dental pain, neck stiffness.
  - *Chest:* cough, sputum, pleuritic pain, breathlessness.
  - *Abdomen:* pain, vomiting, diarrhoea, jaundice.
  - *Urinary:* dysuria, frequency, flank pain, haematuria.
  - *Skin and soft tissue:* rash, wounds, bites, redness, swelling.
  - *Musculoskeletal:* joint or bone pain, back pain.
  - *Genital:* discharge, ulcers, pelvic pain.
  - *Neurological:* confusion, weakness, seizures.
- **Contacts:** sick people at home, school or work; healthcare exposure.
- **Travel:** where, when, how long, accommodation, activities (see 21.7).
- **Exposures:** animals (birds, cats, farm animals, rodents), insect and tick bites, unpasteurised dairy, raw or undercooked food, freshwater swimming, sexual contact (new partners, unprotected sex), injecting drug use, occupational exposure (abattoir, laboratory, healthcare, sewage).
- **Medications:** new drugs in the last weeks (drug fever), antibiotics (and *Clostridioides difficile* risk), immunosuppressants, antipyretics.
- **Background:** diabetes, valve disease or prosthetic valve, implanted devices, previous infections, immunisation status, pregnancy, splenectomy.
- **Recent procedures or hospital stays:** surgery, catheters, lines, dental work, endoscopy.
- **Associated features:** weight loss, night sweats (consider TB, lymphoma, endocarditis), rash, joint symptoms.

#### The focused examination

Alongside full vital signs (temperature, pulse, BP, respiratory rate, SpO₂, mental state), examine systematically:

| Region | Look for |
|---|---|
| **General** | Toxicity, pallor, jaundice, dehydration, rigors, distress |
| **Skin** | Rash (describe; blanching or not), petechiae, cellulitis, abscess, pressure sores, injection sites, tick bites and eschar, IV line sites, surgical wounds |
| **Eyes** | Conjunctival petechiae or suffusion, uveitis, jaundice |
| **ENT and mouth** | Tonsillar exudate, dental abscess, otitis, sinus tenderness, oral thrush, ulcers |
| **Neck** | Meningism, lymphadenopathy, thyroid tenderness |
| **Lymph nodes and spleen** | Generalised lymphadenopathy, splenomegaly (EBV, malaria, endocarditis, lymphoma, brucellosis, typhoid) |
| **Chest** | Consolidation, effusion, pleural rub |
| **Heart** | New or changing murmur (endocarditis), pericardial rub |
| **Abdomen** | Tenderness, guarding, hepatomegaly, splenomegaly, renal angle tenderness, masses, hernias |
| **Rectal / pelvic / genital** | Prostate tenderness (avoid vigorous palpation in suspected acute prostatitis), pelvic tenderness or cervical motion tenderness, perianal abscess, testicular tenderness |
| **Musculoskeletal** | Joint effusion, vertebral tenderness, bone pain |
| **Lines and devices** | Insertion site erythema, pus, tenderness; urinary catheter; prosthetic joints; pacemaker pocket |
| **Neurological** | Orientation, focal deficit, meningism |
| **Feet / legs** | Diabetic foot ulcers, cellulitis, DVT |

#### Common causes by source

| Source | Typical causes |
|---|---|
| **Upper respiratory** | Viral URTI, streptococcal pharyngitis, infectious mononucleosis, sinusitis, otitis media, peritonsillar abscess |
| **Lower respiratory** | Pneumonia, influenza, COVID-19, bronchitis, empyema, TB |
| **Urinary** | Cystitis (usually afebrile), pyelonephritis, prostatitis, catheter-associated infection, perinephric abscess |
| **Abdominal** | Gastroenteritis, appendicitis, cholecystitis, cholangitis, diverticulitis, liver abscess, pancreatitis, spontaneous bacterial peritonitis, intra-abdominal abscess |
| **Skin and soft tissue** | Cellulitis, abscess, necrotising fasciitis, infected ulcers, line infection |
| **Bone and joint** | Septic arthritis, osteomyelitis, vertebral discitis, prosthetic joint infection |
| **CNS** | Meningitis, encephalitis, brain abscess |
| **Cardiovascular** | Infective endocarditis, pericarditis, mycotic aneurysm, device infection |
| **Genital and gynaecological** | Pelvic inflammatory disease, endometritis, tubo-ovarian abscess, septic abortion, epididymo-orchitis |
| **Systemic viral** | Influenza, EBV, CMV, HIV seroconversion, hepatitis, measles, dengue, COVID-19 |
| **Non-infectious** | Venous thromboembolism, drug reaction, transfusion reaction, haemorrhage (haematoma), tissue injury, pancreatitis, acute gout/pseudogout, thyroid storm, adrenal insufficiency, malignancy |

#### Initial investigations

Choose according to the likely source, not as a blanket panel (Chapter 4). A reasonable first tier for an adult with acute fever and no obvious source includes:

- Full blood count with differential, CRP (± procalcitonin where used locally), urea, electrolytes, creatinine, liver function tests, glucose.
- **Blood cultures** (at least two sets from separate sites, before antibiotics, if the patient is ill or has a suspected bacteraemia or endocarditis).
- Urinalysis and urine culture.
- Chest X-ray if respiratory symptoms or signs, or if the source is unclear.
- Lactate if unwell.
- Targeted tests: throat swab or rapid antigen test, respiratory viral PCR, stool culture/PCR, malaria film and rapid test (if exposure), HIV test, pregnancy test, wound swab, lumbar puncture if meningitis is suspected (after imaging if there are contraindications), joint aspiration if hot swollen joint.

Interpretation is contextual. A normal white count does not exclude serious bacterial infection (and in sepsis may be low); a high CRP supports bacterial infection but is non-specific (inflammation, tissue injury, VTE, malignancy). Procalcitonin may help distinguish bacterial from viral illness in some settings, but must be used within a validated pathway.

### 21.4 Focus on high-risk presentations

#### Fever and sepsis

Sepsis is life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated host response to infection. Features: fever or hypothermia, tachycardia, tachypnoea, hypotension, confusion, oliguria, mottling, raised lactate. Early recognition and treatment (cultures, antibiotics, fluids, source control) reduce mortality. Fever may be absent in older adults, in the very young, and in immunosuppressed patients. Do not wait for a "fever" to consider sepsis (Chapter 39).

#### Fever and meningism

Fever, headache, neck stiffness, photophobia, vomiting and altered consciousness raise concern for **bacterial meningitis**. In meningococcal disease, a non-blanching rash may appear late, so do not wait for it. Give empirical antibiotics (and dexamethasone as per local protocol) **without delay**, ideally after blood cultures, and do not delay treatment for lumbar puncture or imaging. Viral meningitis and encephalitis (HSV: give aciclovir empirically) are the main alternatives (Chapter 41).

#### Fever with a rash

| Pattern | Examples |
|---|---|
| **Non-blanching petechial/purpuric** | Meningococcaemia, other septicaemias, viral haemorrhagic fevers, Rocky Mountain spotted fever, endocarditis, TTP/DIC, vasculitis |
| **Maculopapular** | Measles, rubella, parvovirus, dengue, EBV (especially after amoxicillin), HIV seroconversion, secondary syphilis, drug eruption, Kawasaki disease |
| **Vesicular** | Varicella, herpes zoster, HSV, hand-foot-and-mouth disease, mpox |
| **Erythroderma/desquamation** | Toxic shock syndrome, scarlet fever, Kawasaki, drug reaction (DRESS, SJS/TEN) |
| **Urticarial / migratory** | Serum sickness, hepatitis B prodrome, drug reaction |
| **Erythema migrans** | Lyme disease |
| **Eschar** | Scrub typhus, spotted fevers (rickettsial), anthrax |

See Chapter 34 for rash assessment.

#### Fever in the immunocompromised and neutropenic fever

- **Febrile neutropenia** (a single oral temperature ≥38.3 °C, or ≥38.0 °C sustained for an hour, with an absolute neutrophil count <0.5 × 10⁹/L or expected to fall below that level) is a **medical emergency.** Give broad-spectrum anti-pseudomonal antibiotics **within an hour** of presentation, without waiting for results, and manage in line with a neutropenic sepsis pathway. Signs of inflammation may be minimal (no pus, no infiltrate on X-ray).
- **Steroids** and **biologic drugs** blunt fever and inflammatory markers.
- **Splenectomised or hyposplenic** patients are at risk of overwhelming post-splenectomy infection (encapsulated organisms) and need urgent treatment.
- **HIV:** consider opportunistic infections (pneumocystis pneumonia, TB, cryptococcal meningitis, CMV) according to CD4 count.
- **Transplant recipients:** infections vary with time since transplantation; consult the transplant team.

#### Fever and indwelling devices

Central venous catheters, urinary catheters, prosthetic joints, pacemakers, vascular grafts and other devices create surfaces for biofilm infection. Fever in a patient with a central line: take paired blood cultures (peripheral and from the line), examine the exit site, and consider line removal if tunnel infection, sepsis, or certain organisms (*S. aureus*, *Candida*, *Pseudomonas*).

#### Fever in older adults

Older patients may have **atypical presentations**: low-grade or absent fever with sepsis, confusion, falls, reduced appetite or functional decline. Common sources: urinary tract (but beware over-diagnosing a UTI based on a dipstick, Chapter 2), pneumonia (may have no cough), skin and soft tissue, intra-abdominal infection, endocarditis, and non-infectious causes (giant cell arteritis, VTE, drug fever, malignancy). Define the baseline temperature and functional status with the carers.

### 21.5 Fever in infants and children

Young children have frequent febrile illnesses, most of them viral and self-limiting, but a small proportion have serious bacterial infection. Risk depends strongly on age.

- **Neonates (<28 days) and young infants (<3 months):** serious bacterial infection (sepsis, meningitis, urinary tract infection) is more likely and clinical signs are unreliable. Fever ≥38 °C requires prompt senior review and usually a full sepsis work-up (blood, urine, ± CSF cultures) and often empirical antibiotics, following the local paediatric protocol.
- **Older infants and children:** use **traffic-light systems** (e.g. NICE) that combine colour, activity, respiratory signs, hydration and other features to classify risk. High-risk features include pallor or mottled skin, non-blanching rash, lethargy, weak cry, grunting, tachypnoea, reduced skin turgor, bulging fontanelle, neck stiffness, seizures, bilious vomiting.
- **Fever of unknown source** in a well-looking infant or child should prompt urinalysis (UTI is the commonest occult bacterial infection) and careful follow-up.
- **Kawasaki disease:** fever lasting 5 days or more, with at least four of five features (bilateral non-purulent conjunctival injection, oral changes such as strawberry tongue and cracked lips, polymorphous rash, cervical lymphadenopathy, changes in the extremities such as swelling, erythema and later desquamation). Delay in treatment (IVIG, aspirin) increases the risk of coronary artery aneurysms. Consider also in infants with incomplete forms.
- **Antipyretics** relieve discomfort but do not prevent febrile seizures; do not give them solely to normalise temperature.
- **Safety-net advice** is crucial (Chapter 73).

See Chapter 59 for the full paediatric approach.

### 21.6 Post-operative and hospital-acquired fever

Fever is common after surgery and is often due to the inflammatory response itself. Timing and context help:

| Timing | Common causes |
|---|---|
| **Immediate (first 24–48 hours)** | Inflammatory response to tissue injury, atelectasis (a weak cause), transfusion reaction, drug reaction, malignant hyperthermia (rare), aspiration, pre-existing infection |
| **Days 3–5** | Pneumonia, urinary tract infection (catheter), IV line phlebitis or infection, *C. difficile* if on antibiotics, anastomotic leak, DVT/PE |
| **Days 5–10** | Surgical site infection, intra-abdominal collection or abscess, anastomotic leak, deep infection of implants |
| **After 10 days or later** | Abscess, prosthetic infection, drug fever, VTE, non-infectious causes (pancreatitis, gout) |

The traditional mnemonic ("**5 Ws**": Wind (lungs), Water (urine), Wound, Walking (DVT/PE), Wonder drugs) is a helpful checklist, but an additional list of causes (line sites, intra-abdominal collections, *C. difficile*, medications) should be reviewed.

A systematic approach: review the **operation and complications**, **wound**, **drains**, **lines**, **catheter**, **chest**, **calves**, **abdomen**, **medications and transfusions**, **stool**, then target tests (cultures from blood, urine, wound; chest X-ray; CT if intra-abdominal source suspected; ultrasound for DVT).

### 21.7 Fever in the returned traveller

For any febrile patient who has travelled recently, **fever is malaria until proved otherwise** if they have been in a malaria-endemic area.

#### Key points in the history

- **Destination(s)** and dates, including brief stopovers.
- **Time since return** (incubation period): helps narrow the differential.
- **Malaria prophylaxis:** which drug, adherence (no regimen is 100% effective), dosing after return.
- **Mosquito and tick bites, freshwater exposure, animal contact, raw food or water, sexual contact, medical treatment abroad, vaccinations (yellow fever, typhoid, hepatitis A/B, rabies).**
- **Symptoms:** diarrhoea, rash, jaundice, eschar, neurological signs, bleeding.

#### Typical incubation periods (approximate)

| Incubation | Examples |
|---|---|
| **<10 days** | Dengue, chikungunya, Zika, Rickettsia (spotted fever), travellers' diarrhoea infections, influenza and other respiratory infections, typhoid (may be 1–3 weeks), leptospirosis |
| **10–21 days** | Malaria (most *P. falciparum* within a month), typhoid and paratyphoid, acute HIV, leptospirosis, Q fever, brucellosis |
| **>21 days (and months)** | Malaria (especially *P. vivax*/*ovale* relapses), hepatitis A/B/E, TB, schistosomiasis (Katayama fever), amoebic liver abscess, visceral leishmaniasis, rabies, filariasis, HIV |

#### Specific important diagnoses

- **Malaria:** fever (often with rigors and sweats), headache, myalgia, vomiting; anaemia, thrombocytopenia, splenomegaly, jaundice. Diagnose with thick and thin blood films and rapid antigen tests; repeat tests at 12–24 hour intervals if negative and suspicion persists. Severe malaria: impaired consciousness, seizures, respiratory distress, hypoglycaemia, acidosis, renal impairment, severe anaemia, high parasitaemia, shock; requires IV artesunate and intensive care. **Treat as an emergency.**
- **Dengue:** fever with severe headache, retro-orbital pain, myalgia ("breakbone fever"), rash, leucopenia and thrombocytopenia. Dengue can progress to a **critical phase** around days 3–7, when fever falls: look for warning signs (abdominal pain, persistent vomiting, mucosal bleeding, lethargy, rising haematocrit with falling platelets); manage with careful fluids.
- **Typhoid (enteric) fever:** step-ladder rise in fever over a week, headache, relative bradycardia, abdominal pain, constipation or diarrhoea, "rose spots" (rare), splenomegaly; blood cultures. Complications in the 2nd–3rd week: intestinal perforation and haemorrhage.
- **Rickettsial infections** (spotted fevers, scrub typhus): fever, headache, rash, eschar at the bite site, lymphadenopathy. Treat empirically with doxycycline if suspected.
- **Leptospirosis:** fever, myalgia (especially calves), conjunctival suffusion, jaundice, renal impairment, after freshwater or animal-urine exposure.
- **Viral haemorrhagic fevers** (Ebola, Lassa, Marburg, Crimean–Congo): fever with bleeding, shock, in an endemic area or after contact. Isolate the patient immediately and follow national guidance.
- **Others:** acute HIV, hepatitis A/E, amoebic liver abscess, schistosomiasis, trypanosomiasis, brucellosis, Q fever, chikungunya, Zika, mpox, measles, COVID-19, influenza.

### 21.8 Prolonged, recurrent and unexplained fever

#### Definitions

- **Fever of unknown origin (FUO) (classic):** temperature >38.3 °C on several occasions, lasting more than 3 weeks, with no diagnosis after an appropriate evaluation (traditionally "1 week of inpatient investigation" or the equivalent in outpatient care). Other categories: **nosocomial FUO** (in hospital, ≥3 days), **neutropenic FUO**, and **HIV-associated FUO**.
- **Recurrent (periodic) fever:** repeated episodes separated by fever-free intervals.
- **Prolonged fever** (more than 1–2 weeks) not yet qualifying as FUO.

#### Causes of classic FUO

In modern series, roughly one-fifth to a third of cases are due to **infection**, one-tenth to a fifth to **malignancy**, about a fifth to a quarter to **non-infectious inflammatory disease**, and a minority to miscellaneous causes; **15–30% remain undiagnosed**, often resolving spontaneously. The proportions vary by age, region and the availability of imaging.

| Category | Examples |
|---|---|
| **Infections** | TB (extrapulmonary, miliary), infective endocarditis (especially culture-negative: HACEK, *Bartonella*, *Coxiella*), intra-abdominal and pelvic abscesses, osteomyelitis, sinusitis, dental abscess, prostatitis, EBV, CMV, HIV, brucellosis, Q fever, toxoplasmosis, salmonellosis, malaria, leishmaniasis, syphilis |
| **Malignancy** | Lymphoma (Hodgkin: Pel–Ebstein fever is rare), leukaemia, renal cell carcinoma, hepatocellular carcinoma, colon carcinoma, atrial myxoma, metastases |
| **Non-infectious inflammatory** | Giant cell arteritis and other vasculitis, adult Still disease, polymyalgia rheumatica, SLE, rheumatoid arthritis, sarcoidosis, inflammatory bowel disease, polyarteritis nodosa, Takayasu arteritis, familial Mediterranean fever and other autoinflammatory syndromes, subacute thyroiditis |
| **Miscellaneous** | **Drug fever**, pulmonary embolism and DVT, haematoma, cirrhosis/alcoholic hepatitis, thyroid disease, adrenal insufficiency, factitious fever, granulomatous hepatitis, familial dysautonomia, cyclic neutropenia |

#### Approach to FUO

1. **Revisit the history and examination (repeatedly).** Most diagnoses depend on a clue missed first time: a drug, a travel, an animal, a pattern, a murmur, a tender temporal artery, a splenic tip, a new nodule. Examine daily and look at skin, eyes, nails, nodes, joints, thyroid, abdomen, rectum and genitalia, and ask about weight loss, night sweats and family history.
2. **Confirm the fever.** Document the temperature chart with a reliable thermometer; exclude factitious fever (discordant pulse, normal temperature when observed, fever without sweats).
3. **Review the medications** (including supplements and recent withdrawal). **Drug fever** often arises 7–10 days after starting a drug (but may arise at any time) and the patient may look surprisingly well, with a relative bradycardia, eosinophilia and mild liver enzyme elevation. Withdraw the suspected agent; fever usually resolves within 48–72 hours.
4. **Order a standard first tier** (not exhaustive): full blood count and film, CRP and ESR, ferritin, LDH, liver and renal function, urinalysis and microscopy, **blood cultures x 3 (before antibiotics)**, HIV, chest X-ray, autoantibodies (ANA, RF, ANCA as indicated), serologies as indicated by exposure (EBV, CMV, Q fever, brucella, bartonella, syphilis), tuberculosis tests (IGRA/TST, sputum if indicated).
5. **Imaging in a stepwise manner:** ultrasound; **CT chest/abdomen/pelvis**; **FDG-PET/CT** (increasingly useful in FUO; can identify occult infection, malignancy, vasculitis and inflammation); echocardiography when endocarditis is possible; **MRI** where appropriate.
6. **Tissue diagnosis:** lymph node, liver, bone marrow, temporal artery (in patients over 50 with high ESR/CRP, headache or visual symptoms), skin or other abnormal lesions. Biopsy the abnormality, not "blindly."
7. **Avoid empirical therapy** unless the patient is deteriorating, since steroids or antibiotics may obscure the diagnosis. Exceptions include suspected giant cell arteritis (to protect vision) and suspected TB in a critically ill patient.
8. **Follow-up and watchful waiting.** If the patient is stable and investigations are non-diagnostic, careful follow-up, with regular reassessment and repeated examination, is appropriate; many fevers resolve spontaneously.

#### Periodic and recurrent fevers

- **Infections with relapse** (malaria, borreliosis, brucellosis, lymphoma with Pel–Ebstein pattern, abscess).
- **Autoinflammatory syndromes:** familial Mediterranean fever (episodes of fever with serositis lasting 1–3 days, in people of Mediterranean, Armenian, Turkish and Arab ancestry; complication: amyloidosis; treated with colchicine), TRAPS, hyper-IgD syndrome, cryopyrin-associated periodic syndromes.
- **PFAPA** (periodic fever, aphthous stomatitis, pharyngitis, adenitis): children, regular intervals of 3–6 weeks, resolves spontaneously.
- **Cyclic neutropenia:** fever every ~21 days with oral ulcers and neutropenia.
- **Others:** Behçet disease, adult Still disease, Crohn disease, cancer, factitious.

Document the pattern (diary of temperatures, associated symptoms, triggers) and look for features between episodes.

### 21.9 Fever patterns: what they do and do not tell you

| Pattern | Description | Associations |
|---|---|---|
| **Continuous (sustained)** | Little daily variation | Typhoid (step-ladder), lobar pneumonia, typhus, drug fever |
| **Intermittent (hectic, spiking)** | Wide swings, returning to normal | Abscess, bacteraemia, adult Still disease, TB, lymphoma, malaria (tertian every 48 h; quartan every 72 h) |
| **Remittent** | Daily variation but always above normal | Many infections, endocarditis |
| **Relapsing** | Fever for days, afebrile, then recurs | Borrelia, malaria, brucellosis, rat-bite fever, Pel–Ebstein pattern |
| **Biphasic ("saddle-back")** | Fever, brief remission, second peak | Dengue, many viral illnesses, poliomyelitis, leptospirosis |

The diagnostic value of the pattern is limited: antipyretics, steroids and antibiotics change patterns, and many diseases overlap. **Relative bradycardia** (pulse lower than expected for the temperature; normally the pulse rises by about 10 beats/min per 1 °C) can be a clue in typhoid, brucellosis, legionellosis, leptospirosis, psittacosis, drug fever, and some viral infections.

### 21.10 Hyperthermia and drug-related fever

Always keep these in mind when the temperature is very high (>40 °C) or does not respond to antipyretics:

- **Heat stroke:** core temperature >40 °C, central nervous system dysfunction (confusion, seizures, coma); exertional or classic. Treat with rapid active cooling (cold water immersion or evaporative cooling), fluids and organ support.
- **Neuroleptic malignant syndrome:** antipsychotic exposure, rigidity ("lead pipe"), fever, autonomic instability, raised creatine kinase, altered mental state; develops over days.
- **Serotonin syndrome:** serotonergic drugs (SSRIs, MAO inhibitors, tramadol, linezolid, MDMA, triptans), agitation, tremor, clonus, hyperreflexia, diaphoresis, diarrhoea, hyperthermia; develops within hours.
- **Malignant hyperthermia:** during or after general anaesthesia (volatile agents, suxamethonium); rising end-tidal CO₂, muscle rigidity, tachycardia, fever; treat with dantrolene.
- **Thyroid storm:** hyperthyroidism with fever, tachyarrhythmia, agitation, vomiting, diarrhoea; precipitated by infection, surgery, or iodine.
- **Anticholinergic toxicity** ("hot as a hare, dry as a bone"), **sympathomimetic toxicity** (cocaine, amphetamines), **salicylate poisoning** and others.
- **Drug fever:** see above. Typical offenders: antibiotics (beta-lactams, sulphonamides), anticonvulsants (phenytoin, carbamazepine), allopurinol, antiarrhythmics (procainamide, quinidine), amphotericin, heparin, methyldopa, hydralazine, NSAIDs, chemotherapy agents and biologics.

### 21.11 Worked cases

#### Case 1: The febrile teenager with a sore throat

*A 17-year-old student has had 5 days of fever (38.9 °C), severe sore throat and extreme fatigue. Examination: exudative tonsillitis, tender posterior cervical lymphadenopathy, palpable spleen tip, and a mild periorbital swelling. He took amoxicillin from a friend and developed a diffuse maculopapular rash.*

**Reasoning.** Fever, pharyngitis, posterior cervical nodes, splenomegaly and fatigue form the classic picture of **infectious mononucleosis (EBV).** The rash after amoxicillin is common in EBV and does not indicate true penicillin allergy. The differential includes streptococcal pharyngitis (anterior cervical nodes, no cough), CMV, HIV seroconversion, toxoplasmosis, and (rarely) lymphoma.

**Next steps.** Full blood count and film (atypical lymphocytosis), monospot or EBV serology, liver function (mild hepatitis is common), throat swab if streptococcal infection is suspected. Supportive care. Advise **avoiding contact sports for several weeks** (splenic rupture risk). Return urgently for severe abdominal pain, difficulty breathing or swallowing, or bleeding.

#### Case 2: The returned traveller

*A 38-year-old man returned 12 days ago from three weeks in Ghana (no prophylaxis). He has had 2 days of fever with rigors, headache, vomiting and myalgia. Temperature 39.6 °C, pulse 108, BP 112/68. Mild jaundice, spleen tip palpable. Haemoglobin 105 g/L, platelets 62 × 10⁹/L.*

**Reasoning.** Fever in a returning traveller from sub-Saharan Africa without prophylaxis, with thrombocytopenia, anaemia, jaundice and splenomegaly: **malaria** (probably *P. falciparum*). He must be assessed for severity: conscious level, glucose, lactate, renal function, parasite density, chest X-ray.

**Action.** Urgent thick and thin films plus rapid antigen test. If *falciparum* malaria is confirmed, treat according to local guidelines (oral artemisinin-based combination for uncomplicated disease; IV artesunate for severe disease or if vomiting). Admit for monitoring, as deterioration can be rapid. Consider co-infection (typhoid, dengue, bacteraemia). Notify public-health authorities as required.

*Lesson.* A normal film does not exclude malaria: repeat at 12–24 h intervals for a total of three sets if suspicion remains.

#### Case 3: Fever of unknown origin in an older woman

*A 74-year-old woman has had 6 weeks of intermittent fever up to 38.5 °C, malaise, 6 kg weight loss and a new, persistent bilateral shoulder ache with morning stiffness. ESR 98 mm/h, CRP 120 mg/L, mild normochromic anaemia. Blood cultures, chest X-ray, urinalysis, and a CT chest/abdomen/pelvis are unremarkable. She mentions that her scalp is tender when she combs her hair and that her jaw aches when she chews tough food.*

**Reasoning.** Fever, weight loss, high inflammatory markers, proximal shoulder pain with stiffness, scalp tenderness and jaw claudication in a woman over 70: **giant cell arteritis with polymyalgia rheumatica** (a common cause of FUO in older adults). Other possibilities: lymphoma, endocarditis, TB, other vasculitis.

**Action.** Start **high-dose prednisolone immediately** (to protect vision) without waiting for biopsy; arrange temporal artery biopsy or ultrasound within days (the diagnostic yield remains good for at least 1–2 weeks of treatment). Ask about visual symptoms (amaurosis fugax, diplopia), check visual acuity and fundi, and consider imaging of large vessels (PET or MRA) for extracranial disease. Add bone and gastric protection.

#### Case 4: Neutropenic fever

*A 52-year-old woman with breast cancer, 9 days after cycle 2 of chemotherapy, feels unwell with chills. Temperature 38.4 °C, pulse 112, BP 98/58, RR 22. No localising signs except a tender erythematous area around her central venous port. Neutrophils 0.2 × 10⁹/L.*

**Reasoning.** Febrile neutropenia with possible line-related infection and early sepsis (haemodynamic compromise). This is an **oncological emergency.**

**Action.** Blood cultures (peripheral and port), lactate; **IV broad-spectrum anti-pseudomonal antibiotic within 60 minutes** according to the local neutropenic sepsis pathway; IV fluids; urine culture, chest X-ray, other cultures as indicated. Notify the oncology team; consider adding vancomycin for suspected line infection. Do not delay antibiotics for imaging or for the neutrophil count if the clinical picture is clear. Remove the port if the infection is severe or if cultures grow certain organisms.

#### Case 5: Post-operative fever

*A 68-year-old man has a temperature of 38.6 °C on day 6 after a sigmoid colectomy. He is tachycardic and has increasing abdominal pain and mild distension, with reduced bowel sounds. The wound is clean. Urine dipstick is negative. WBC 18 × 10⁹/L, CRP 220 mg/L.*

**Reasoning.** Fever at day 5–7 with increasing abdominal pain, distension and rising inflammatory markers: **anastomotic leak** or **intra-abdominal collection** until proved otherwise, rather than the "wind, water, wound" causes (chest clear, urine negative, wound clean).

**Action.** Urgent senior surgical review, resuscitation, broad-spectrum antibiotics, **CT abdomen/pelvis with contrast** (or water-soluble contrast), consider early re-operation or drainage. Check lactate and renal function. Avoid false reassurance from a clean wound or normal chest.

#### Case 6: Drug fever

*A 61-year-old woman was admitted with cellulitis and has been on IV flucloxacillin for 10 days. The cellulitis has settled, but she has persistent fever (38.2–38.8 °C) for 4 days. She looks well and has a heart rate of 82. Examination, chest X-ray, cultures and a CT scan are negative. WBC is normal with eosinophils 0.7 × 10⁹/L; ALT mildly elevated.*

**Reasoning.** A persistent fever in a patient who looks well, with relative bradycardia, eosinophilia, mildly raised liver enzymes, after a prolonged beta-lactam course, with a negative infectious work-up: **drug fever** is likely.

**Action.** Stop the suspected drug (change to an alternative agent if antibiotic therapy is still needed); fever should resolve within 48–72 hours. Document the reaction in the allergy record (as an adverse drug reaction), avoid rechallenge unless essential.

#### Case 7: A febrile infant

*A 5-week-old girl has had a temperature of 38.4 °C for 8 hours, with reduced feeding and sleepiness. She is mottled and her capillary refill is 3 seconds.*

**Reasoning.** A febrile infant under 3 months with poor feeding, drowsiness and poor perfusion has presumed **serious bacterial infection** (sepsis, meningitis, urinary tract infection) until proved otherwise. Clinical features are unreliable in this age group.

**Action.** Emergency assessment, senior paediatric input, IV access, blood (culture, count, CRP, glucose, gas), urine (catheter or suprapubic sample), consider lumbar puncture when stable, and **early empirical IV antibiotics** (including cover for *Listeria* in infants <1 month), according to local guidelines.

### 21.12 Common pitfalls

- **Treating the temperature, not the patient** (reassured by a normal reading or alarmed by a high one).
- **Relying on a normal temperature** in older, frail or immunosuppressed patients to exclude infection.
- **Measuring temperature at the wrong site** or using unreliable devices in an unwell patient.
- **Anchoring on a urinary source** in older patients because the dipstick is positive.
- **Forgetting malaria** in any traveller with fever, or stopping at a single negative film.
- **Delaying antibiotics** in suspected sepsis, meningitis or neutropenic fever while awaiting investigations.
- **Starting antibiotics blindly in a stable FUO,** masking the diagnosis.
- **Ordering large panels** without hypotheses.
- **Forgetting non-infectious causes:** drugs, VTE, vasculitis, malignancy, thyroid.
- **Not repeating the history and examination** in prolonged fever.
- **Failing to examine lines, devices and the feet/skin** in hospital patients.
- **Missing Kawasaki disease** in a child with prolonged fever.

### 21.13 Key points

- Fever is a symptom of many conditions; first decide how ill the patient is, then find the source, then consider the pattern and duration.
- Distinguish fever from hyperthermia; antipyretics do not treat hyperthermia.
- Red flags: haemodynamic compromise, altered consciousness, non-blanching rash, meningism, neutropenia, immunosuppression, travel to malaria areas, extremes of age.
- Treat sepsis, meningitis and febrile neutropenia **immediately**; do not wait for results.
- For acute fever, use history and examination by system to localise the source and order targeted tests.
- In returned travellers, think malaria first, then dengue, typhoid, rickettsia, and others; use incubation periods.
- In FUO: re-take the history, review drugs, use a stepwise investigation plan including PET/CT and tissue diagnosis, and avoid empirical therapy unless the patient deteriorates (exceptions: giant cell arteritis, critical illness).
- Fever in infants under 3 months is a high-risk presentation.
- Think of drug fever when a well-looking patient has persistent fever without a source.

### 21.14 Self-assessment

1. What is the difference between fever and hyperthermia, and why does it matter?
   *Answer: Fever is a raised hypothalamic set-point and responds to antipyretics; hyperthermia is failure of heat loss or excess heat production without a set-point change and does not respond to antipyretics. It needs cooling and treatment of the cause.*

2. Define febrile neutropenia and the time-critical action required.
   *Answer: Single oral temperature ≥38.3 °C (or ≥38.0 °C sustained for 1 hour) with ANC <0.5 × 10⁹/L (or expected to fall below it); give broad-spectrum anti-pseudomonal antibiotics within 1 hour.*

3. List four causes of fever of unknown origin from different categories.
   *Answer (examples): TB or endocarditis (infection); lymphoma or renal cell carcinoma (malignancy); giant cell arteritis or adult Still disease (inflammatory); drug fever or PE (miscellaneous).*

4. In a traveller returning from West Africa with fever, which test is essential, and how many times should negative results be repeated?
   *Answer: Thick and thin blood films with rapid antigen testing for malaria; repeat at 12–24 h intervals (three sets in total) if negative and suspicion persists.*

5. Name three features suggesting drug fever.
   *Answer (any three): patient looks well despite fever; relative bradycardia; eosinophilia; mildly raised liver enzymes; onset ~1–2 weeks after starting a drug; no source on investigation; resolves within 48–72 h of stopping the drug.*

6. A 6-week-old infant has a temperature of 38.5 °C and is feeding poorly. What is the appropriate approach?
   *Answer: Treat as possible serious bacterial infection: urgent senior assessment, full sepsis work-up (blood, urine, ± CSF), and early empirical IV antibiotics according to local guidelines.*
