# Chapter 76. Ethics and Patient-Centered Diagnosis

## Learning objectives

After studying this chapter you should be able to:

1. Apply the four principles of biomedical ethics (autonomy, beneficence, non-maleficence, justice) and relate them to virtue, duty, and relational approaches in everyday diagnostic decisions.
2. Describe what patient-centred diagnosis means: understanding the person's ideas, concerns, expectations, values and context, and sharing the diagnostic process with them.
3. Obtain valid consent for tests, disclose diagnostic information, and manage the question of how much, how and to whom to tell, including bad news and uncertain results.
4. Assess decision-making capacity and manage situations of refusal, disagreement, surrogate decision-making, minors, and advance directives.
5. Protect confidentiality and privacy, and identify the legitimate exceptions to confidentiality (risk to others, legal duty, public health, safeguarding).
6. Recognise and mitigate bias, stigma and inequity in diagnosis (sex, gender, ethnicity, language, disability, age, weight, socioeconomic status, mental illness, substance use).
7. Handle the ethical issues of testing: screening, incidental and genetic findings, overdiagnosis, resource stewardship, conflicts of interest, and the use of artificial intelligence.
8. Practise candour when diagnostic errors occur, and respond to patients' complaints and clinicians' own distress.
9. Communicate using respectful, culturally humble language, with interpreters where needed, and avoid stigmatising labels.

> **Note.** Ethical and legal standards vary between countries. References to Italian and European frameworks (such as Law 219/2017 on informed consent and advance treatment directives, the Italian Code of Medical Deontology, the General Data Protection Regulation and the Oviedo Convention) are illustrative; always follow the law, professional code and institutional policy where you practise.

## 1. Ethical foundations for diagnosis

### 1.1 The four principles

| Principle | Meaning | Diagnostic examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Respect for autonomy** | Support informed, voluntary decisions by capacitated persons | Offering a test only with understanding of what it can show; respecting refusal of HIV testing or a biopsy |
| **Beneficence** | Act for the patient's benefit | Choosing tests that will change management; timely diagnosis of treatable disease |
| **Non-maleficence** | Avoid harm | Avoiding unnecessary radiation, invasive tests, false-positive cascades, stigmatising labels |
| **Justice** | Fairness in distribution of benefits and burdens | Equal access to diagnosis; avoiding discrimination; resource stewardship |

The principles often conflict (autonomy vs beneficence when a patient declines a life-saving investigation). They provide a **language for deliberation**, not a formula.

### 1.2 Other lenses

- **Virtue ethics**: what would a compassionate, honest, courageous, practically wise clinician do? Cultivate humility, integrity and care.
- **Duty/rights-based**: duties of truthfulness, promise-keeping, confidentiality; rights to information, to refuse, to privacy.
- **Consequentialist**: weigh outcomes for the patient and for others (public health).
- **Care ethics and relational autonomy**: decisions are made in relationships (family, community); attention to vulnerability, dependence, and power.
- **Narrative ethics**: understanding the patient's story and meaning of illness.

### 1.3 A practical framework for an ethical problem

1. **Identify the facts** (clinical, patient wishes, capacity, social, legal).
2. **Identify the ethical question** (what conflicts?).
3. **List options** and consequences.
4. **Apply principles and professional standards**; consult law/policy.
5. **Seek the patient's view** and, where appropriate, family and team input.
6. **Decide, communicate, document.**
7. **Review** and seek ethics consultation if unresolved.

Four-box method (Jonsen–Siegler): medical indications; patient preferences; quality of life; contextual features.

## 2. Patient-centred diagnosis

### 2.1 What it is

Patient-centred (or person-centred) diagnosis means that the diagnostic process (history, tests, interpretation, communication) is built around **the patient's concerns, preferences, values and context**, while remaining rigorously evidence-based. It recognises that the diagnosis is a **joint product**: the patient provides the story, experience, context and values; the clinician provides expertise, probabilities and options.

### 2.2 Eliciting the patient's perspective (ICE and beyond)

| Domain | Questions |
|---|---|
| **I**deas | "What do you think is causing this?" |
| **C**oncerns | "What worries you most?" "Is there something you're afraid it might be?" |
| **E**xpectations | "What were you hoping we could do today?" |
| **F**unction | "How is this affecting your life, work, sleep, relationships?" |
| **V**alues/goals | "What matters most to you right now?" |
| **C**ontext | Family, culture, faith, finances, housing, caring responsibilities, previous experiences of healthcare |

Unspoken concerns (fear of cancer, of dementia, of losing a job, of burdening family) often drive the visit. Asking directly improves satisfaction, adherence, and reduces unnecessary requests for tests.

### 2.3 Sharing the diagnostic process

1. **Explain your thinking**: "Here's what I think is going on, and here's why."
2. **Share uncertainty**: "These are the possibilities; here's what we'll do to find out."
3. **Discuss tests**: purpose, what results might mean, risks and alternatives, what happens next.
4. **Agree on a plan**, including who will do what and by when.
5. **Check understanding** (teach-back) and emotional response.
6. **Invite questions** and revisit.

### 2.4 Diagnosis as a social and emotional event

A diagnosis can bring relief (validation), fear (prognosis), identity change (chronic illness), stigma (HIV, mental illness, obesity), and practical consequences (work, insurance, driving). Anticipate these; pace information; provide support and written materials.

### 2.5 Language and labelling

- Avoid stigmatising or blaming language ("non-compliant", "drug abuser", "frequent flyer", "obese patient"); use person-first language ("person with obesity", "person who uses drugs", "has not been able to take the medication").
- Labels travel: be careful with diagnoses that attach to the record (e.g., "borderline personality disorder", "drug-seeking"), which can lead to diagnostic overshadowing and under-treatment.
- **Diagnostic overshadowing**: attributing physical symptoms to an existing mental health, intellectual disability or substance use diagnosis, leading to missed organic disease. Counter: always assess physical symptoms on their own merit.

## 3. Consent, truth-telling and disclosure

### 3.1 Valid consent

Elements: **capacity**, **information** (nature, purpose, benefits, material risks, alternatives including no test), **voluntariness** (free from coercion), **understanding**, **decision**, documented as proportionate to risk. Verbal consent suffices for routine tests (blood tests); written/explicit consent for invasive procedures, sedation, biopsy, genetic testing, HIV testing in many jurisdictions, and research.

The **reasonable patient standard** (what a reasonable person would want to know) and the **"material risk"** concept (what this particular patient would consider significant) guide disclosure, rather than only the "reasonable doctor" standard. Italian Law 219/2017 emphasises the "time of care" (*tempo di cura*), the relationship of care and trust, and the right to know or to decline information, designating a trusted person.

### 3.2 Special aspects of consent for testing

- **Implied vs explicit**: patients should not be surprised by an HIV, drug, genetic or pregnancy test; offer opt-out testing where policy exists with clear information.
- **Opportunistic or incidental testing**: avoid "tick-box" panels done without explanation.
- **Genetic testing**: pre-test counselling about implications for relatives, insurance, employment (legal protections vary), reproductive choices, and results of uncertain significance; the right not to know.
- **Screening**: informed choice with benefits and harms (false positives, overdiagnosis) presented; participation voluntary.
- **Imaging and radiation**: explain purpose and radiation dose in understandable terms; pregnancy check.
- **Research** and **teaching**: separate consent.

### 3.3 Truth-telling and the right not to know

Honesty is the default. Patients have a right to accurate information about their condition and prognosis, and a right to decline information or delegate it to a trusted person. Withholding information in the patient's presumed interest (therapeutic privilege) is rarely justified and requires strong, time-limited reasons. In some cultures, family members expect to receive diagnoses first or shield patients; the compassionate approach is to ask the patient how they prefer information to be shared ("Some people like to know all the details, others prefer that I speak to a family member. What would you like?"). Avoid collusion that deprives a capacitated patient of the truth.

### 3.4 Breaking bad news

Frameworks: **SPIKES** (**S**etting, **P**erception, **I**nvitation, **K**nowledge, **E**mpathy/Emotions, **S**trategy/Summary) or **ABCDE** (Advance preparation, Build a relationship, Communicate well, Deal with reactions, Encourage and validate emotions).

Practical points:
- Prepare: know the facts, arrange privacy, uninterrupted time, support person, avoid corridors and phones where possible.
- "Warning shot": "I'm afraid the results are not what we hoped for."
- Use plain language; avoid euphemism and jargon; give information in small chunks.
- Allow silence, respond to emotion ("This is a lot to take in"; "I can see how worrying this is").
- Check understanding; offer next steps; provide a contact for questions.
- Document what was said and who was present.
- Remote delivery (phone/video) when unavoidable: confirm identity and privacy, ensure that the patient is not alone if possible.

### 3.5 Communicating uncertain or incidental results

Be transparent about what is known and unknown ("This test shows a small nodule that is most likely harmless, but we need to check it again in 3 months"). Avoid minimising and avoid alarmism. Offer support in the interval. See also chapters 69 and 73.

### 3.6 Disclosure to third parties (family)

With the patient's permission, share information with family. If the patient lacks capacity, share what is necessary for best-interest decisions. Respect the patient's wishes about who may know.

## 4. Capacity, refusal, minors and surrogate decision-making

### 4.1 Decision-making capacity

Capacity is **decision-specific and time-specific**. A person has capacity if they can: (1) **understand** relevant information; (2) **retain** it long enough to decide; (3) **appreciate/use and weigh** it, including consequences; (4) **communicate** a choice. Presume capacity unless there is reason to doubt. Unwise decisions do not by themselves indicate incapacity. Assess when: acute confusion, intoxication, severe psychiatric disease, intellectual disability, dementia, extremes of age, emotional distress. Support capacity (clear language, interpreter, time, quiet environment, treat reversible causes such as delirium, hypoglycaemia).

### 4.2 Refusal of diagnostic tests or treatment

A capacitated adult may refuse any test or treatment, even if the refusal may lead to death. Steps: explore reasons (fear, misunderstanding, cost, past experience, beliefs), correct misinformation, offer alternatives or staged options, explain consequences in plain terms, involve trusted people, document the discussion, keep the door open ("You can change your mind"), and provide safety-netting. In emergencies where capacity is lacking and no valid refusal is known, act in the patient's best interests.

### 4.3 When the patient lacks capacity

- Look for **advance directives** / advance care planning documents (in Italy: *DAT*, disposizioni anticipate di trattamento; the *fiduciario*; shared care planning), lasting power of attorney / health care proxy.
- If none, consult those who know the patient to determine **substituted judgement** (what the patient would want), otherwise **best interests**.
- Choose the **least restrictive** option; revisit as capacity changes.
- Emergency treatment proceeds if delay would cause serious harm and no known refusal.
- Use ethics support or the legal system for contested decisions.

### 4.4 Children and adolescents

- Parents/guardians usually give consent, but the child's **assent**, views and developing autonomy matter.
- Mature adolescents may have the right to confidential care (contraception, STI testing, mental health) in many jurisdictions; apply local law and competence assessments (Gillick competence in the UK, mature minor doctrines elsewhere). Italy generally requires parental consent for minors with growing emphasis on the minor's views; know local rules.
- Confidentiality limits: risk of serious harm, abuse; explain at the start.
- Safeguarding concerns override parental consent when needed (chapter 72).
- Disagreements between parents and clinicians about diagnosis or testing: seek mediation, ethics consultation, and court involvement only when the child's welfare is at stake.

### 4.5 Compulsory assessment and treatment

Mental health law (in Italy, *TSO*, trattamento sanitario obbligatorio) and public health law (infectious disease) allow compulsion under strict criteria (risk, lack of alternatives, proportionality, oversight). Use the least restrictive option, respect dignity, document.

## 5. Confidentiality and privacy

### 5.1 Principles

Patient information is confidential; disclosure requires consent, legal obligation, or overriding public interest. Data protection law (GDPR) governs processing, storage, and sharing of health data: lawful basis, minimisation, security, transparency, patient rights (access, rectification), and data breach notification.

### 5.2 Exceptions

- **Serious risk of harm** to the patient or others (suicidal intent, threats of violence, child abuse).
- **Legal duties**: notifiable infectious diseases, certain injuries (firearm, abuse), driving fitness reports, court orders, communicable disease contact tracing.
- **Public health**: surveillance, outbreak control (with minimum necessary data).
- **Patient safety and quality audit** (with safeguards).
- Disclose **the minimum necessary** to the appropriate person and inform the patient where possible.

### 5.3 Practical situations

- **Family members asking for information**: ask the patient; avoid confirming facts to callers.
- **Phone calls, SMS, email, portals**: verify identity; consider who may see messages; get consent for method.
- **Shared medical records**: access controls; avoid snooping.
- **Social media, public spaces, corridor conversations, whiteboards**: avoid identifiable information.
- **Teenagers and sensitive services**: explain limits; consider billing and portal visibility.
- **Employers, insurers, police**: do not release without consent or legal basis.
- **Partner notification** for HIV/STI: support patient to inform partners; public health partner services; breach only in exceptional, legally supported circumstances.
- **Genetic information** shared with at-risk relatives: encourage the patient to inform family; ethical guidance allows disclosure in rare cases with serious preventable harm when the patient refuses and law permits.

## 6. Bias, stigma and equity in diagnosis

### 6.1 Evidence of disparities

Studies show differences in diagnosis, pain management and referral by sex/gender (delayed recognition of MI and autoimmune disease in women; endometriosis diagnostic delay of 7–10 years), race and ethnicity (underestimation of pain in Black patients; pulse oximeter bias; race-based eGFR equations now removed), language (interpreter use), disability (diagnostic overshadowing), age (ageism), weight (attribution of symptoms to obesity), socioeconomic status, immigration status, mental illness, and substance use. These arise from **implicit bias**, structural barriers (access, cost, transport), non-representative research, and device/algorithm design.

### 6.2 Mitigation

1. **Awareness**: recognise your own biases (affective and stereotype-based); reflect on differences in how you treat patients.
2. **Systematic reasoning**: use checklists and structured approaches that reduce reliance on stereotypes (the same differential regardless of the patient's label).
3. **Equitable communication**: interpreters (not family members, particularly minors), plain language, health-literacy-sensitive materials, longer appointments for those who need it, accessible environments.
4. **Challenge labels**: "frequent attender", "drug-seeking", "difficult" lead to cognitive shortcuts.
5. **Know the evidence of disparities** in your specialty and adjust practice (e.g., women with chest pain, older adults with atypical presentations).
6. **Use equitable tools**: question race-based correction factors, use validated tools in diverse populations, check devices for bias.
7. **System actions**: audit outcomes by demographic groups, community engagement, diversity in the workforce, structural interventions.
8. **Cultural humility**: lifelong learning and self-critique, respecting patients as experts in their own lives, avoiding stereotypes about "cultures".

### 6.3 Social determinants of health

Housing, income, food security, education, discrimination, and environment affect risk and access. Ask about **social needs** (cannot afford medication, unsafe housing, caring burden) as part of diagnosis and management; link to social prescribing, financial counselling and community resources.

### 6.4 Specific populations

- **Migrants and refugees**: language, trauma history, unfamiliar systems; screen sensitively for TB, hepatitis, HIV, parasites, mental health; ensure rights to care.
- **People experiencing homelessness**: higher TB, hepatitis, trauma, mental illness; fragmented care; opportunistic assessment.
- **People with intellectual or physical disabilities**: reasonable adjustments, communication aids, involvement of carers, avoid overshadowing.
- **LGBTQI+ people**: respectful history-taking, use of chosen name and pronouns, awareness of specific health needs and barriers.
- **People who use drugs**: harm-reduction approach, treat pain adequately, avoid assumptions.
- **Older adults**: ageism, polypharmacy, atypical presentations, capacity support.
- **People in prison**: equivalence of care, confidentiality.

## 7. Ethical issues in testing and diagnosis

### 7.1 Overdiagnosis and resource stewardship

Using tests that do not change management consumes resources and may harm. Clinicians have a duty to patients and to the community to practise **stewardship**: avoid low-value care (chapter 69), but not at the expense of an individual's legitimate needs. Bedside rationing (decisions based on cost for an individual) is ethically fraught; use transparent, evidence-based and equitable criteria, and avoid financial incentives that bias decisions.

### 7.2 Incidental and genetic findings

Policy: discuss before testing whether results unrelated to the question might be found (incidental findings in imaging or genomic sequencing), what will be disclosed (actionable findings), and patient choices. Provide counselling and follow-up. Carrier status and findings relevant to relatives raise duties of disclosure and confidentiality. Children: genetic testing for adult-onset conditions is usually deferred until the child can decide, unless there is a medical benefit in childhood.

### 7.3 Screening and public health

Population screening may impose harms on individuals for community benefit; consent must be informed; information should be balanced, including false positives and overdiagnosis. Pressure to participate (target-driven programmes) can undermine autonomy.

### 7.4 Artificial intelligence and digital tools in diagnosis

Issues: **accuracy and bias** (training data), **transparency/explainability**, **accountability** (who is responsible for errors), **automation bias** (over-reliance), **deskilling**, **privacy and data governance**, **consent for data use**, **equity of access**, and **human oversight**. Principles: use validated tools within approved indications; the clinician retains responsibility; inform patients when AI materially informs decisions; verify outputs; document; report errors. Large language models and symptom checkers can be inaccurate or hallucinate; they do not replace examination or clinical judgment; do not input identifiable patient data into unapproved systems.

### 7.5 Conflicts of interest and commercial influences

Financial relationships with industry, testing laboratories, or imaging facilities can bias test selection or referral. Declare, avoid or manage conflicts; separate clinical decisions from financial incentives; be sceptical of marketing; use independent guidelines. Self-referral to owned facilities is regulated in many jurisdictions.

### 7.6 Direct-to-consumer testing and wearables

Patients bring results from consumer genetic, microbiome, hormone, nutritional and wearable devices. Respond respectfully: check validity and clinical relevance; avoid ordering unnecessary confirmatory cascades; discuss uncertainty; support informed decisions.

### 7.7 Dual loyalty and medico-legal reports

Clinicians writing reports for employers, insurers, courts or immigration authorities must clarify role, obtain consent, remain objective, and disclose only what is required. Fitness-to-drive and occupational assessments may conflict with the therapeutic relationship; be transparent.

### 7.8 Medical certificates, benefits and fraud

Be accurate, avoid certifying what you have not assessed, and resist pressure. Suspicion of factitious illness or malingering should be handled with sensitivity, objectivity and appropriate evaluation, not assumption.

## 8. Diagnostic error, candour and professional resilience

### 8.1 Diagnostic error as an ethical issue

Missed, wrong or delayed diagnosis harms patients and clinicians. Contributors are cognitive (biases) and systemic (communication failures, follow-up gaps). The ethical response is **honesty, responsibility and learning**.

### 8.2 Duty of candour and disclosure

When harm occurs or a significant error is discovered:

1. **Inform the patient** (and family where appropriate) promptly, in person, with a senior clinician if possible.
2. **Explain** what happened, in factual terms, without speculation or blame.
3. **Apologise** sincerely (apology laws in many jurisdictions protect expressions of regret).
4. **Describe** the immediate and future care, and remediation.
5. **Offer support** (liaison, counselling, complaints process).
6. **Document** and **report** via incident systems; participate in review.
7. **Share learning** across the organisation.

### 8.3 The second victim

Clinicians involved in errors experience guilt, shame, anxiety, and may withdraw or practise defensively. Offer peer support, debriefing, and time; avoid blame; encourage reflection; recognise systemic contributions.

### 8.4 Complaints and litigation

Respond with openness, empathy and honesty; engage with the process; seek advice from professional bodies/defence organisations; learn from the case. Good documentation and communication reduce harm and conflict.

### 8.5 Speaking up

Clinicians have a duty to raise concerns about unsafe practice, impaired colleagues, or inadequate resources. Psychological safety allows junior team members to question seniors. Provide channels for whistleblowing and protect those who use them.

## 9. Communication skills for ethical, person-centred diagnosis

| Skill | Practice |
|---|---|
| Opening | Introduce yourself, confirm identity, ask what the patient wants to discuss, set an agenda |
| Active listening | Allow the patient to speak without interruption for the first minutes (a median of about 90 seconds suffices for many patients), reflect, summarise |
| Empathy | Name the emotion ("It sounds frightening"), validate, avoid premature reassurance |
| Explaining | Chunk and check, use analogies, avoid jargon, use visual aids |
| Shared decision-making | Offer options, explain pros/cons, elicit preferences, deliberate, decide together |
| Interpreters | Use trained interpreters, speak to the patient, brief the interpreter, allow extra time |
| Difficult conversations | Anger, denial, requests for unnecessary tests, disagreement, demands for antibiotics: acknowledge, explore concerns, explain reasoning, find common ground |
| Closing | Summarise, safety-net, ask "Is there anything else?", confirm next steps |

**Dealing with a patient's demand for a test you consider inappropriate**: Explore the reason ("What do you hope the test will show?"), acknowledge the worry, explain why the test is unlikely to help and the harms, offer an alternative plan (observation, other tests, referral), and agree on a safety net; if after informed discussion the patient still wants the test and risk is low, consider it as a shared decision (documenting).

**Dealing with requests for unnecessary antibiotics, sick notes, or controlled substances**: use empathy and boundaries; explain evidence; offer symptomatic management; document.

## Worked cases

### Case 1: The family wants the diagnosis hidden

**Presentation.** Mr A., 78, from a culture in which family members often receive serious diagnoses first, has biopsy-proven lung cancer. His sons ask the oncologist not to tell him: "He will lose hope and give up."

**Analysis.** Ethical considerations: patient's autonomy and right to information; the right not to know; family's cultural expectations; best interests; risks of collusion (isolation, impaired decision-making, mistrust). Capacity is intact.

**Action.** Meet the sons to understand their fears and explain the importance of honest communication; then meet Mr A. and ask how much he wishes to know and who should be present. Use an "ask-tell-ask" approach: "Some people want all details, others prefer to leave decisions to their family. How would you like us to proceed?" He chooses to know the diagnosis and wants his sons involved in planning. If he declines information, document that and agree on how decisions will be made. The oncologist provides honest, compassionate information with hope for supportive care and agrees to revisit.

**Lessons.** Elicit the patient's own preferences; avoid unilateral concealment; respect culture without presuming.

### Case 2: Refusing a biopsy

**Presentation.** Ms B., 54, has a breast lump with suspicious imaging (BI-RADS 5). She refuses biopsy: "If it's cancer, I'd rather not know; my mother died after chemotherapy." Capacity assessment: she understands the information and consequences; she is not depressed or psychotic.

**Analysis.** Respect her capacity and right to refuse, but ensure she is informed and explore the fear. Her refusal is influenced by her mother's experience (a story, not simply misinformation). Options: provide information about modern treatment, discuss that diagnosis does not oblige treatment, offer to separate "knowing" from "treating", involve a breast nurse specialist, offer time and a second appointment, involve the trusted person, offer palliative or supportive care if she maintains the refusal.

**Action.** Several conversations over two weeks. She agrees to biopsy after being reassured that she can decide about treatment after hearing the results and that care will be tailored to her values. Diagnosis: early-stage hormone receptor-positive cancer. She chooses surgery and endocrine therapy without chemotherapy.

**Lessons.** Refusal often signals fear; patient-centred exploration and time may open options; respect the final decision.

### Case 3: A teenager and confidentiality

**Presentation.** Ms C., 15, attends alone, requests testing for chlamydia and contraception; she asks that her parents not be told. She has a 17-year-old partner.

**Analysis.** Autonomy and confidentiality for mature minors; public health benefit; potential exceptions if abuse or coercion; local law (in many jurisdictions, adolescents can consent to contraception/STI services). Assess maturity (understanding, voluntariness), exclude exploitation (age differences, coercion, power imbalance), and consider safeguarding policy.

**Action.** Explain the limits of confidentiality at the start; assess competence and sexual consent (age of consent and partner age, signs of coercion); test for STIs, offer contraception counselling, condoms, vaccination; encourage (but not force) involvement of a parent or trusted adult; ensure test results are communicated discreetly (preferred contact method). No safeguarding concerns are identified.

**Lessons.** Confidential adolescent services improve access and health; competence and safeguarding are assessed together.

### Case 4: Diagnostic overshadowing

**Presentation.** Mr D., 36, with schizophrenia and an intellectual disability, brought by carers with several weeks of "agitation", poor eating and abdominal discomfort. The ED notes "behavioural". He is sedated and sent home. Three days later he returns with obstruction caused by a large faecal impaction and sepsis.

**Analysis.** Diagnostic overshadowing: symptoms attributed to the psychiatric diagnosis. Communication barriers, clozapine/anticholinergic constipation risk, lack of structured physical assessment.

**Action (system and clinician).** Always perform a physical assessment (vital signs, abdominal examination, rectal examination, bloods) in patients with behavioural change, use communication aids/carer expertise, review medications (clozapine-induced constipation is potentially fatal), involve learning disability liaison. After this event, the unit updates its policy and conducts candid disclosure to carers.

**Lessons.** Behaviour is a symptom; treat the person as having both mental and physical health needs.

### Case 5: Incidental genetic finding

**Presentation.** Mrs E., 42, undergoes exome sequencing for unexplained developmental delay in her child. The laboratory reports a pathogenic *BRCA1* variant in the mother (found as part of trio sequencing) that is unrelated to the child's condition. She had consented to the analysis but not discussed secondary findings.

**Analysis.** Autonomy and the "right not to know" vs beneficence (actionable, preventable cancer risk). Pre-test counselling should have covered secondary findings. Now the result exists.

**Action.** Genetic counsellor meets the mother, explains that an unexpected actionable finding has been identified and asks if she wishes to know (many labs and guidelines recommend offering this possibility during consent; if she declined secondary findings, the laboratory should not have reported it). She chooses to learn; discussion of risks (breast, ovarian cancer), surveillance, risk-reducing options, cascade testing of relatives (sisters, daughter in adulthood), implications for insurance and employment (legal protections vary); psychological support. The institution revises its consent process.

**Lessons.** Plan for secondary findings before testing; counsel, support, and enable informed choices.

### Case 6: An AI tool and a discordant result

**Presentation.** Dr F. uses an approved AI-based chest X-ray triage tool that labels an image "normal". The patient, a 62-year-old smoker with 3 weeks of cough and 5 kg weight loss, has a subtle left apical opacity that Dr F. suspects on reviewing the film.

**Analysis.** Automation bias could lead to dismissal. The clinician retains responsibility; AI sensitivity for subtle lesions is limited; pre-test probability is high.

**Action.** Trust clinical judgment: request CT chest; document that AI output was reviewed but clinical suspicion prevailed; report the discordance to the AI governance team for quality monitoring. CT shows a 2 cm apical nodule with rib invasion (Pancoast tumour).

**Lessons.** AI supports but does not replace clinical reasoning; maintain scepticism; report errors.

### Case 7: Disclosing a diagnostic error

**Presentation.** Ms G., 58, was discharged from the ED with "musculoskeletal chest pain". An ECG with subtle ST depression was misread; troponin was not repeated. She re-presents the next day with an NSTEMI and mild left ventricular dysfunction. The ED consultant discovers the missed signals on review.

**Analysis.** Diagnostic error causing harm; duty of candour; patient autonomy and trust; learning culture.

**Action.** The consultant meets the patient (with a family member, a nurse and, if desired, an advocate) the same day: states facts ("When you were seen yesterday, the ECG showed changes that, in retrospect, suggested a heart problem, and we did not repeat the blood test. We should have kept you for further tests."); apologises; explains care now, offers support; informs her of the review process and how she can ask questions; files an incident report; the team conducts a no-blame review to improve ECG review and troponin protocol; supports the junior doctor involved.

**Lessons.** Prompt, honest disclosure with an apology preserves trust and meets professional duty; system learning follows.

## Common pitfalls

1. **Treating consent as a signature** rather than a conversation; ordering tests without explaining.
2. **Paternalism**: withholding information or deciding for the patient when they have capacity.
3. **Collusion with family** to hide information.
4. **Ignoring cultural and language needs**; using family members (especially children) as interpreters.
5. **Assuming incapacity** because a decision seems unwise, or assuming capacity without checking in acute illness.
6. **Breaching confidentiality** casually (corridors, calls, social media), or hiding behind it when disclosure is mandatory.
7. **Stigmatising labels and diagnostic overshadowing.**
8. **Bias**: attributing symptoms to weight, mental illness, substance use or ethnicity.
9. **Over-testing in response to pressure** or fear, or under-testing in the name of cost without transparency.
10. **Failing to plan for incidental and genetic findings.**
11. **Uncritical use of AI/commercial tools**; ignoring conflicts of interest.
12. **Not disclosing errors**; blaming individuals instead of systems.
13. **Neglecting clinician well-being** after adverse events.

## Key points

- Ethical practice in diagnosis rests on respect for autonomy, beneficence, non-maleficence and justice, applied with compassion and practical wisdom.
- Patient-centred diagnosis elicits ideas, concerns, expectations, function and values, shares reasoning and uncertainty, and builds decisions with the patient.
- Valid consent requires capacity, information, voluntariness and understanding; adults with capacity may refuse tests; surrogates use substituted judgement or best interests.
- Truth-telling is the default; respect the right not to know; break bad news with a structured, empathic approach.
- Confidentiality is strong but not absolute; disclose the minimum necessary when law or serious risk requires.
- Combat bias, stigma and inequity by structured reasoning, language access, equitable tools, and awareness of social determinants.
- Plan for incidental findings; use AI critically; manage conflicts of interest; practise stewardship.
- Disclose errors honestly, support patients and colleagues, and learn.

10. What are the steps in disclosing a diagnostic error?

**Answers**

1. Autonomy, beneficence, non-maleficence, justice. Example: a patient refuses a CT scan recommended to exclude a serious condition (autonomy vs beneficence); or ordering many tests (beneficence) vs risk of harm/overdiagnosis (non-maleficence).
2. Capacity, adequate information (nature, purpose, benefits, material risks, alternatives incl. no test), voluntariness, understanding, and a decision; documented proportionately.
3. Presume capacity; for the specific decision check ability to understand, retain, use/weigh and communicate; support and repeat if fluctuating; treat reversible causes; document.
4. Explore reasons and fears, give information and alternatives, involve trusted people, allow time, respect capacity, document, offer safety net, keep the door open.
5. Setting, Perception, Invitation, Knowledge, Empathy/Emotions, Strategy and Summary: prepare, ask what the patient knows and wants to know, give a warning shot and information in small chunks, respond to emotions, agree on a plan.
6. Serious risk of harm to self/others, legal obligation (notifiable disease, child protection, court order, driving), public health, with minimum necessary disclosure and patient informed where possible.
7. Attribution of symptoms to a pre-existing mental health, intellectual disability or substance condition leading to missed physical illness; prevent by structured physical assessment, communication adaptations, avoiding labels, and involving carers.
8. Explore their fears, explain the patient's right to information and risks of collusion, then ask the patient how much they want to know and who should be involved; respect the patient's choices (including not wishing to know).
9. Accuracy and bias, explainability, accountability, automation bias, consent/privacy for data, equity, and the need for human oversight and reporting of errors.
10. Prompt personal disclosure; factual explanation; sincere apology; description of remedial actions; support; documentation and incident reporting; system learning and feedback.
