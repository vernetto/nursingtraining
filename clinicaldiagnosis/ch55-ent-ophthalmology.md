# Chapter 55. ENT and Ophthalmology

## Learning objectives

After studying this chapter you should be able to:

1. Distinguish the causes of the red eye and identify the features that mandate urgent ophthalmology referral.
2. Approach sudden and gradual visual loss systematically and recognise the emergencies: central retinal artery occlusion, giant cell arteritis, acute angle-closure glaucoma, retinal detachment, optic neuritis and chemical injury.
3. Diagnose and manage common eyelid, conjunctival and corneal conditions, and screen for diabetic and hypertensive eye disease.
4. Classify hearing loss as conductive, sensorineural or mixed, and recognise sudden sensorineural hearing loss as an emergency.
5. Diagnose and treat acute otitis media, otitis externa, and otitis media with effusion, and recognise their complications.
6. Distinguish viral from bacterial rhinosinusitis, manage allergic rhinitis and epistaxis, and recognise complications of sinusitis.
7. Assess sore throat with validated scores, and recognise peritonsillar abscess, epiglottitis, retropharyngeal abscess and deep neck infection.
8. Evaluate dizziness of ENT origin (BPPV, vestibular neuritis, Ménière disease), hoarseness, dysphagia and neck lumps, and identify head and neck cancer warning signs.
9. Handle airway emergencies (foreign body, angioedema, stridor) in ENT practice.

> **Note on thresholds and doses.** Regimens follow commonly used guidelines (AAO-HNS, NICE, AAO, RCOphth, IDSA) at the time of writing. Use your national formulary and local resistance patterns.

# Part A: The eye

## 1. Eye examination essentials

The eye can be examined in about five minutes with a torch, a pen, an eye chart, and an ophthalmoscope. Do these in order:

1. **Visual acuity** in each eye separately, with glasses or pinhole (a pinhole improves acuity if the cause is refractive). Use a Snellen chart at 6 m, a near card, or if poor, count fingers, hand movements, light perception. **Visual acuity is the vital sign of the eye**: always record it, and a drop is the most important finding.
2. **Pupils**: size, symmetry, reaction to light (direct and consensual), and the **relative afferent pupillary defect (RAPD)** using the swinging-light test: the affected eye's pupil dilates when light swings to it (optic nerve or extensive retinal disease).
3. **Visual fields** by confrontation (finger counting in four quadrants, each eye alone); hemianopia points to a lesion behind the chiasm, bitemporal loss to the chiasm.
4. **Eye movements** and alignment (diplopia, nystagmus), light reflex position.
5. **External**: lids, lashes, lacrimal system, conjunctiva (pull down lower lid; evert upper lid if foreign body suspected), sclera, cornea (clarity, shape, fluorescein stain under blue light for abrasions or dendrites), anterior chamber (depth, hypopyon, hyphaema), iris, lens.
6. **Intraocular pressure** (if tonometer available; never in suspected open globe).
7. **Fundoscopy**: red reflex (leucocoria in a child is retinoblastoma until proven otherwise), optic disc (swelling, pallor, cupping), vessels, macula, haemorrhages, exudates, retinal detachment. A mydriatic can be used after checking for narrow angles and documenting pupil reactions.

Bedside red flags: reduced acuity not correcting with pinhole, RAPD, painful eye movements, photophobia, corneal opacity, irregular pupil, shallow anterior chamber, hypopyon or hyphaema, proptosis, fixed dilated pupil, or loss of red reflex.

## 2. The red eye

Most red eyes are benign, but a minority threaten sight. The history and four questions separate them:

1. **Is vision reduced?** (and does it correct with pinhole?)
2. **Is there pain, and what type?** (gritty, foreign body feeling suggests surface; deep aching or severe pain suggests intraocular).
3. **Is there photophobia?** (corneal or intraocular inflammation).
4. **Is there discharge?** (watery: viral, allergic; mucopurulent: bacterial).

Also examine the pattern of redness: **diffuse** (conjunctival) versus **ciliary flush** (circumcorneal, deeper, violaceous: indicates corneal, iris or glaucoma).

### 2.1 Differential diagnosis at a glance

| Condition | Vision | Pain | Discharge | Pupil | Key signs |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Viral conjunctivitis** | Normal | Gritty | Watery | Normal | Follicles, pre-auricular node, recent URI, often bilateral sequential |
| **Bacterial conjunctivitis** | Normal | Mild | Purulent, lids stuck | Normal | Papillae, no photophobia |
| **Allergic conjunctivitis** | Normal | Itch | Watery, stringy | Normal | Chemosis, bilateral, seasonal |
| **Subconjunctival haemorrhage** | Normal | None | None | Normal | Flat bright red patch, sharply demarcated |
| **Episcleritis** | Normal | Mild | None | Normal | Sectoral redness that blanches with phenylephrine |
| **Scleritis** | Normal or reduced | Severe, boring, wakes at night | None | Normal | Violaceous, deep, does not blanch; associated with rheumatoid arthritis, vasculitis |
| **Keratitis (corneal ulcer)** | Reduced | Moderate to severe | Watery or purulent | Normal or small | Corneal opacity, stains with fluorescein; contact lens wearers; hypopyon |
| **Herpes simplex keratitis** | Reduced | Moderate | Watery | Normal | Dendritic ulcer on fluorescein |
| **Herpes zoster ophthalmicus** | Variable | Severe | Variable | Variable | Dermatomal vesicles V1, Hutchinson sign (nose tip) |
| **Anterior uveitis (iritis)** | Reduced | Aching, photophobia | Watery | Small, irregular (posterior synechiae) | Ciliary flush, cells and flare in anterior chamber, keratic precipitates |
| **Acute angle-closure glaucoma** | Markedly reduced (halos) | Severe, headache, vomiting | Watery | Mid-dilated, oval, fixed | Hazy cornea, hard eye, shallow anterior chamber |
| **Endophthalmitis** | Markedly reduced | Severe | Variable | Variable | After surgery or injection or penetrating injury; hypopyon |
| **Chemical injury** | Variable | Severe | Watery | Variable | History |
| **Orbital cellulitis** | Reduced late | Painful eye movements | Variable | RAPD if severe | Proptosis, ophthalmoplegia, fever, lid swelling |
| **Blepharitis / chalazion / stye** | Normal | Mild | Crusts | Normal | Lid margin changes |

### 2.2 Management by condition

**Viral conjunctivitis (adenovirus).** Self-limiting in 1–3 weeks. Cool compresses, lubricants, hand hygiene; highly contagious (off work/school in some settings until discharge stops). Avoid steroids and unneeded antibiotics. Adenoviral keratoconjunctivitis may leave subepithelial infiltrates reducing vision for weeks.

**Bacterial conjunctivitis.** Often self-limiting within 5–7 days; topical chloramphenicol or fusidic acid or similar (per local guidelines) for severe or persistent cases. **Neonatal conjunctivitis** within the first month, or any purulent conjunctivitis with rapid onset and copious pus in an adult (gonococcal) is an emergency: systemic antibiotics, urgent ophthalmology, test for Chlamydia/Gonorrhoea.

**Allergic conjunctivitis.** Cool compresses, topical antihistamine–mast-cell stabilisers (olopatadine, ketotifen), avoid allergen; avoid long-term steroids. Vernal keratoconjunctivitis (young boys with atopy, giant papillae) and atopic keratoconjunctivitis need specialist input.

**Subconjunctival haemorrhage.** Benign; reassure. Check blood pressure and consider coagulation if recurrent. Heavy trauma with 360° haemorrhage: consider open globe.

**Episcleritis.** Self-limiting; lubricants, occasionally topical NSAID/steroid. Scleritis needs ophthalmology and systemic work-up (rheumatoid factor, ANCA, ANA), because 30–50% have an associated systemic condition, and treatment is with oral NSAID or systemic steroids/immunosuppression.

**Corneal ulcer / keratitis.** Same-day ophthalmology referral for contact lens wearers with red painful eye, any opacity on cornea, or hypopyon. Do not patch. Corneal scraping and intensive topical antibiotics (fluoroquinolone or fortified drops) are started by ophthalmology. Contact lens wearers should stop wearing lenses at the first sign of pain or redness.

**Herpes simplex keratitis.** Topical aciclovir or ganciclovir gel; avoid steroids without specialist supervision (can worsen epithelial disease).

**Herpes zoster ophthalmicus.** Oral antiviral (valaciclovir 1 g three times daily for 7 days) within 72 h; ophthalmology review within days, since it can cause keratitis, uveitis, raised intraocular pressure, retinitis and chronic neuralgia. Risk of ocular involvement is high when the nasociliary branch is affected.

**Anterior uveitis.** Pain, photophobia and ciliary injection; slit-lamp shows cells and flare. Topical steroids and cycloplegic by ophthalmology. Associations: HLA-B27 spondyloarthropathies (ankylosing spondylitis, reactive arthritis, psoriatic arthritis, inflammatory bowel disease), sarcoidosis, juvenile idiopathic arthritis (often silent: screening), TB, syphilis, herpes viruses, Behçet disease. Recurrent or bilateral uveitis warrants a work-up (HLA-B27, ACE, chest X-ray, syphilis serology, quantiferon, others by context).

**Acute angle-closure glaucoma** (see section 3.1).

**Chemical injury.** The one eye emergency where treatment comes before assessment: **irrigate immediately** with copious water or saline for at least 15–30 minutes (until pH 7–7.5 on testing with litmus paper), evert the lids, remove particles, then examine. Alkali (lime, ammonia, drain cleaner) is worse than acid. Refer immediately.

**Open globe injury.** After penetrating or high-velocity injuries (hammering metal, power tools, projectiles): pupil irregular or teardrop, prolapsed uveal tissue, hyphaema, low pressure. Do not apply pressure, do not instil drops or measure pressure; **place a rigid shield** (not a patch), keep nil by mouth, give antiemetic, tetanus, systemic antibiotics, and arrange urgent ophthalmic surgery.

**Hyphaema** (blood in anterior chamber after blunt trauma): sit upright, shield, refer; risk of rebleeding and raised intraocular pressure (sickle cell trait increases risk).

**Blowout fracture** of the orbital floor after blunt trauma: diplopia on upgaze, enophthalmos, infraorbital numbness, subcutaneous emphysema after nose blowing; CT; avoid nose blowing and refer to ENT/maxillofacial/ophthalmology.

**Eyelid conditions.** Stye (external hordeolum): warm compresses, topical antibiotic rarely needed; chalazion: warm compresses, incision and curettage if persistent; blepharitis: lid hygiene, warm compresses, and a trial of azithromycin or doxycycline for meibomian gland dysfunction; recurrent unilateral chalazion in an older adult can mimic sebaceous carcinoma (biopsy). Entropion/ectropion, ptosis (see chapter 51 for neurological causes: Horner, oculomotor palsy, myasthenia).

**Dacryocystitis.** Painful red swelling below the medial canthus; oral antibiotics, drainage if abscess, later dacryocystorhinostomy. **Nasolacrimal duct obstruction** in infants: tearing and discharge; massage; most resolve by 12 months.

**Orbital cellulitis** (post-septal): lid oedema, proptosis, painful and restricted eye movements, fever, reduced vision, colour desaturation, RAPD. A medical emergency, most commonly secondary to ethmoid sinusitis in children. Admit, contrast CT orbits and sinuses, IV antibiotics (e.g., ceftriaxone plus metronidazole, with vancomycin if MRSA risk), ophthalmology and ENT. Surgical drainage of subperiosteal or orbital abscess. **Preseptal cellulitis** (lid swelling and erythema without proptosis, pain on movement or visual change) is treated with oral antibiotics (amoxicillin–clavulanate) but must be reassessed within 24–48 hours. If in doubt, treat as orbital.

### 2.3 Rules for the primary care or emergency doctor

- **Never prescribe topical steroids** for a red eye without ophthalmology input (may worsen herpetic keratitis, glaucoma, cataract).
- **Never patch** an eye with possible infection or contact lens-related ulcer.
- **Refer same day** for: reduced vision, severe pain, photophobia, corneal opacity or staining, abnormal pupil, hypopyon, hyphaema, recent eye surgery or injection, contact lens wearer with pain, trauma, chemical injury, suspected scleritis, herpes zoster near the eye, neonatal conjunctivitis.
- **Always check visual acuity.**

## 3. Loss of vision

### 3.1 Structured approach

Ask: **Sudden or gradual? Painful or painless? Monocular or binocular? Transient or persistent? Associated symptoms?** (flashes and floaters, headache, jaw claudication, scalp tenderness, neurological symptoms, systemic illness, medications, trauma).

| Pattern | Likely causes |
|---|---|
| Transient (minutes), monocular, painless: "curtain" | Amaurosis fugax (retinal embolus or carotid disease; treat as TIA) |
| Transient visual loss, bilateral, on standing | Postural hypotension; vertebrobasilar insufficiency |
| Transient visual loss with migraine | Visual aura (bilateral, expanding scintillating scotoma, 20–30 min) |
| Sudden, painless, persistent, monocular | Retinal artery occlusion, retinal vein occlusion, retinal detachment, vitreous haemorrhage, ischaemic optic neuropathy (arteritic or non-arteritic), stroke if homonymous |
| Sudden, painful | Acute angle-closure glaucoma, optic neuritis (pain on movement), uveitis, corneal disease, endophthalmitis, giant cell arteritis (arteritic ION is usually painless in the eye but with head/jaw pain) |
| Gradual, painless | Cataract, open-angle glaucoma (peripheral field loss first), age-related macular degeneration, diabetic retinopathy, refractive error, compressive optic neuropathy (pituitary tumour, meningioma) |
| Binocular visual field defect | Homonymous hemianopia (post-chiasmal lesion: stroke, tumour); bitemporal hemianopia (chiasm: pituitary adenoma) |
| Visual loss with normal appearing eye and normal fundus | Optic neuritis (retrobulbar), cortical blindness, functional visual loss, toxic/nutritional optic neuropathy |

### 3.2 The emergencies

**Central retinal artery occlusion (CRAO).** Sudden, profound, painless monocular visual loss (often counting fingers or worse), RAPD, pale retina with a cherry-red spot at the fovea, "box-carting" of arterioles. Cause: embolus from carotid atheroma or heart; giant cell arteritis in older patients. Treat as stroke: window for intervention is hours (some centres consider thrombolysis within 4.5 h, intra-arterial in selected cases); urgent stroke pathway, carotid imaging, ECG/echo, and **exclude GCA** (ESR, CRP, platelets, symptoms). Ocular massage and lowering intraocular pressure have little evidence.

**Retinal vein occlusion.** Sudden painless blurred vision; "blood and thunder" fundus with widespread haemorrhages (central) or sector (branch). Risk factors: hypertension, diabetes, hyperlipidaemia, glaucoma, thrombophilia, hyperviscosity, oral contraceptive, age. Treatment of macular oedema with intravitreal anti-VEGF or steroid; panretinal photocoagulation for neovascularisation; treat systemic risk factors.

**Giant cell arteritis (GCA).** Age > 50, new headache, scalp tenderness, jaw claudication, polymyalgia rheumatica symptoms, fever, amaurosis fugax, diplopia, sudden visual loss (arteritic anterior ischaemic optic neuropathy: pale swollen disc, chalky white). ESR often > 50 mm/h, CRP raised, thrombocytosis; but normal ESR in up to 5–10%. **If visual symptoms or high suspicion: start prednisolone 40–60 mg daily (or IV methylprednisolone 500 mg–1 g for 3 days in visual loss) immediately, before temporal artery biopsy or ultrasound**, which can be done within days to weeks. Fellow eye involvement can occur within days if untreated. Add gastric and bone protection, and consider tocilizumab as steroid-sparing therapy. See chapter 52.

**Non-arteritic anterior ischaemic optic neuropathy (NAION).** Sudden painless visual loss, usually on waking, with swollen hyperaemic disc, "disc at risk" (small crowded optic disc), vascular risk factors; associated with PDE-5 inhibitors and amiodarone in some. No proven treatment; control risk factors; exclude GCA.

**Acute angle-closure glaucoma.** Typically hypermetropic, older, Asian or female. Sudden severe unilateral eye pain, headache, nausea and vomiting, blurred vision with haloes, red eye, steamy cornea, fixed mid-dilated oval pupil, firm eye on palpation (compare with other eye), shallow anterior chamber. Precipitants: dim light, mydriatic drugs, anticholinergics, some antidepressants, topiramate. **Emergency**: lower pressure with acetazolamide 500 mg IV/oral, topical beta-blocker, topical pilocarpine, topical alpha-agonist (brimonidine) and steroid, analgesia and antiemetic, then definitive laser peripheral iridotomy. Untreated, permanent blindness can occur within 24–48 hours.

**Retinal detachment.** Preceded by flashes of light (photopsia) and new floaters ("shower" or "cobweb"), then a peripheral "curtain" or shadow that spreads. Risk factors: myopia, previous cataract surgery, trauma, family history, posterior vitreous detachment. Fundus shows grey elevated retina; Shafer sign (pigmented cells in vitreous). Needs same-day or next-day vitreoretinal assessment. If the macula is still attached, urgent surgery preserves central vision. Advise sitting upright or positioning as instructed.

**Vitreous haemorrhage.** Sudden painless floaters or "cobwebs" and reduced vision; diabetic retinopathy with neovascularisation, retinal tear, trauma. Refer urgently.

**Optic neuritis.** Subacute unilateral loss over hours to days, pain on eye movement, colour desaturation (red), RAPD, central scotoma, normal or swollen disc (disc normal in 2/3). Young adults, more in women. Associated with multiple sclerosis (MRI brain and orbits), NMO spectrum disorder (AQP4 antibody), MOG-antibody disease, sarcoid, infection. IV methylprednisolone 1 g for 3 days speeds recovery but doesn't change final vision; urgent neurology referral and MRI.

**Papilloedema.** Bilateral optic disc swelling from raised intracranial pressure; vision initially normal with enlarged blind spot and transient visual obscurations; headache worse lying down or on straining, nausea, pulsatile tinnitus, diplopia (VI palsy). Causes: mass, idiopathic intracranial hypertension (young obese women), venous sinus thrombosis, hydrocephalus, accelerated hypertension. Urgent neuroimaging, then lumbar puncture with opening pressure if imaging is normal. See chapter 41.

**Stroke affecting vision.** Sudden homonymous hemianopia, with or without other neurological signs (posterior cerebral artery territory). Manage as stroke.

**Functional visual loss.** Diagnosis of exclusion; tubular fields, inconsistent examination findings, normal optokinetic response with "blindness". Do not dismiss; sensitive explanation.

### 3.3 Gradual vision loss

**Cataract.** Lens opacity; blurred vision, glare, reduced contrast, myopic shift. Risk factors: age, diabetes, steroids, smoking, UV, trauma, uveitis. Treatment: phacoemulsification with intraocular lens implant when visual disability affects daily life; one of the safest and most effective operations. **Leucocoria in a child needs urgent referral (retinoblastoma)**.

**Primary open-angle glaucoma.** Silent, progressive optic neuropathy with raised intraocular pressure in most; cupped discs, arcuate visual field loss, often discovered at routine optometry. Risk factors: age, family history, African ancestry, myopia, steroid use, diabetes. Treatment: prostaglandin analogue drops (first-line), beta-blockers, alpha agonists, carbonic anhydrase inhibitors, laser trabeculoplasty or surgery. Patients should not stop drops.

**Age-related macular degeneration (AMD).** Leading cause of blindness in older adults in high-income countries. Dry AMD (drusen, geographic atrophy) is slowly progressive; wet (neovascular) AMD causes sudden central distortion (metamorphopsia) and central scotoma. Use an Amsler grid. **Wet AMD needs urgent referral** (within days): intravitreal anti-VEGF (aflibercept, ranibizumab, faricimab) preserves vision. AREDS2 supplements for intermediate dry AMD; smoking cessation.

**Diabetic retinopathy.** See section 4.

**Hypertensive retinopathy.** Arteriolar narrowing, AV nipping, flame haemorrhages, cotton-wool spots, hard exudates, optic disc swelling in malignant hypertension. Treat the blood pressure.

**Refractive error.** The most common cause of reduced vision worldwide; test with pinhole; children need cycloplegic refraction; amblyopia ("lazy eye") is treatable if detected before about age 7–8.

**Compressive optic neuropathy and chiasmal lesions.** Gradual painless central or bitemporal loss; pale discs. MRI. Pituitary tumours (check prolactin, TSH, cortisol, IGF-1; see chapter 48).

## 4. Eye disease in systemic illness

- **Diabetes.** Retinopathy is the leading cause of blindness in working-age adults. Stages: non-proliferative (microaneurysms, dot-blot haemorrhages, hard exudates, cotton-wool spots, venous beading), proliferative (new vessels at the disc or elsewhere, vitreous haemorrhage, traction detachment), and **diabetic macular oedema** (the commonest cause of vision loss in type 2). **Screen at diagnosis in type 2, within 5 years in type 1 (from age 12), then every 1–2 years**; more often in pregnancy. Treatment: glycaemic and blood pressure control, lipid control (fenofibrate has benefit in some), laser photocoagulation, intravitreal anti-VEGF/steroid, vitrectomy. Rapid reduction of HbA1c can transiently worsen retinopathy; refer pregnant patients early.
- **Hypertension**: retinopathy, retinal vein/artery occlusion, ischaemic optic neuropathy.
- **Thyroid eye disease** (Graves orbitopathy): lid retraction, lid lag, proptosis, periorbital oedema, diplopia, exposure keratopathy, compressive optic neuropathy (loss of colour vision, RAPD: emergency). Smoking cessation, euthyroid state, selenium in mild disease, steroids, teprotumumab, orbital radiotherapy or decompression. See chapter 48.
- **Autoimmune/inflammatory disease**: uveitis, scleritis, dry eye in Sjögren.
- **Infection**: CMV retinitis in advanced HIV (floaters, visual field loss; ganciclovir); syphilis (any uveitis pattern); toxoplasma; toxocara; TB.
- **Drugs**: hydroxychloroquine (bull's-eye maculopathy; screen after 5 years), ethambutol (optic neuropathy; check colour vision), amiodarone, steroids (cataract, glaucoma), tamsulosin (floppy iris during cataract surgery), vigabatrin (field defects), topiramate (angle closure), PDE-5 inhibitors, anticholinergics.
- **Neurological**: ptosis and diplopia (myasthenia, cranial nerve palsy, Horner), nystagmus, papilloedema.

## 5. Ophthalmology in children

- **Red reflex** examination in all newborns and at the 6-week check; absent or white reflex needs urgent referral (cataract, retinoblastoma, retinopathy of prematurity sequelae).
- **Squint (strabismus)**: refer any fixed squint at any age; intermittent squint after 3 months of age; amblyopia treatment (patching, glasses) works best early. A new squint in an older child needs neurological examination. Normal in newborns up to 3 months.
- **Screening**: visual acuity checks from age 4–5; family history of retinoblastoma; premature infants (< 32 weeks or < 1500 g) need retinopathy of prematurity screening.
- **Congenital nasolacrimal duct obstruction**: see above.
- **Leucocoria, nystagmus, absent fixation by 6–8 weeks, eye rubbing, photophobia, watery eyes in infants** (congenital glaucoma: large cloudy cornea) all need urgent assessment.
- **Non-accidental injury**: retinal haemorrhages in infants (especially extensive, multilayered) may indicate abusive head trauma.

# Part B: Ear, nose and throat

## 6. The ear

### 6.1 Examination

Inspect the pinna and post-auricular area; pull the pinna back and up (down in children under 3) to straighten the canal; otoscopy: canal skin, discharge, wax, tympanic membrane colour, translucency, position of the light reflex, landmarks (handle of malleus, pars tensa, pars flaccida), bulging or retraction, perforation, effusion level, air bubbles. Pneumatic otoscopy checks movement. **Tuning fork tests**: Rinne (air conduction vs bone conduction; positive if air > bone) and Weber (lateralises to the ear with conductive loss or away from the ear with sensorineural loss). Formal audiometry and tympanometry confirm.

### 6.2 Acute otitis media (AOM)

Most common in children 6 months to 3 years. Acute onset ear pain (in infants: irritability, pulling the ear, fever, poor feeding), bulging opaque erythematous tympanic membrane with reduced mobility, with or without otorrhoea after perforation. Often follows viral upper respiratory infection. Causes: *Streptococcus pneumoniae*, non-typeable *Haemophilus influenzae*, *Moraxella catarrhalis*, viruses.

Management:

- **Pain relief** (paracetamol, ibuprofen) is the priority.
- **Watchful waiting** for 48–72 hours is appropriate for children over 2 years with mild unilateral disease and no systemic upset, with a safety net and, in some places, a delayed prescription.
- **Antibiotics** for children under 6 months, under 2 years with bilateral disease, systemic illness, otorrhoea, or symptoms persisting over 3 days: amoxicillin (e.g., 40–90 mg/kg/day in divided doses depending on guideline) for 5–7 days; amoxicillin–clavulanate or cefuroxime for treatment failure; macrolide or other if penicillin allergic. Shorter courses (5 days) are as effective for those over 2 years with non-severe disease.
- **Recurrent AOM** (≥ 3 in 6 months or ≥ 4 in 12 months): consider grommets (tympanostomy tubes).
- **Complications**: perforation (usually heals), mastoiditis (post-auricular swelling and erythema, protruding pinna, fever: admit, CT, IV antibiotics, ENT), facial palsy, labyrinthitis, meningitis, brain abscess, lateral sinus thrombosis, petrous apicitis, cholesteatoma.

### 6.3 Otitis media with effusion (glue ear)

Fluid behind an intact tympanic membrane without acute infection. Common in children aged 2–7, causes conductive hearing loss of 20–40 dB, speech and language delay, school difficulty, behavioural issues. Dull, retracted, amber or grey membrane; type B tympanogram (flat). Most resolve within 3 months; **watchful waiting for 3 months** then hearing test; if persistent and hearing loss > 25–30 dB, consider grommets (with or without adenoidectomy in older children). Down syndrome and cleft palate children need audiological follow-up. Unilateral persistent effusion in an adult: **examine the nasopharynx** (nasopharyngeal carcinoma, especially in people of southern Chinese or North African descent).

### 6.4 Otitis externa

Painful ear with itching, discharge, tenderness on moving the pinna or tragus, oedematous canal, debris. Causes: *Pseudomonas*, *S. aureus*, fungi (*Aspergillus*, *Candida*); water exposure, trauma, eczema, hearing aids. Management: aural toilet (microsuction), topical antibiotic–steroid drops (ciprofloxacin–dexamethasone; or per local guidelines), wick if canal swollen, keep dry, analgesia; antifungal drops if fungal. **Malignant (necrotising) otitis externa**: elderly, diabetic or immunosuppressed patient with severe persistent otalgia, granulation tissue at the bony–cartilaginous junction, cranial nerve palsy (VII); *Pseudomonas*; ESR/CRP, CT/MRI/nuclear imaging; prolonged anti-pseudomonal antibiotics; mortality is significant.

**Perforated tympanic membrane**: most heal; keep dry; do not syringe; if ototoxic drops are considered, check that the membrane is intact (aminoglycoside drops are ototoxic with perforation; quinolone drops are preferred).

### 6.5 Cholesteatoma and chronic suppurative otitis media

Chronic foul-smelling painless otorrhoea, conductive hearing loss, attic retraction pocket or marginal perforation with white keratin debris. Erodes ossicles and bone; complications: facial palsy, labyrinthine fistula (vertigo on pressure), meningitis, brain abscess. Needs ENT and CT/MRI; surgery.

### 6.6 Wax, foreign bodies and trauma

Impacted wax: softening drops (olive oil, sodium bicarbonate) for several days; irrigation or microsuction when no perforation or previous ear surgery. Foreign bodies: live insect (kill with oil before removal), button batteries (**urgent removal**: caustic injury in hours), vegetable matter that swells in water (do not irrigate). **Auricular haematoma** after blunt trauma: drain to avoid cauliflower ear. **Barotrauma**: pain after flight/diving; decongestants, Valsalva; perforation, round window rupture requires ENT.

### 6.7 Hearing loss

**Classification and clues.**

| Type | Mechanism | Rinne | Weber | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Conductive | Outer/middle ear | Bone > air (negative) in affected ear | Towards affected ear | Wax, otitis media with effusion, perforation, otosclerosis, ossicular disruption, cholesteatoma |
| Sensorineural | Cochlea/nerve | Air > bone (positive, both reduced) | Away from affected ear | Presbycusis, noise, ototoxic drugs, Ménière disease, acoustic neuroma, sudden SNHL, congenital |
| Mixed | Both | | | Advanced otosclerosis, chronic otitis media + presbycusis |

**Key causes.**

- **Presbycusis**: symmetric high-frequency loss with difficulty in noisy environments; common; hearing aids; associated with cognitive decline and isolation, so address it.
- **Noise-induced**: notch at 4 kHz; prevention.
- **Otosclerosis**: young adults, conductive loss, familial, Schwartze sign, tinnitus; stapedectomy or hearing aids.
- **Ototoxic drugs**: aminoglycosides, loop diuretics (high dose), cisplatin, salicylates (reversible), macrolides (rare).
- **Ménière disease**: episodic vertigo (20 minutes to 12 hours), fluctuating low-frequency hearing loss, tinnitus, aural fullness. Low-salt diet, diuretics, betahistine (limited evidence), intratympanic steroids/gentamicin, surgery.
- **Vestibular schwannoma (acoustic neuroma)**: asymmetrical sensorineural hearing loss, tinnitus, imbalance; large tumours cause facial numbness (V) and weakness (VII); MRI with gadolinium for any unilateral or asymmetrical SNHL or unilateral tinnitus.
- **Congenital**: genetic (connexin 26), CMV, rubella, prematurity; universal newborn hearing screening is used in many countries.

**Sudden sensorineural hearing loss (SSNHL).** Loss of ≥ 30 dB over at least 3 contiguous frequencies within 72 hours, usually unilateral. Cause often unidentified (viral, vascular, autoimmune). It is an **otological emergency**: urgent audiogram; **systemic corticosteroids** (prednisolone 1 mg/kg up to 60 mg daily for 7–14 days, ideally within 2 weeks) or intratympanic steroids; MRI to rule out retrocochlear lesions; exclude conductive causes (wax, effusion) which must not be mistaken for SSNHL. About one-third recover completely, one-third partially.

**Tinnitus.** Pulsatile tinnitus (synchronous with the pulse) needs vascular imaging (carotid disease, AV malformation, dural fistula, glomus tumour, idiopathic intracranial hypertension). Unilateral or asymmetrical tinnitus: audiogram and MRI. Bilateral non-pulsatile tinnitus with hearing loss: management with sound therapy, cognitive behavioural therapy, hearing aids; reassurance. Consider depression and anxiety.

### 6.8 Vertigo and dizziness of ear origin

See also chapter 24. Peripheral vestibular causes:

- **Benign paroxysmal positional vertigo (BPPV)**: brief (< 1 minute) spinning triggered by head position (turning in bed, looking up); no hearing loss; **Dix–Hallpike test** reproduces vertigo with torsional, upbeating nystagmus (posterior canal, 85%) after a latency of seconds that fatigues. Treatment: **Epley manoeuvre** (canalith repositioning), 80–90% success; supine roll test for lateral canal. Vestibular suppressants are not helpful.
- **Vestibular neuritis**: acute prolonged vertigo (days), nausea, vomiting, unidirectional horizontal-torsional nystagmus, no hearing loss, positive head-impulse test (corrective saccade). Labyrinthitis if hearing loss too. Short course of vestibular suppressants (prochlorperazine) for 1–3 days only, early mobilisation and vestibular rehabilitation, steroids debated.
- **Central causes** (stroke, especially posterior circulation; cerebellar haemorrhage; MS): the **HINTS** examination in acute continuous vertigo with nystagmus (normal head impulse test, direction-changing nystagmus, skew deviation) suggests central cause, but is only valid in trained hands in patients with continuous vertigo and spontaneous nystagmus. Red flags: severe headache, neck pain, new neurological signs, inability to walk or sit unaided, vascular risk factors. MRI is more sensitive than CT for posterior fossa stroke.

## 7. The nose and sinuses

### 7.1 Rhinitis

- **Viral rhinitis (common cold)**: nasal obstruction, rhinorrhoea, sneezing, sore throat, 7–10 days. Symptomatic care: saline, analgesics, decongestants short-term (not > 5–7 days: rhinitis medicamentosa), no antibiotics.
- **Allergic rhinitis**: sneezing, watery rhinorrhoea, itch (nose, eyes, palate), obstruction, pale boggy mucosa, "allergic salute," associated asthma and eczema. Seasonal or perennial. Management: allergen avoidance, saline irrigation, **intranasal corticosteroids** (first-line for moderate to severe disease: fluticasone, mometasone, budesonide), second-generation oral antihistamines, intranasal antihistamines (azelastine), montelukast less so (neuropsychiatric warning), allergen immunotherapy (sublingual or subcutaneous) for selected patients. Avoid sedating antihistamines in children and older adults; avoid long-term decongestants.
- **Non-allergic rhinitis (vasomotor, drug-induced, hormonal, gustatory, atrophic)**: ask about ACE inhibitors, beta-blockers, alpha-blockers, PDE-5 inhibitors, oral contraceptives, intranasal cocaine, and chronic decongestant use.
- **Unilateral nasal obstruction or discharge**: foreign body (children), deviated septum, polyp, tumour (inverted papilloma, squamous carcinoma, adenocarcinoma in wood workers), CSF rhinorrhoea. Unilateral purulent discharge in a child is a foreign body until proven otherwise. Button batteries and magnets: emergency removal.

### 7.2 Rhinosinusitis

**Acute rhinosinusitis.** Symptoms: nasal obstruction or purulent discharge plus facial pain/pressure or reduced smell, < 12 weeks. 98% are viral. **Suspect bacterial sinusitis** if: symptoms persist ≥ 10 days without improvement, or severe onset (fever ≥ 39 °C, purulent discharge, facial pain for 3–4 consecutive days), or "double worsening" (initial improvement then deterioration). Management: analgesia, saline irrigation, intranasal steroid; watchful waiting; antibiotics (amoxicillin, or amoxicillin–clavulanate; doxycycline; phenoxymethylpenicillin per local guidance) for severe or persistent bacterial cases for 5–7 days. Do not request imaging for uncomplicated disease.

**Complications** (more common in children and adolescents with ethmoid disease): orbital cellulitis and abscess, subperiosteal abscess, cavernous sinus thrombosis (bilateral eye signs, ophthalmoplegia, papilloedema, fever, meningism), meningitis, epidural or subdural empyema, brain abscess, osteomyelitis of the frontal bone (Pott puffy tumour: forehead swelling). Red flags: periorbital oedema, displaced eye, double vision, severe frontal headache, neurological signs, meningism, drowsiness. Admit, contrast CT/MRI, IV antibiotics, surgery.

**Chronic rhinosinusitis.** ≥ 12 weeks of two or more of: obstruction, discharge, facial pain, reduced smell, with objective evidence of inflammation. With or without polyps. Treatment: saline irrigation, intranasal steroids, short courses of oral steroids for polyps, surgery (FESS); biologics (dupilumab, omalizumab, mepolizumab) for severe type 2 disease. Aspirin-exacerbated respiratory disease (Samter triad: polyps, asthma, aspirin sensitivity). **Unilateral symptoms, bleeding, or neurological or ocular features** raise suspicion of tumour or fungal sinusitis. **Invasive fungal sinusitis** (mucormycosis) in diabetic ketoacidosis or immunocompromised patients: black necrotic turbinate, facial pain, orbital signs; emergency debridement and amphotericin.

### 7.3 Epistaxis

90% anterior from Kiesselbach plexus. Causes: digital trauma, dry air, rhinitis, anticoagulants and antiplatelets, hypertension (usually an aggravator rather than cause), hereditary haemorrhagic telangiectasia (HHT: recurrent spontaneous bleeding, mucocutaneous telangiectasia, family history), clotting disorders, tumours (juvenile nasopharyngeal angiofibroma in adolescent males), cocaine, nasal foreign body.

Management:

1. Sit upright, leaning forward; **pinch the soft part of the nose firmly for 10–15 minutes** without releasing; spit blood out; cold compress.
2. If persisting: topical vasoconstrictor (oxymetazoline) and/or tranexamic acid topical or oral; identify the bleeding point; silver nitrate cautery (one side only); anterior packing (dissolvable, inflatable balloons, or ribbon gauze).
3. Posterior bleeding (blood in throat despite anterior packing): posterior balloon or double balloon, admit, ENT, consider sphenopalatine artery ligation or embolisation.
4. Assess haemodynamics, give IV fluids, check FBC, coagulation, group and save in significant bleeds; review anticoagulants (do not stop abruptly without balancing thrombotic risk).
5. Look for hypertensive crisis, bleeding disorders (children with recurrent epistaxis plus easy bruising), and consider HHT.

### 7.4 Smell and taste

Loss of smell (anosmia) causes: viral (post-COVID, other upper respiratory infection), chronic sinusitis and polyps, head trauma, neurodegenerative disease (Parkinson, Alzheimer disease, often early), tumours (olfactory groove meningioma), drugs, Kallmann syndrome (with hypogonadism). Most post-viral anosmia recovers within weeks to months; olfactory training helps. Safety counselling (gas, smoke, spoiled food).

### 7.5 Nasal trauma

Examine for septal haematoma (dark, boggy, bulging swelling of the septum: drain urgently to avoid cartilage necrosis and saddle deformity). Fractured nasal bones: reduce within 5–10 days after swelling resolves. Look for CSF rhinorrhoea (clear fluid, halo sign on a tissue, beta-2 transferrin) and facial fractures.

## 8. The mouth, throat and neck

### 8.1 Sore throat

Most are viral. Group A beta-haemolytic streptococcus (GAS) causes 5–15% of adult and 20–30% of child cases.

**Clinical scores.**

- **Centor** (adults): tonsillar exudate, tender anterior cervical nodes, fever by history, absence of cough: 1 point each.
- **McIsaac/modified Centor**: add age (3–14 years +1; 15–44 0; ≥ 45 −1). Scores of 0–1: no test or antibiotics; 2–3: rapid antigen test or culture (or delayed prescription); ≥ 4: consider test and treat empirically. In practice, use local guidance (e.g., FeverPAIN in the UK). See chapter 70.

**Treatment of GAS pharyngitis**: penicillin V 10 days (or amoxicillin once daily 10 days); cephalosporin or macrolide in allergy (clindamycin or azithromycin as alternative). The main aim is symptomatic relief and prevention of rheumatic fever (very rare in high-income settings; relevant in endemic regions), not prevention of peritonsillar abscess. Analgesia (paracetamol, ibuprofen), fluids, salt-water gargles. Steroids (single dose dexamethasone) shorten pain in severe cases. Avoid amoxicillin in suspected infectious mononucleosis (rash).

**Infectious mononucleosis (EBV).** Teenagers and young adults; fatigue, exudative pharyngitis, posterior cervical lymphadenopathy, splenomegaly, palatal petechiae. Atypical lymphocytes, positive monospot, EBV serology. Avoid contact sports for 3–4 weeks (splenic rupture risk).

**Diphtheria** (grey adherent pseudomembrane, bull neck, toxin-mediated myocarditis and neuropathy): rare, in unvaccinated or travel-related; antitoxin and penicillin/erythromycin; notify.

**Peritonsillar abscess (quinsy).** Severe unilateral sore throat, trismus, "hot potato" voice, uvula deviated to the contralateral side, unilateral palatal swelling, drooling, fever. Needle aspiration or incision and drainage, IV antibiotics (benzylpenicillin plus metronidazole), steroids, analgesia, hydration. Tonsillectomy considered for recurrent episodes.

**Parapharyngeal and retropharyngeal abscess.** Children under 5 (retropharyngeal) with fever, neck stiffness or torticollis, drooling, stridor, muffled voice, refusal to swallow; adults with deep neck infection after dental or pharyngeal infection or foreign body. Lateral neck X-ray (widened prevertebral soft tissue) or CT with contrast. IV antibiotics and surgical drainage. **Ludwig angina** (bilateral submandibular and sublingual cellulitis from lower molar infection): rapidly progressive floor-of-mouth swelling, tongue elevation, trismus, stridor; airway first (anaesthetic and ENT), IV antibiotics, drainage.

**Acute epiglottitis / supraglottitis.** Now rare in children thanks to Hib vaccination; now more often in adults. Rapid onset of severe sore throat with little to see in the mouth, muffled "hot potato" voice, drooling, dysphagia, stridor, tripod position, fever. **Do not examine the throat with a spatula, do not lie flat, do not agitate**. Secure the airway in theatre by a senior anaesthetist with ENT surgeon present; IV ceftriaxone; steroids. See chapter 40.

**Croup (laryngotracheobronchitis).** Children 6 months to 3 years; barking cough, hoarseness, inspiratory stridor, preceded by coryza; parainfluenza. Dexamethasone 0.15–0.6 mg/kg single dose orally (even in mild cases), nebulised adrenaline for moderate to severe stridor at rest, humidified air is not helpful; admit severe cases or if adrenaline is required (observe 2–4 hours for rebound).

### 8.2 Oral conditions

- **Aphthous ulcers**: recurrent painful round ulcers with grey base and red halo; trauma, stress, deficiencies of iron, folate and B12, coeliac disease, inflammatory bowel disease, Behçet disease (also genital ulcers, uveitis). Topical steroid/ antiseptic/ analgesic; any ulcer persisting > 3 weeks needs biopsy.
- **Oral candidiasis**: white plaques that scrape off; inhaled steroids, antibiotics, diabetes, HIV, chemotherapy, dentures. Nystatin or miconazole gel; fluconazole systemic.
- **Herpes simplex gingivostomatitis**: children; fever, vesicles on gums and oral mucosa; aciclovir in severe cases; fluids.
- **Leukoplakia and erythroplakia**: persistent white or red patches that cannot be wiped off: premalignant; biopsy; erythroplakia has a high malignant potential. Cause: tobacco, alcohol, HPV.
- **Oral cancer**: ulcer, lump or white/red patch not healing within 3 weeks, painless induration, loose teeth, numbness, unexplained neck node. Risk factors: tobacco (including smokeless), betel quid (pan), alcohol, HPV16 (oropharyngeal), immunosuppression, poor oral hygiene. Early referral.
- **Dental abscess and odontogenic infection**: drain; metronidazole plus penicillin; spreading infection to the floor of the mouth or neck needs admission.
- **Salivary gland disorders**: sialolithiasis (pain and swelling of the submandibular gland on eating), sialadenitis (bacterial: pus from duct; antibiotic, hydration, sialogogues), mumps (parotitis, vaccine-preventable), Sjögren syndrome, tumours (most parotid tumours are benign pleomorphic adenomas; facial nerve palsy suggests malignancy).
- **Burning mouth syndrome**, geographic tongue, black hairy tongue, glossitis (B12, iron deficiency), angular cheilitis.

### 8.3 Hoarseness and voice

Hoarseness (dysphonia) lasting more than 3–4 weeks in an adult needs **laryngoscopy**, especially in smokers or heavy drinkers or those with dysphagia, stridor, haemoptysis, neck mass, or weight loss. Causes: acute laryngitis (viral, voice overuse), vocal cord nodules and polyps, reflux, hypothyroidism, **recurrent laryngeal nerve palsy** (lung apex tumour, thyroid surgery, thyroid cancer, mediastinal mass, aortic aneurysm), laryngeal cancer, neurological disease (Parkinson, myasthenia, ALS), inhaled steroids (candida), vocal cord palsy after intubation. Unexplained hoarseness in a child needs referral.

### 8.4 Dysphagia and globus

**Oropharyngeal dysphagia** (difficulty initiating swallow, coughing or choking, nasal regurgitation): stroke, Parkinson, motor neuron disease, myasthenia, bulbar palsy, head and neck cancer, Zenker diverticulum, post-radiotherapy. Speech and language therapist assessment, videofluoroscopy, nutrition, aspiration risk.

**Oesophageal dysphagia**: see chapter 47 (carcinoma, stricture, achalasia, eosinophilic oesophagitis, pill oesophagitis).

**Globus** (sensation of a lump in the throat that eases when swallowing): common, usually benign (reflux, anxiety, muscle tension); examine neck and throat, and refer if dysphagia (true), odynophagia, weight loss, pain, neck mass, voice change, or risk factors.

### 8.5 Neck lumps

Approach by age, site and tempo (chapter 31).

- **Children**: reactive lymphadenopathy (most), lymphadenitis (staph, strep, atypical mycobacteria, *Bartonella*), thyroglossal duct cyst (midline, moves with tongue protrusion), branchial cleft cyst (anterior border of sternocleidomastoid), cystic hygroma, dermoid, lymphoma, rhabdomyosarcoma, neuroblastoma.
- **Adults** under 40: reactive nodes, infection (EBV, CMV, toxoplasma, TB, HIV), lymphoma, thyroid nodules.
- **Adults over 40, smokers and drinkers**: **metastatic squamous carcinoma until proven otherwise**: hard, painless, fixed node in the upper or mid neck; look for primary in the oral cavity, oropharynx (tonsil, base of tongue; HPV-related), nasopharynx, larynx, hypopharynx; skin of scalp and face; thyroid. Check for hoarseness, dysphagia, otalgia (referred), unilateral serous otitis media, epistaxis, nasal obstruction (nasopharynx).
- **Thyroid lump** (see chapter 48): ultrasound; fine-needle aspiration; hoarseness, rapid growth, hard fixed nodule, node involvement are red flags.
- **Salivary gland lump**.
- **Supraclavicular node (Virchow)**: abdominal or thoracic malignancy.

Investigation: ultrasound with FNA or core biopsy; flexible nasendoscopy; CT/MRI; PET-CT for unknown primary. **Do not perform open excision biopsy of a suspicious neck node first** (compromises later surgical management); FNA/core biopsy first.

### 8.6 Head and neck cancer: warning signs

Symptoms lasting > 3 weeks: persistent hoarseness, non-healing mouth ulcer or white/red patch, unexplained neck lump, persistent unilateral sore throat or otalgia with a normal ear examination, dysphagia, unilateral nasal obstruction or bleeding, unexplained loose teeth, trismus, unexplained weight loss, stridor. Risk factors: tobacco and alcohol (synergistic), HPV16, EBV (nasopharynx), betel quid, occupational wood/nickel dust, poor dental hygiene, Plummer–Vinson syndrome. Refer urgently via the suspected cancer pathway.

## 9. Airway emergencies in ENT

- **Foreign body inhalation**: sudden choking, cough, wheeze, stridor in a toddler; back blows and chest thrusts (infants), abdominal thrusts (children > 1 year and adults); rigid bronchoscopy if persistent. Delayed presentation with recurrent unilateral wheeze or pneumonia.
- **Angioedema of the upper airway** (ACE inhibitor, allergic, hereditary): see chapter 54 and 40; early airway assessment; nasendoscopy; adrenaline for allergic; C1-inhibitor/icatibant for hereditary and ACE-inhibitor angioedema may be considered.
- **Post-tonsillectomy bleeding**: sit upright, call ENT, secure IV access, may need return to theatre. Primary (< 24 h) and secondary (5–10 days).
- **Stridor**: noisy breathing from upper airway narrowing; inspiratory (supraglottic/glottic), expiratory (lower trachea/bronchi), biphasic (subglottic). Causes: croup, foreign body, epiglottitis, anaphylaxis, tumour, vocal cord palsy, laryngomalacia (infants: improves with age), subglottic stenosis, burns. Do not agitate the child. Heliox, nebulised adrenaline, steroids, urgent senior anaesthetic/ENT.
- **Surgical airway**: cannot intubate, cannot oxygenate: front-of-neck access (scalpel–bougie–tube technique).

## Worked cases

### Case 1: One red eye with blurred vision

**Presentation.** Ms Y., 68, hypermetropic, woke at 4 a.m. with severe right eye pain, frontal headache, nausea and vomiting. She sees haloes around lights. She had been to the cinema in the evening and took an over-the-counter cold remedy containing pseudoephedrine and diphenhydramine. Examination: visual acuity right 6/36, left 6/6; right eye red with ciliary flush, hazy cornea, mid-dilated oval fixed pupil, the globe feels hard compared with the left. Shallow anterior chamber on torch side-lighting.

**Reasoning.** Acute angle-closure glaucoma, precipitated by dim light and an anticholinergic/sympathomimetic drug. Gastrointestinal symptoms may mimic an abdominal emergency.

**Action.** Emergency ophthalmology. Lower pressure with oral or IV acetazolamide 500 mg, topical timolol, brimonidine, pilocarpine (once pressure drops), steroid drops; analgesia and antiemetic; then laser iridotomy to the affected eye and prophylactically to the fellow eye. Avoid mydriatics and anticholinergics subsequently; counsel.

**Lessons.** A red eye with reduced vision, pain and a mid-dilated fixed pupil is angle-closure until proven otherwise. Check vision and pupils.

### Case 2: "I think I need glasses"

**Presentation.** Mr B., 72, hypertensive, has noticed for a week that his left vision is worse. Last 3 days jaw pain when chewing, scalp tenderness when combing hair, fatigue, weight loss 3 kg, and stiffness of shoulders in the morning. Today at 6 a.m., he suddenly could not see from the left eye. Acuity: counting fingers; RAPD on the left; pale swollen disc with a few flame haemorrhages. ESR 96 mm/h; CRP 120 mg/L; platelets 520.

**Reasoning.** Arteritic anterior ischaemic optic neuropathy from giant cell arteritis. The fellow eye is at high risk within days.

**Action.** Start IV methylprednisolone 1 g daily for 3 days **immediately** (do not wait for biopsy), then prednisolone 1 mg/kg; gastric protection, bone protection, glucose monitoring, PJP prophylaxis if prolonged high dose. Temporal artery ultrasound or biopsy within 1–2 weeks of starting steroids; rheumatology review and consider tocilizumab to spare steroids. Counsel about visual symptoms in the other eye. Vision in the affected eye rarely recovers.

**Lessons.** GCA is an ophthalmic emergency and treatment cannot await confirmation. Ask about jaw claudication in every older patient with new headache or visual symptoms.

### Case 3: Flashing lights and a curtain

**Presentation.** A 55-year-old myopic man reports flashing lights in the right eye for 3 days, many new floaters this morning, and a dark shadow in the upper-outer field that is gradually spreading. Acuity 6/9; no RAPD. Dilated fundus: a grey elevated retina superior-temporally with a horseshoe tear.

**Reasoning.** Rhegmatogenous retinal detachment after posterior vitreous detachment with a retinal tear. Macula still on, central vision still good; speed matters.

**Action.** Same-day vitreoretinal referral: surgery (vitrectomy, scleral buckle or pneumatic retinopexy) within 24 hours to protect the macula. Advise lying with the head positioned as told by the surgeon, avoiding strenuous activity and flying. Fellow eye examination for tears and lattice degeneration.

**Lessons.** Flashes plus new floaters plus field loss = detachment until a dilated examination proves otherwise. Do not delay for a routine appointment.

### Case 4: A child with a painful swollen eye

**Presentation.** Sam, 6, had a cold and nasal congestion for a week. Over 24 hours the left eyelids became red and swollen, he has a fever of 39.2 °C, severe headache, and complains that it hurts to move his eye. The eye appears pushed forward and the movements are restricted; he cannot see well. CRP 85.

**Reasoning.** Orbital cellulitis (post-septal) from ethmoid sinusitis, with proptosis, restricted painful eye movements, and visual compromise.

**Action.** Admit under joint ENT, ophthalmology and paediatrics. Blood cultures, IV ceftriaxone plus metronidazole (or local equivalent, adding vancomycin if MRSA risk), contrast CT orbits and sinuses (subperiosteal abscess seen). Monitor visual acuity, pupils, colour vision and eye movements every 2–4 hours, early drainage if abscess is large, vision deteriorates, or no improvement in 24–48 hours. Look for intracranial extension (headache, drowsiness, meningism: MRI).

**Lessons.** Painful eye movements, proptosis and ophthalmoplegia separate orbital from preseptal cellulitis. Do not treat as a simple "stye."

### Case 5: Ear pain in a diabetic

**Presentation.** Mr F., 76, type 2 diabetes, severe right ear pain for 3 weeks, worse at night, foul discharge, treated with two courses of topical ciprofloxacin–dexamethasone and oral amoxicillin without relief. Now facial weakness on the right. Examination: oedematous canal with granulation tissue on the floor at the bony–cartilaginous junction; right lower motor neurone facial palsy. ESR 105; CRP 78; HbA1c 78 mmol/mol (9.3%); cultures: *Pseudomonas aeruginosa*.

**Reasoning.** Necrotising (malignant) otitis externa, with skull base osteomyelitis and facial nerve involvement, in an older diabetic patient.

**Action.** Admit. CT temporal bone and MRI skull base (bony erosion, soft tissue extension), blood cultures, biopsy of granulation tissue to exclude carcinoma and for culture. Prolonged anti-pseudomonal therapy (ciprofloxacin 750 mg twice daily, or IV ceftazidime/piperacillin–tazobactam initially; commonly 6–12 weeks, guided by ESR/CRP and imaging; infectious disease specialist). Optimise diabetes. Aural toilet. Monitor cranial nerves IX–XII, jugular foramen and sigmoid sinus thrombosis.

**Lessons.** "Otitis externa that does not respond" with severe night pain in a diabetic needs a high index of suspicion; avoid steroid-containing drops and repeated oral amoxicillin without a diagnosis.

### Case 6: Hoarseness

**Presentation.** Mr C., 63, 40 pack-year smoker and regular drinker, has had a gradually worsening hoarse voice for 8 weeks, with intermittent right ear pain and a feeling of food sticking in the throat. No cough or haemoptysis. Examination: no cervical nodes palpable; otoscopy normal.

**Reasoning.** Hoarseness > 3 weeks in a smoker, with referred otalgia (via vagus and glossopharyngeal nerves) and dysphagia: laryngeal or hypopharyngeal carcinoma until proven otherwise.

**Action.** Urgent (two-week) referral to ENT for flexible nasendoscopy: right vocal cord lesion with impaired mobility. CT/MRI neck and chest, biopsy under general anaesthesia (direct laryngoscopy). Staging: T2 glottic squamous carcinoma. Multidisciplinary team: radiotherapy or laser resection; smoking and alcohol cessation, speech and swallow therapy, dental assessment before radiotherapy.

**Lessons.** Persistent hoarseness and unexplained otalgia are red flags for head and neck cancer. Chest X-ray is not an adequate substitute for laryngoscopy.

### Case 7: Sudden deafness

**Presentation.** A 44-year-old woman woke two days ago with muffled hearing and tinnitus in the left ear, with a sense of fullness but no vertigo, no ear pain or discharge. Otoscopy: normal canals and drums. Rinne: air conduction better than bone in both ears. Weber lateralises to the right. Audiogram: 60 dB sensorineural loss in the left ear across all frequencies.

**Reasoning.** Sudden sensorineural hearing loss (SSNHL): an otological emergency. Conductive causes are excluded (normal otoscopy, positive Rinne).

**Action.** Same-day ENT. Start oral prednisolone 60 mg daily (or intratympanic steroids), ideally within 72 hours, for 7–14 days with taper; MRI internal auditory meati to exclude vestibular schwannoma or other retrocochlear lesions; blood glucose, syphilis and Lyme serology as indicated; repeat audiogram in 1–2 weeks and at 3 months. Give realistic prognosis and discuss hearing aid options if recovery is incomplete.

**Lessons.** Sudden hearing loss should be treated as urgently as a stroke. Check whether the loss is truly sensorineural, and do not attribute it to "a cold".

## Common pitfalls

1. **Not recording visual acuity** in any eye complaint.
2. **Prescribing steroid drops** for a red eye in primary care (missed herpetic keratitis, glaucoma, cataract).
3. **Patching** an eye with infected keratitis or a contact-lens ulcer.
4. **Attributing acute angle-closure glaucoma to migraine or gastroenteritis**, given headaches and vomiting.
5. **Delaying steroids in suspected GCA** until biopsy results.
6. **Missing the possibility of orbital cellulitis** in a child with eyelid swelling (check eye movements and acuity).
7. **Irrigating a chemical injury late**, after examination or transfer.
8. **Pressing or measuring pressure on a possible open globe.**
9. **Prescribing antibiotics for viral rhinosinusitis, sore throat or ear pain** without applying criteria.
10. **Treating sudden hearing loss as wax or otitis** without a tuning fork test or audiogram.
11. **Syringing ears with perforation, grommets or previous surgery.**
12. **Failing to examine the nasopharynx** in unilateral serous otitis media in adults.
13. **Explaining persistent hoarseness without laryngoscopy** in smokers.
14. **Open excisional biopsy of a neck lump** before FNA and ENT assessment.
15. **Examining the throat of a child with suspected epiglottitis**, causing airway obstruction.
16. **Relying on HINTS in intermittent or positional vertigo**, or without training.
17. **Forgetting button battery or magnet ingestion/insertion** in the child's nose or ear.

## Key points

- Visual acuity, pupils and fluorescein are the minimum eye examination; reduced vision, pain, photophobia, or corneal changes mean same-day referral.
- Chemical injury: irrigate first. Suspected open globe: shield, do not press. Suspected GCA: steroids before biopsy.
- Sudden painless monocular visual loss is a vascular emergency (CRAO, GCA, carotid disease) until proven otherwise; flashes and a curtain mean retinal detachment.
- Orbital cellulitis presents with proptosis, painful and restricted eye movements, and reduced vision; it is an emergency.
- Screen diabetic eyes regularly; control blood pressure and glucose.
- Acute otitis media: pain relief first; antibiotics selectively; watch for mastoiditis. Glue ear: watch for 3 months. Necrotising otitis externa in diabetics.
- Sudden sensorineural hearing loss is an emergency and needs steroids and MRI.
- Unilateral or persistent symptoms in the nose, throat, ear or neck lasting more than 3 weeks in smokers and drinkers may signify cancer: laryngoscopy and urgent referral.
- Epiglottitis, retropharyngeal abscess, Ludwig angina and angioedema threaten the airway: do not distress the patient, and call senior help.

## Self-assessment

**Questions**

1. List four features of a red eye that mandate same-day ophthalmology referral.
2. A 70-year-old has new headache, jaw claudication, and amaurosis fugax. ESR is 25 mm/h. What do you do?
3. Differentiate preseptal from orbital cellulitis at the bedside.
4. A patient presents with flashes, new floaters and a field defect. What is the likely diagnosis and how urgent is the review?
5. How would you interpret Rinne positive on both sides and Weber lateralising to the left?
6. What are the Centor criteria and how are they used?
7. A 3-year-old has stridor, barking cough and a low-grade fever after a cold. What is the treatment?
8. A 58-year-old smoker has had unilateral hearing loss with a middle ear effusion for 6 weeks. What should you examine?
9. When should you refrain from irrigating an ear?
10. What is the first action in a patient with alkali splash to the eye?

**Answers**

1. Any four of: reduced visual acuity that does not correct with pinhole; severe pain; photophobia; corneal opacity or fluorescein uptake; abnormal pupil (irregular, fixed, mid-dilated); hypopyon or hyphaema; contact lens wearer with pain; recent eye surgery or injection; history of trauma or penetrating injury; chemical injury; herpes zoster ophthalmicus; suspected scleritis; neonatal conjunctivitis.
2. Suspect giant cell arteritis. Normal ESR does not exclude it (5–10%); check CRP and platelets. With visual symptoms, start high-dose steroids immediately (e.g., prednisolone 40–60 mg, or IV methylprednisolone if visual loss), arrange same-day specialist review, temporal artery ultrasound/biopsy within days to weeks, and protect bones and stomach.
3. Orbital cellulitis: proptosis, restricted or painful eye movements, reduced vision, colour desaturation, RAPD, fever; preseptal: lid swelling and redness with normal movements, normal vision and no proptosis. If in doubt, CT and treat as orbital.
4. Retinal detachment (with a tear). Same-day or next-day vitreoretinal assessment, since macula-on detachment is repairable with good vision outcomes.
5. Rinne positive bilaterally (air > bone) means no significant conductive loss; Weber to the left suggests sensorineural loss in the right ear (or conductive in the left, but Rinne excludes this). The right ear is the abnormal ear: audiogram, and consider MRI if asymmetrical.
6. Tonsillar exudate, tender anterior cervical lymphadenopathy, fever history, absence of cough, one point each (plus age modification in McIsaac). Low scores (0–1) need no antibiotics or test; 2–3 consider a rapid antigen test or delayed prescription; ≥ 4 treat or test depending on local guidelines.
7. Croup: oral dexamethasone (about 0.15–0.6 mg/kg) once; nebulised adrenaline for stridor at rest, keep the child calm, observe; admit if severe or recurrent stridor at rest. Exclude epiglottitis, foreign body, and bacterial tracheitis if toxic.
8. The nasopharynx (flexible nasendoscopy) and neck: nasopharyngeal carcinoma causes unilateral Eustachian tube obstruction and effusion; examine cervical nodes and cranial nerves; refer urgently.
9. When there is a known or suspected perforation, grommet, previous ear surgery, active otitis externa/pain, or a vegetable foreign body that swells with water, or a button battery; use microsuction or specialist care.
10. Immediate copious irrigation of the eye with water or saline (at least 15–30 min until the pH is neutral), before any examination or transfer, with eyelids held open and a check of the pH, then urgent ophthalmology.
