# Chapter 63. Palliative and End-of-Life Assessment

## Learning objectives

After studying this chapter you should be able to:

1. Distinguish palliative care, end-of-life care and hospice, and identify patients who would benefit from a palliative approach early in the illness trajectory.
2. Recognise typical illness trajectories (cancer, organ failure, frailty/dementia) and use prognostic indicators and the "surprise question" appropriately.
3. Perform a structured, multidimensional palliative assessment: physical, psychological, social, spiritual, and practical.
4. Assess and grade common symptoms: pain, breathlessness, nausea and vomiting, constipation, delirium, anorexia–cachexia, fatigue, death rattle, terminal agitation.
5. Use validated tools (ESAS, IPOS, PPS, ECOG, Karnofsky, Abbey Pain Scale) and know their limits.
6. Diagnose dying and recognise reversible causes of deterioration that should be excluded first.
7. Communicate prognosis, hold goals-of-care and advance care planning conversations, and document treatment escalation and resuscitation decisions.
8. Identify palliative emergencies (spinal cord compression, SVC obstruction, hypercalcaemia, bleeding, raised ICP, bowel obstruction) and decide how actively to investigate them.
9. Support carers, recognise grief and complicated bereavement, and avoid moral distress in the team.

> **Note on doses and legal frameworks.** Drug doses are typical starting doses for opioid-naive adults with normal renal function and must be adjusted to the patient, local formularies and palliative-care advice. Laws on advance directives, resuscitation orders, sedation and assisted dying differ between countries and regions; follow local law and policy.

## 1. Concepts and scope

### 1.1 Definitions

- **Palliative care**: an approach that improves quality of life of patients and families facing life-threatening illness, through prevention and relief of suffering by early identification, assessment and treatment of pain and other physical, psychosocial and spiritual problems. It is appropriate **from diagnosis**, alongside disease-modifying treatment, and is not limited to cancer.
- **End-of-life care**: care in the last months to days of life (the period during which the patient is likely to die within roughly 12 months).
- **Terminal / dying phase**: the last days to hours.
- **Hospice**: a model of care (inpatient, home, day services) delivering specialist palliative care.
- **Generalist vs specialist palliative care**: every clinician provides generalist palliative care (basic symptom control, communication, planning); specialist teams are called for complex symptoms, family distress, difficult decisions, and complex social situations.

### 1.2 Why early integration matters

Randomised trials (for instance in metastatic lung cancer) show that early specialist palliative care improves quality of life, mood and sometimes survival, and reduces aggressive end-of-life treatment. A palliative approach is not "giving up": it is deliberate attention to what matters to the patient, alongside appropriate treatment.

### 1.3 Illness trajectories

| Trajectory | Pattern | Examples | Implications |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Cancer** | Relatively preserved function, then rapid decline over weeks to months | Metastatic solid tumours | Prognosis predictable in last weeks: act early on planning while patient is well |
| **Organ failure** | Gradual decline with acute exacerbations and unpredictable death | Heart failure, COPD, cirrhosis, CKD stage 5 | Every admission may be the last; start discussions at stable moments |
| **Frailty / dementia** | Long, slow decline, low reserve; death often after minor events (infection, fall) | Advanced dementia, multimorbidity (see chapter 61) | Prognosis uncertain; focus on comfort, avoidance of burdensome transfers |
| **Sudden** | Unexpected | Trauma, MI | Not a palliative trajectory; bereavement care |

### 1.4 Who should be identified?

- **The "surprise question"**: "Would I be surprised if this patient died in the next 12 months?" If not surprised, consider a palliative approach, advance care planning and a register. Its predictive value is modest (sensitivity moderate, specificity limited), so it is a prompt for conversation, not a prognostic instrument.
- **General indicators**: repeated unplanned admissions, declining performance status (ECOG ≥ 3, PPS ≤ 50–60%), weight loss > 10% in 6 months, serum albumin low, reduced oral intake, dependence in ADLs, patient or carer request for comfort-focused care, progressive disease despite optimal treatment.
- **Disease-specific indicators** (examples; use local tools such as SPICT or GSF): heart failure NYHA III–IV on optimal therapy with repeated admissions, intolerance of prognostic medications, symptomatic hypotension or renal dysfunction; COPD with FEV₁ < 30%, oxygen dependence, hypercapnia, repeated exacerbations; cirrhosis with refractory ascites, hepatorenal syndrome, encephalopathy, not a transplant candidate; CKD eGFR < 15 with symptomatic conservative management or dialysis withdrawal; advanced dementia with inability to walk, speak a few words, incontinence, recurrent aspiration or infections; motor neurone disease with respiratory involvement (FVC < 50%), swallowing or weight loss; advanced Parkinson's disease or stroke with dysphagia.

## 2. Prognostication

### 2.1 Principles

Prognosis is probabilistic. Clinicians tend to be **overoptimistic** (by a factor of about 3 on average in terminal cancer), and the longer the relationship, the greater the optimism. Use:

1. **Performance status**: ECOG 0–4; Karnofsky 100–0; **Palliative Performance Scale (PPS)** (mobility, activity/disease evidence, self-care, intake, consciousness). In advanced cancer, PPS 70% has median survival of several months, PPS 30% around 2–3 weeks, PPS 10% days.
2. **Clinical prediction signs** in advanced cancer: dyspnoea, anorexia, dysphagia, delirium, weight loss, cognitive impairment, oedema, tachycardia, low albumin, raised CRP, neutrophilia, lymphopenia.
3. **Scores**: Palliative Prognostic Score (PaP), Palliative Prognostic Index (PPI), Glasgow Prognostic Score (CRP + albumin), disease-specific (MELD-Na in cirrhosis, Seattle Heart Failure Model, BODE index in COPD, ADEPT in dementia/FAST staging).
4. **Trajectory of change**: rate of decline over weeks matters more than a single measurement.
5. **Time-limited trials**: "Let us try this for 2 weeks and see whether it helps; if not, we stop."

### 2.2 Communicating prognosis

Offer ranges rather than numbers when uncertain: "hours to days", "days to weeks", "weeks to months", "months to a year or more". Use "hope for the best, prepare for the worst". Ask what the patient wants to know, and how much. Avoid "there is nothing more we can do": there is always something that can be done.

## 3. Comprehensive palliative assessment

### 3.1 Structure

Use a multidimensional framework, repeated as things change:

1. **Illness understanding and information needs**: What do you understand about your illness? What are you expecting? How much would you like to know?
2. **Physical**: symptoms (below), function, nutrition, medications, investigations in progress that no longer change management.
3. **Psychological**: anxiety, depression, adjustment, fear, cognition/delirium, coping style, previous psychiatric history, demoralisation.
4. **Social and family**: living situation, carers, caring burden, finances, children and dependants, cultural context, who makes decisions, safeguarding.
5. **Spiritual / existential**: meaning, faith, guilt, legacy, "total pain" (Cicely Saunders).
6. **Practical**: legal documents, will, power of attorney, advance directive, preferred place of care and death, funeral wishes, equipment, home care, respite, funding.
7. **Goals and priorities**: "What matters most to you now?" "What are you most worried about?" "What would a good day look like?" "Are there things you would not want?"
8. **Decisions**: resuscitation status, hospital transfer, ICU, ventilation, artificial nutrition and hydration, dialysis, antibiotics, deactivation of devices (ICD), organ donation.

### 3.2 Symptom assessment tools

- **Edmonton Symptom Assessment System (ESAS-r)**: 0–10 numerical ratings for pain, tiredness, drowsiness, nausea, appetite, shortness of breath, depression, anxiety, wellbeing.
- **IPOS (Integrated Palliative care Outcome Scale)**: patient-reported physical, emotional, communication and practical concerns; staff and family versions.
- **Distress Thermometer** for psychological distress.
- **Abbey Pain Scale, PAINAD, Doloplus-2** for non-verbal or cognitively impaired patients.
- **Delirium**: 4AT, CAM; **Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale**, PHQ-2/9 (limited in the dying).
- **Caregiver burden**: Zarit Burden Interview.

Ask the patient directly and repeatedly. Patients under-report symptoms to avoid "bothering" staff; families over- or under-estimate.

### 3.3 Principles of symptom control

1. **Diagnose the cause and mechanism** of the symptom before treating (e.g., nausea from opioids vs hypercalcaemia vs bowel obstruction).
2. **Treat reversible causes** when proportionate to goals.
3. **Explain** to the patient and family.
4. **Combine non-pharmacological and pharmacological measures.**
5. **Use regular dosing with breakthrough**; review often; titrate to effect; prefer oral route, then subcutaneous (syringe driver) or transdermal; avoid intramuscular.
6. **Anticipate** side effects (prescribe laxative with every opioid, antiemetic as needed).
7. **Reassess** after each intervention and stop drugs that no longer help (deprescribing: statins, antihypertensives, supplements, anticoagulants where proportionate).

## 4. Common symptoms: assessment and approach

### 4.1 Pain

**Assess**: site, radiation, quality, intensity (0–10, or behavioural scale), timing (constant, incident, breakthrough), aggravating/relieving factors, effect on function and sleep, previous analgesics and response, beliefs and fears about opioids, psychological and spiritual contributors. Examine the painful area; consider neurological examination. Distinguish **nociceptive** (somatic, visceral), **neuropathic** (burning, shooting, allodynia), **bone pain** (movement-related, focal tenderness; risk of fracture), **mixed**, **"total pain"**.

**Approach**:
1. Treat the cause where reasonable: radiotherapy for painful bone metastases (single fraction effective), bisphosphonates/denosumab, surgery for impending fracture, nerve blocks, drainage of ascites, treat constipation, infection, pressure sores.
2. **WHO analgesic ladder** (simplified to: non-opioid ± weak or strong opioid ± adjuvant; step 1 is not mandatory before strong opioids in severe pain).
   - Paracetamol regularly (1 g q6h, 3 g/day if frail/low weight); NSAIDs with caution (renal, GI, cardiovascular).
   - **Opioid-naive moderate to severe pain**: oral morphine immediate release 2.5–5 mg every 4 hours (start low in frail/older adults and renal impairment) plus the same dose as breakthrough up to hourly as needed; after 24–48 hours, convert the total daily dose to modified release (12-hourly) with a breakthrough dose of about one-sixth of the total daily dose.
   - **Renal impairment (eGFR < 30)**: avoid morphine (active metabolite accumulation); prefer oxycodone with caution, fentanyl, buprenorphine, or hydromorphone/methadone under specialist advice.
   - Oral : subcutaneous morphine approximately 2:1 (e.g., 20 mg oral = 10 mg SC), per local equianalgesic tables; use caution with conversions, reducing by 25–50% when switching opioids because of incomplete cross-tolerance.
   - **Always co-prescribe a stimulant laxative** (senna ± macrogol) with opioids; antiemetic (haloperidol 0.5–1.5 mg or metoclopramide) for transient nausea.
   - **Adjuvants**: neuropathic pain — gabapentin/pregabalin (renal dosing), amitriptyline/nortriptyline, duloxetine; bone pain — dexamethasone, NSAID, radiotherapy; raised ICP — dexamethasone; visceral/colicky — hyoscine butylbromide; muscle spasm — baclofen, benzodiazepines; steroid for nerve compression/liver capsule pain.
3. **Opioid toxicity**: drowsiness, hallucinations, myoclonus, pinpoint pupils, confusion, hyperalgesia, respiratory depression (late); manage by dose reduction, hydration, opioid rotation, treating renal failure; naloxone only in life-threatening respiratory depression at titrated small doses (e.g., 100–200 µg IV) to avoid precipitating withdrawal and pain crisis.
4. **Myths**: appropriately titrated opioids do not shorten life in dying patients; addiction is rare in patients with cancer pain; tolerance is manageable.

### 4.2 Breathlessness

**Assess**: severity (0–10, mMRC), triggers, impact on function, anxiety, cough, orthopnoea, sputum. Examine for reversible causes: pleural effusion, pericardial effusion, pneumonia, PE, heart failure, anaemia, airway obstruction, lymphangitic spread, bronchospasm, SVCO, ascites.

**Management**: treat reversible causes proportionately (drain effusion, diuretics, bronchodilators, transfuse if symptomatic, steroids for lymphangitis or airway obstruction, radiotherapy/stent for airway or SVC lesion). Non-drug: fan directed at the face, positioning, pacing, breathing exercises, calm reassurance, pulmonary rehabilitation, handheld fan. Oxygen only helps if hypoxaemic (and trial for benefit). **Opioids** reduce the sensation of breathlessness (low dose oral morphine 2.5 mg every 4 hours as needed, or modified release 5 mg twice daily). Benzodiazepines (lorazepam 0.5–1 mg SL, midazolam 2.5 mg SC) for associated panic when opioids are inadequate. For terminal breathlessness, continuous SC infusion of midazolam and opioid.

### 4.3 Nausea and vomiting

Identify the mechanism and choose antiemetic accordingly:

| Cause | Mechanism | First-line agent |
|---|---|---|
| Drugs, metabolic (opioids, hypercalcaemia, uraemia, infection) | Chemoreceptor trigger zone (D2) | Haloperidol 0.5–1.5 mg nocte/bd |
| Gastric stasis, squashed stomach, hepatic congestion | Gastric (prokinetic) | Metoclopramide 10 mg tds (not in complete obstruction) or domperidone |
| Raised ICP, brain mets, motion-related | Vestibular/cerebral | Cyclizine 50 mg tds (avoid in heart failure), dexamethasone |
| Complete bowel obstruction | Visceral | Hyoscine butylbromide, octreotide, haloperidol, cyclizine |
| Chemotherapy/radiotherapy | 5-HT₃, NK₁ | Ondansetron, aprepitant, dexamethasone |
| Anxiety, anticipatory | Cortical | Lorazepam, psychological support |
| Broad spectrum / refractory | Multiple | Levomepromazine 3–6 mg nocte, olanzapine 2.5–5 mg |

### 4.4 Constipation and bowel obstruction

Constipation: nearly universal with opioids; check diet, hydration, immobility, hypercalcaemia, hypothyroidism, hypokalaemia, anticholinergics. Rectal examination to exclude impaction. Treat with stimulant (senna) and osmotic laxative (macrogol), rectal measures (glycerine, bisacodyl suppositories, enemas), methylnaltrexone or naloxegol in opioid-induced constipation.

**Malignant bowel obstruction** (ovarian, colorectal, pancreatic cancers; peritoneal carcinomatosis): colicky pain, vomiting, distension, absolute constipation. Assess: surgical candidacy (single level, good performance status, no carcinomatosis), CT. If inoperable: symptom control with **subcutaneous opioid, anti-secretory (hyoscine butylbromide or octreotide), antiemetics (haloperidol, cyclizine), dexamethasone**; venting gastrostomy; minimal IV fluids (or hydration SC); NG tube temporary; allow sips and small amounts of food/drink.

### 4.5 Anorexia, cachexia, dysphagia, thirst

Cancer cachexia is a metabolic syndrome (weight loss > 5% in 6 months, sarcopenia, systemic inflammation); it is not simply reversible by feeding. Assess nutrition and contributors (pain, nausea, dysgeusia, constipation, depression, candidiasis, dental problems, drugs). Offer small, favourite meals, flexible timing, dietitian advice; corticosteroids (dexamethasone 2–4 mg or prednisolone) or progestins/olanzapine for appetite (time-limited, side effects); consider anamorelin where available. **Artificial nutrition** is rarely indicated in the last weeks and not in advanced dementia (no benefit on survival, aspiration or pressure ulcers; "careful hand feeding" preferred). **Thirst/dry mouth**: mouth care, sips, ice chips, saliva substitutes; clinically assisted hydration is a time-limited trial depending on goals, may help with delirium or opioid toxicity but can worsen secretions and oedema.

### 4.6 Fatigue

Prevalent (> 70%); evaluate anaemia, hypothyroidism, hypogonadism, depression, sleep, drugs, infection, hypoxia, electrolyte disorders, heart failure. Treat reversible factors; exercise (when feasible), energy conservation, sleep hygiene; methylphenidate or corticosteroids short term in selected cases.

### 4.7 Delirium and terminal agitation

Common in the last weeks (up to 80% near death). **Assess**: fluctuating attention and consciousness, hallucinations, reversed sleep-wake cycle, agitation or hypoactive state; 4AT/CAM. **Look for reversible causes**: opioid toxicity, drugs (steroids, benzodiazepines, anticholinergics), urinary retention, constipation, pain, infection, hypercalcaemia, hyponatraemia, uraemia, hepatic encephalopathy, hypoxia, dehydration, brain metastases, alcohol/benzodiazepine withdrawal. **Manage**: non-drug measures (family presence, orientation, quiet, light, glasses/hearing aids), treat causes proportionately, **haloperidol 0.5–1 mg** orally/SC (repeated), **levomepromazine** or **midazolam** if severely agitated or distressing (benzodiazepines can worsen delirium, so reserve for refractory states, alcohol withdrawal, seizures). Distinguish from pain, anxiety, spiritual distress, and unresolved family conflict.

### 4.8 Depression, anxiety, demoralisation, suicidal ideation

Sadness is normal; persistent anhedonia, hopelessness, guilt, and suicidal thoughts suggest depression (somatic symptoms overlap with disease: rely on mood and cognitive items). Ask directly about wish to die: distinguish **passing thoughts of "it would be better if it were over"** from **active request to hasten death** (explore the reasons, symptoms, fears, depression, loneliness, loss of dignity, control). Treat depression (psychotherapy, SSRIs/mirtazapine; methylphenidate for short prognosis), anxiety (benzodiazepines short term), involve psychology and chaplaincy. Know local law on requests for assisted dying; keep the conversation open and non-judgemental.

### 4.9 Other symptoms (brief)

- **Cough**: treat cause; codeine/morphine, simple linctus; nebulised saline; for death rattle see below.
- **Hiccup**: baclofen, metoclopramide, chlorpromazine, haloperidol, gabapentin.
- **Pruritus**: cholestasis (colestyramine, sertraline, rifampicin), uraemia (gabapentin), opioid-induced (antihistamines, ondansetron).
- **Sweats**: infection, tumour, medications; review drugs, cool environment, NSAID, steroid, cimetidine.
- **Oral problems**: candida (fluconazole/nystatin), mucositis, xerostomia; regular mouth care.
- **Pressure ulcers and wounds**: position, mattresses, dressings, odour control (metronidazole topical).
- **Lymphoedema**: skin care, compression, decongestive therapy.
- **Seizures**: midazolam buccal/SC, levetiracetam; steroids for cerebral oedema.
- **Hiccups, hypersalivation, hemoptysis, bleeding**: see below.
- **Insomnia**: sleep hygiene, treat pain/anxiety; short-term melatonin, mirtazapine, zopiclone.

## 5. Palliative emergencies

Identify whether intervention matches goals, remembering that some emergencies are **reversible and quickly relieved** (e.g., hypercalcaemia) while others require immediate decisions in the context of prognosis.

| Emergency | Recognition | Immediate actions | Considerations |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Metastatic spinal cord compression (MSCC)** | Back pain (new, worse lying, thoracic), band-like pain, leg weakness, sensory level, bladder/bowel dysfunction | Keep flat if unstable spine; dexamethasone 16 mg (local protocols); urgent whole-spine MRI (within 24 h; immediately if neurological deficit); oncology/spinal team for radiotherapy or surgery | Ambulatory status at presentation determines outcome; treat urgently when consistent with goals and prognosis |
| **Superior vena cava obstruction** | Facial/arm swelling, dyspnoea, dilated chest veins, headache, stridor if severe | Sit up, oxygen, dexamethasone, CT venogram; stenting or radiotherapy; chemotherapy for lymphoma/SCLC | Stridor/cerebral oedema = emergency |
| **Hypercalcaemia** | Thirst, polyuria, constipation, nausea, confusion, drowsiness, corrected Ca > 2.8 mmol/L | IV fluids 0.9% saline, IV zoledronate (renal dosing) or pamidronate; denosumab if renal failure; stop calcium/vitamin D, thiazides | Often a sign of advanced disease; relieves symptoms; recurrent episodes → reconsider goals |
| **Massive bleeding (haemoptysis, haematemesis, wound)** | Visible bleeding; risk areas known (head and neck, lung, fungating tumours) | Dark towels, stay with the patient, calm; crisis medications prepared in advance: midazolam 5–10 mg IM/SC/buccal ± opioid; tranexamic acid; radiotherapy/embolisation for slow bleeding | Anticipate with family; document plan |
| **Raised intracranial pressure** | Morning headache, vomiting, drowsiness, focal signs, seizures | Dexamethasone 8–16 mg daily, head up, anticonvulsant, radiotherapy/neurosurgery consideration | Steroid side effects, taper according to symptoms |
| **Seizure** | | Midazolam 10 mg buccal/SC; ensure airway; anticonvulsant | |
| **Malignant bowel obstruction** | See section 4.4 | | |
| **Neutropenic sepsis** | See chapter 62 | Consider whether treatment aligns with goals | |
| **Pathological fracture / impending** | Bone pain on weight-bearing | Analgesia, immobilise, radiology, orthopaedic and radiotherapy review | |
| **Airway obstruction/stridor** | Noisy breathing, distress | Humidified oxygen, dexamethasone, heliox, stent/radiotherapy; sedation if irreversible | |
| **Acute urinary retention, renal obstruction** | | Catheterisation; nephrostomy if consistent with goals | |
| **Tumour lysis syndrome, hypoglycaemia, adrenal crisis** | | As per respective chapters | |

## 6. Recognising and caring for the dying patient

### 6.1 Diagnosing dying

Dying is likely when, over days, the patient:
- is **bedbound**, semi-comatose or deeply drowsy, **no longer able to take oral fluids and medications**, has **minimal oral intake**,
- shows progressive deterioration in multiple systems **despite reversible causes being excluded or treated**,
- has **changes in breathing** (Cheyne–Stokes, apnoeic pauses), **peripheral cooling and mottling**, **weak or impalpable pulses**, **falling urine output**, **terminal secretions**, and **jaundice or renal failure** if relevant.

Always ask: **Is something reversible?** (opioid toxicity, hypercalcaemia, infection, urinary retention, dehydration, steroid withdrawal, hypoglycaemia, hyponatraemia, medication effect, adrenal insufficiency). Seek a second opinion (senior doctor, palliative team) when diagnosis is uncertain. Multidisciplinary agreement documented. Prognostic uncertainty: patients may rally; reassess daily.

### 6.2 Individualised care plan in the last days

1. **Communicate** with the patient (if able) and family: the diagnosis of dying, what to expect, who to call, what will and will not be done. Check understanding; ask about cultural and religious needs, rituals, preferred place of death.
2. **Review and rationalise medications**: stop non-essential (statins, antihypertensives, antibiotics unless symptom benefit, anticoagulants, vitamins, hypoglycaemics adjusted), continue those needed for symptom control. Transition to **subcutaneous** route (syringe driver, intermittent injection) when swallowing fails.
3. **Anticipatory prescribing ("just-in-case" medicines)** PRN SC, written in advance so relief is immediate:

| Symptom | Typical drug and dose (adult, opioid-naive, normal renal function; adjust) |
|---|---|
| Pain / breathlessness | Morphine 2.5 mg SC hourly PRN (if on regular opioid, one-sixth of 24-h SC dose); reduce for renal impairment or frailty (consider oxycodone/fentanyl alternatives) |
| Agitation/delirium | Haloperidol 0.5–1.5 mg SC up to 2-hourly PRN (max ~5 mg/24 h unless specialist advises); midazolam 2.5–5 mg SC for severe agitation or anxiety; levomepromazine 6.25–12.5 mg |
| Nausea/vomiting | Haloperidol 0.5–1.5 mg SC, cyclizine 50 mg SC, levomepromazine 2.5–6.25 mg |
| Respiratory secretions ("death rattle") | Hyoscine butylbromide 20 mg SC (does not cross BBB), glycopyrronium 200 µg SC, hyoscine hydrobromide 400 µg SC (may cause delirium) |
| Seizures | Midazolam 10 mg buccal/SC |

4. **Continuous SC infusion** (syringe driver) if two or more PRN doses are needed in 24 hours: calculated from PRN usage, reviewed daily.
5. **Hydration and nutrition**: individual decision; mouth care, comfort; avoid starting burdensome interventions; explain that dying patients do not feel hunger and that withholding fluids is not the cause of death; if clinically assisted hydration is considered, set a time-limited trial with goals.
6. **Stop unnecessary tests** (routine blood tests, observations beyond comfort-driven assessments), alarms and devices; deactivate ICD shocks after discussion.
7. **Resuscitation and escalation plans** documented clearly (DNACPR, ceilings of treatment).
8. **Environment**: privacy, family presence, spiritual care, music; allow rituals.
9. **Care of the family**: practical information (what to do at death, who to call, verification and certification), anticipatory grief support, assess children's needs.
10. **After death**: confirm death (absence of pulse, heart sounds, breath sounds, fixed dilated pupils, no response to stimuli), notify, certify per local law, respectful personal care, support the family, information on bereavement services; consider organ/tissue donation, post-mortem (in line with the family's wishes and legal requirements).

### 6.3 Palliative sedation

For intolerable and refractory symptoms in the last days (severe agitation, dyspnoea, pain, terminal bleeding), proportionate sedation with midazolam (or levomepromazine/phenobarbital) titrated to the **lowest dose that relieves distress**, with specialist involvement, informed consent from patient/family when possible, regular reassessment and documentation. It is distinct from euthanasia: intention is symptom relief, proportionality and reversibility guide dosing, and the doctrine of double effect does not make it equivalent to hastening death when properly applied.

## 7. Advance care planning, goals of care and decisions

### 7.1 Advance care planning (ACP)

A process where patients reflect on values, discuss with loved ones and clinicians, and record preferences for future care. Components: designation of a **healthcare proxy / lasting power of attorney**, **advance directives / living will** (specific refusals of treatment, legally binding in many jurisdictions), **statement of wishes** (values, preferred place of care), **resuscitation preference**, **treatment escalation plans** (e.g., POLST/MOLST or ReSPECT form). Revisit with changes in health. In Italy, the law 219/2017 recognises informed consent, DAT (disposizioni anticipate di trattamento), the fiduciario, and shared care planning (pianificazione condivisa delle cure); in other countries consult local legislation.

### 7.2 Goals-of-care conversation: a framework (e.g., Serious Illness Conversation Guide)

1. **Set up**: ask permission, choose time and place, include family as the patient wishes.
2. **Assess understanding** and information preferences.
3. **Share prognosis** in headline form, tailored: "I'm worried that time may be shorter than we hoped." Allow silence.
4. **Explore key topics**: goals, fears, sources of strength, critical abilities (what abilities are so important that you cannot imagine living without them), trade-offs (how much are you willing to go through for the possibility of more time), family.
5. **Close**: summarise, make a recommendation consistent with values, check agreement, document.

Use **NURSE** statements for emotion (Name, Understand, Respect, Support, Explore). Avoid asking "Do you want everything done?" which is misleading; instead offer a **clinician recommendation** after exploring goals.

### 7.3 Resuscitation (CPR) discussion

CPR survival to discharge is low in advanced illness (about 10–15% for in-hospital arrest overall; much lower in metastatic cancer, advanced dementia, severe organ failure, and frailty, often < 5%), and survivors frequently have significant disability. A "do not attempt CPR" decision is **a medical decision not to offer a treatment that will not work**, communicated sensitively, with the patient involved when appropriate; it does not mean "do not treat". Local law regulates whether consent is required; always inform the patient unless this would cause harm, and explain to the family. Escalation plans should specify what **will** be done (antibiotics, oxygen, hospital transfer, NIV, ICU).

### 7.4 Decision-making capacity and best interests

Assess capacity for each decision (understand, retain, use/weigh, communicate; chapter 61, 76). If lacking: use advance directive, proxy, best-interest process involving carers, with the least restrictive option. Disagreement between family members: meet, listen, reframe on the patient's wishes ("what would she say if she could see this?"), second opinion, ethics consultation.

### 7.5 Withholding and withdrawing treatment

Withholding and withdrawing are ethically equivalent when a treatment is no longer beneficial or desired. Examples: stopping dialysis (death in days to weeks), ventilator withdrawal with symptom management, ICD deactivation, discontinuing antibiotics, stopping artificial nutrition. Always plan symptom control, communicate, and support.

### 7.6 Assisted dying and euthanasia

Legal in some jurisdictions with strict criteria; illegal in many (including at the time of writing, Italy, subject to the Constitutional Court ruling 242/2019 on assisted suicide in defined circumstances). Clinicians should know local law and institutional policy, respond to requests with exploration of underlying suffering, and refer appropriately; conscientious objection must be respected along with duty of care.

## 8. Supporting families and carers, grief and bereavement

- **Carers**: assess burden, health, sleep, finances; offer respite, training (medicines, safe moving), community services, information on what to expect; ensure out-of-hours contacts.
- **Children**: honest, age-appropriate information; include in rituals when they wish; involve child specialists.
- **Anticipatory grief** is normal; **normal grief** shows waves of sadness, yearning, guilt, sleep and appetite changes, over months; **prolonged grief disorder** (persisting intense yearning beyond 6–12 months with functional impairment) and **depression** need treatment. Risk factors: sudden or traumatic death, ambivalent relationship, previous losses, lack of support, mental illness, the carer's high burden, regrets about care.
- **Bereavement follow-up**: a call or letter at 2–6 weeks; offer groups, counselling, and GP review.
- **Staff support**: moral distress, compassion fatigue; debriefing, peer support, reflective practice.

## 9. Special populations

- **Non-cancer disease**: heart failure (maintain guideline therapy as tolerated, diuretics for symptom relief, deactivate ICD at end of life, opioids for breathlessness); COPD (opioids, fans, pulmonary rehab, NIV decisions, advance care plan before crisis); advanced CKD (conservative kidney management vs dialysis: shared decision with realistic expectations; withdrawal from dialysis); cirrhosis (palliative paracentesis, terlipressin discussions, hepatic encephalopathy lactulose/rifaximin, avoid sedatives); neurodegenerative (MND: NIV, gastrostomy, communication aids; Parkinson's: non-oral rotigotine; dementia: see below).
- **Advanced dementia**: goals of comfort, avoid hospital transfer and feeding tubes, careful hand feeding, treat pain (PAINAD), minimise polypharmacy, manage infections symptomatically unless the patient benefits, support the family.
- **Children** (paediatric palliative care): family-centred, play therapy, symptom control with weight-based dosing, home/hospice deaths, sibling support.
- **Intellectual disability and communication difficulties**: use proxy pain scales, involve those who know the patient best.
- **Cultural and religious diversity**: ask about practices around truth-telling, family decision-making, rituals, diet, touching, post-mortem, and body handling; do not assume.
- **Prisoners, homeless people, migrants**: face barriers; adapt care and advocacy.
- **Patients with substance use disorder**: treat pain adequately (consider long-acting opioid, avoid abrupt cessation of opioid substitution, specialist advice).

## Worked cases

### Case 1: "There's nothing more to be done"

**Presentation.** Mr A., 71, metastatic pancreatic cancer, third-line chemotherapy failing, ECOG 3, PPS 50%, weight loss 12 kg in 3 months. Admitted with pain 8/10, nausea and exhaustion. His wife says, "The oncologist told us there is nothing more to be done, so now we just wait." Mr A. has not discussed his preferences.

**Reasoning.** Disease-modifying therapy is ending, but much can be done. Prognosis likely weeks to a few months (declining PPS, weight loss). The immediate task is a multidimensional assessment and a goals conversation.

**Action.** Assess pain: epigastric radiating to the back (visceral/neuropathic from coeliac plexus): start morphine IR 5 mg 4-hourly + breakthrough, titrate; add laxative, antiemetic (haloperidol, as nausea is likely due to gastric stasis—metoclopramide may be better; assess), dexamethasone for appetite and pain short term; consider coeliac plexus block. Ask about understanding and goals: he wants to be home for his daughter's wedding in 6 weeks and not to die in hospital. Introduce DNACPR discussion with a clear recommendation and rationale; agree ceilings (no ICU, hospital admission only if symptoms cannot be controlled at home). Arrange community palliative care and home equipment, enzyme supplements, carer support, advance care plan documented and shared, hospice day services, anticipatory medications at home.

**Lessons.** "Nothing more to do" is never correct; shift the focus. Ask what matters. Plan early, before crisis.

### Case 2: Distressing breathlessness in COPD

**Presentation.** Mrs B., 78, severe COPD (FEV₁ 24%), home oxygen, three admissions in a year, now breathless at rest, anxious, fearing suffocation. Chest clear of acute infection; CXR unchanged; ABG: pH 7.38, PaCO₂ 7.2 kPa, PaO₂ 8.4 kPa on oxygen.

**Reasoning.** Refractory breathlessness in end-stage COPD despite optimal therapy; trajectory of repeated decline. Reversible factors: infection excluded, treatment optimised, anaemia, heart failure and anxiety assessed. Palliative symptom management is appropriate while continuing bronchodilators.

**Action.** Hand-held fan, positioning, pacing, breathing exercises; low-dose oral morphine 2.5 mg 4-hourly PRN (monitor for sedation, constipation, CO₂ retention—usually safe at low dose), laxative; low-dose benzodiazepine or SSRI trial for anxiety; occupational therapy; discuss NIV and ICU preferences, advance care plan, "what would you want if you get worse?"; community palliative care and a rescue pack with a plan to treat at home when possible; hospice day care.

**Lessons.** Opioids are safe and effective for breathlessness at low doses; non-cancer illnesses merit palliative care.

### Case 3: Is he dying, or is this reversible?

**Presentation.** Mr C., 66, metastatic prostate cancer with bone metastases, on increasing morphine for 2 weeks, now drowsy, confused, with myoclonic jerks, reduced oral intake, and hallucinations. eGFR has fallen from 55 to 22. Family members believe he is dying.

**Reasoning.** Rapid deterioration with myoclonus, hallucinations and drowsiness in a patient on escalating morphine with new renal impairment suggests **opioid toxicity from accumulation of morphine-6-glucuronide**, possibly with dehydration, hypercalcaemia, or infection. Reversible causes must be excluded before diagnosing dying.

**Action.** Calcium (corrected 3.1 mmol/L: hypercalcaemia), urea/creatinine, glucose, urine retention (bladder scan), constipation (rectal examination), infection screen. Reduce or stop morphine and switch to a renally safer opioid (e.g., low-dose oxycodone or fentanyl SC), IV/SC fluids, treat hypercalcaemia with zoledronate (dose-adjusted) after hydration, haloperidol for hallucinations briefly, treat constipation. Within 48 hours he is alert, pain controlled on fentanyl. Discuss prognosis with the family: weeks to months; update plan.

**Lessons.** Always look for reversible causes before labelling someone as dying. Renal function dictates opioid choice.

### Case 4: Last days at home

**Presentation.** Mrs D., 84, metastatic breast cancer, now bedbound, sleeping most of the day, sipping fluids only, unable to swallow tablets since yesterday. Family wish to care for her at home; the district nurse calls the GP.

**Reasoning.** Probably dying (days): progressive deterioration, no oral intake, can't take medicines, reversible causes assessed (calcium normal at last check, no infection, opioid dose stable, no retention). Priorities: comfort, anticipation, communication, support at home.

**Action.** Convert regular morphine MR 30 mg bd to SC infusion (oral to SC ratio 2:1: 60 mg oral/24 h = 30 mg SC/24 h) via syringe driver with midazolam 10 mg/24 h for agitation/anxiety and hyoscine butylbromide 60 mg/24 h for secretions if developing; PRN SC morphine 5 mg (one-sixth of 24-h SC dose), midazolam 2.5 mg, haloperidol 0.5–1 mg, levomepromazine, hyoscine; stop non-essential tablets; mouth care and pressure care taught to family; DNACPR form at home; explain breathing changes and secretions, that the patient is not distressed by them; 24-hour contact numbers; chaplain visit; arrange who to call at death and verification procedures. She dies peacefully two days later; the nurse supports the family; bereavement call at 4 weeks.

**Lessons.** Anticipatory prescribing, a syringe driver, clear information and 24-hour support make home death possible.

### Case 5: The family who asks to stop fluids—and the family who asks to start them

**Presentation.** Mr E., 79, advanced dementia, bedbound, recurrent aspiration pneumonia, weight loss, spits out food. His daughter demands a PEG tube: "You can't let him starve." His son says he would never have wanted tubes.

**Reasoning.** Feeding tubes in advanced dementia do not prolong survival, prevent aspiration or heal pressure ulcers, and may cause agitation, restraints and complications. Decision-making: patient lacks capacity; need to establish his previously expressed values. Family conflict reflects love and guilt.

**Action.** Family meeting with the palliative team, the GP, and the speech therapist; review his previous statements (he told his son: "no machines, let me go in my own bed"); explain the evidence and what comfort feeding means (offer favourite foods, careful hand feeding, stop when he refuses, oral care), sit with daughter's fear of starvation ("he is not suffering from hunger; we will make sure he is comfortable"); agree treatment ceiling: treat infections symptomatically, avoid hospital transfer; time-limited review at 2–4 weeks; ethics consultation if the conflict persists; support for the daughter with chaplaincy/bereavement service.

**Lessons.** Address emotions behind demands; give evidence and a recommendation; use substituted judgement; offer alternatives to "doing nothing".

### Case 6: A young father with metastatic melanoma asks about dying

**Presentation.** Mr F., 38, metastatic melanoma progressing after immunotherapy, ECOG 2, two young children. He asks: "Am I going to die? How long?" His wife asks you not to tell him.

**Reasoning.** The patient has a right to information if he wants it; collusion by family protects but isolates. Ask what he knows and wants to know; offer information at his pace.

**Action.** Meet the patient (with or without his wife, as he prefers). "What is your understanding of where things are?" "Some people want to know everything and others prefer not to; what is it like for you?" He says he wants to know. Prognosis: "I'm worried that your illness is progressing despite treatment, and time may be limited—it could be months, hopefully longer, though it might be shorter." Allow silence and emotion; NURSE statements; explore fears (children, finances, pain); refer to psycho-oncology, social work for dependants, child-support services for memory boxes; consider clinical trials in line with his goals; discuss advance care planning gradually; early palliative care involvement alongside treatment; hold the wife's feelings with empathy, explaining that honest communication usually reduces regret.

**Lessons.** Elicit preferences for information; don't collude with non-disclosure; combine honesty with hope and practical support.

### Case 7: Spinal cord compression at the end of life

**Presentation.** Mrs G., 62, metastatic lung cancer on palliative chemotherapy, presents with 3 days of thoracic back pain, then leg heaviness and unsteady walking, urinary hesitancy. Examination: power 4/5 in legs, sensory level T8, upgoing plantars.

**Reasoning.** Probable metastatic spinal cord compression. She is still ambulatory; urgent treatment can preserve function, which matters greatly to her. Prognosis still months.

**Action.** Dexamethasone 16 mg now (per protocol, with PPI and glucose monitoring), analgesia, bed rest with spinal precautions pending stability assessment; whole-spine MRI within hours; oncology and spinal surgery review → radiotherapy (single or short-course) ± surgery; physiotherapy, bladder and bowel care, pressure care; discuss goals and prognosis; if non-ambulatory and prognosis weeks, treat for comfort and rehabilitation.

**Lessons.** Palliative does not mean passive: some emergencies deserve urgent, goal-concordant action.

## Common pitfalls

1. **Equating palliative care with end-of-life care** or cancer only; referring too late.
2. **Prognostic optimism**; not using performance status and trajectory.
3. **Saying "there is nothing more we can do."**
4. **Not asking what matters** to the patient; assuming goals.
5. **Failing to identify reversible causes** (opioid toxicity, hypercalcaemia, infection, retention, drugs) before diagnosing dying.
6. **Under-treating pain** or fearing opioids; or **over-treating** with sedatives in delirium without searching for causes.
7. **No laxative** with opioids; using morphine in renal failure.
8. **Intramuscular injections** and unnecessary investigations in the dying.
9. **Using "Do you want everything done?"**; failing to give a recommendation; avoiding the resuscitation conversation until crisis.
10. **Starting feeding tubes** in advanced dementia or forcing nutrition at the end of life.
11. **No anticipatory prescribing or syringe driver** for dying patients at home; poor out-of-hours support.
12. **Collusion** with families against disclosure; ignoring cultural context.
13. **Neglecting carers, children, and bereaved families**; ignoring staff distress.
14. **Treating the emergency without considering goals** (and vice versa).
15. **Poor documentation** and communication across settings.

---

## Key points

- Palliative care starts early, runs alongside disease-modifying treatment, and covers physical, psychological, social and spiritual needs.
- Identify patients with the surprise question, performance status and disease-specific indicators; know the three trajectories.
- Prognosis is uncertain and clinicians are optimistic; use ranges and time-limited trials.
- Assess symptoms systematically and treat according to mechanism: opioids for pain and breathlessness, mechanism-based antiemetics, laxatives always.
- Before concluding that a patient is dying, exclude reversible causes; once dying is diagnosed, individualise the care plan, prescribe anticipatory medications and communicate.
- Conduct goals-of-care and resuscitation conversations with a recommendation, empathy, and documentation; respect capacity, advance directives and law.
- Palliative emergencies (MSCC, SVCO, hypercalcaemia, bleeding) require rapid, goal-concordant decisions.
- Care extends to families before and after death; support staff too.

## Self-assessment

**Questions**

1. Define the "surprise question" and state its limitations.
2. Describe three illness trajectories and give an example of each.
3. A patient with advanced cancer on morphine has drowsiness, myoclonus and hallucinations with eGFR 20. What is the likely cause and what do you do?
4. Which antiemetic is best for nausea due to opioid or hypercalcaemia (chemoreceptor trigger zone), and which for gastric stasis?
5. List five features suggesting a patient is entering the dying phase, and four reversible conditions to exclude first.
6. What is the evidence about artificial feeding in advanced dementia?
7. How would you respond to a family asking you to conceal a prognosis from the patient?
8. What drugs and doses are typically included in anticipatory prescribing for the last days?
9. Distinguish palliative sedation from euthanasia.
10. A patient with known bone metastases develops new back pain and leg weakness. What do you do?

**Answers**

1. "Would I be surprised if this patient died in the next 12 months?" It prompts palliative assessment; it has limited sensitivity/specificity and should not be used as a standalone prognostic tool.
2. Cancer (preserved function then rapid decline: metastatic colorectal cancer); organ failure (gradual decline with exacerbations: heart failure, COPD); frailty/dementia (prolonged decline: advanced Alzheimer's).
3. Opioid toxicity due to accumulation of active metabolites in renal impairment (with possible dehydration/hypercalcaemia/infection). Reduce or stop morphine, switch to a renally safe opioid (fentanyl, buprenorphine, low-dose oxycodone/hydromorphone with advice), hydrate, check calcium, bladder and constipation, treat causes, symptomatic haloperidol if needed.
4. CTZ (opioids, hypercalcaemia, uraemia): haloperidol; gastric stasis: metoclopramide (or domperidone); raised ICP/vestibular: cyclizine ± dexamethasone.
5. Bedbound, semi-comatose, unable to take oral intake/medication, changes in breathing, mottling and cold extremities, reduced urine output, secretions; reversible: opioid toxicity, hypercalcaemia, infection, urinary retention (also dehydration, hypoglycaemia, hyponatraemia, drugs, adrenal insufficiency).
6. Feeding tubes do not prolong survival, prevent aspiration pneumonia, improve function or heal pressure ulcers, and are associated with agitation, restraint use and complications; careful hand feeding is recommended.
7. Explore the family's fears, explain that the patient has the right to know and that collusion often increases isolation, ask the patient what he wants to know, then share information accordingly with support.
8. PRN SC opioid (e.g., morphine 2.5 mg), midazolam 2.5–5 mg, haloperidol 0.5–1.5 mg, antiemetic (haloperidol/levomepromazine/cyclizine), anti-secretory (hyoscine butylbromide 20 mg or glycopyrronium); adjusted for renal function, previous doses and local protocols.
9. Palliative sedation: intention to relieve refractory suffering using proportionate titrated doses with monitoring, not to shorten life; euthanasia: intentional administration of drugs to end life at the request of the patient, legally restricted.
10. Suspect metastatic spinal cord compression: dexamethasone per protocol, immediate whole-spine MRI, urgent oncology/spinal team for radiotherapy or surgery; maintain spinal precautions and analgesia; decide in light of goals and prognosis.
