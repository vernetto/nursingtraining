## Chapter 5. Diagnosis with Limited Resources

### Learning objectives

After studying this chapter you should be able to:

1. Explain why history and examination are the core diagnostic tools in every setting, and why they matter even more where tests are scarce.
2. Use syndromic approaches and validated clinical scoring rules to make decisions without laboratory or imaging support.
3. Decide when empirical treatment or a therapeutic trial is justified, and how to monitor it.
4. Recognise the limits of what can be managed locally, and apply clear referral and transfer criteria.
5. Use time, follow-up and community resources as diagnostic tools.
6. Make equitable, ethical decisions when resources are constrained.

---

### 5.1 Resources are always limited

"Limited resources" is not only a description of rural clinics in low-income countries. It applies to:

- a night shift when the CT scanner is down or the radiologist is not on site;
- a primary care consultation of ten minutes with no same-day access to tests;
- a remote community, ship, expedition, or mission clinic;
- a hospital facing a surge of patients;
- a telehealth consultation without examination equipment;
- any setting in which a test is available but unaffordable for the patient.

Every clinician will face such situations. The skills in this chapter are therefore not a lesser form of medicine; they are **core clinical skills**, and the best way to become a good diagnostician with all the technology available is to be able to do without it.

A useful reminder: for many presentations, the history alone yields the correct diagnosis in a large share of cases; adding the examination improves it further; investigations then confirm or refine. The order matters, and when the later steps are unavailable, the earlier ones must be done exceptionally well.

---

### 5.2 Principles

1. **Maximise the value of the history.** Take it thoroughly and in the patient's language. Ask about duration, progression, exposures, contacts, travel, medicines (including traditional remedies), and what has been tried.
2. **Examine systematically and completely.** Vital signs (including respiratory rate and capillary refill), general inspection, and a focused examination for the likely systems. Use all senses.
3. **Think in syndromes and danger signs** when specific diagnoses cannot be confirmed.
4. **Use validated rules** rather than gut feeling where such rules exist.
5. **Know the local epidemiology.** Prevalence changes everything: malaria, tuberculosis, HIV, typhoid, dengue, schistosomiasis and others vary by region and season.
6. **Treat empirically when it is safe and reasonable**, and plan how to evaluate the response.
7. **Know when to refer or transfer,** and do it early, with stabilisation and clear communication.
8. **Use time and follow-up** as part of the work-up.
9. **Keep the record,** so the next person can build on your findings.
10. **Look after the patient's overall situation:** nutrition, safety, ability to return, costs, and family.

### 5.3 The bedside as a laboratory

#### Vital signs and simple measurements

A great deal can be learned with a few inexpensive instruments:

- **Respiratory rate**, counted for a full minute. It is among the most sensitive early signs of serious illness, and is often poorly recorded.
- **Heart rate, blood pressure, temperature.**
- **Pulse oximetry** where available. Remember its limits (poor perfusion, dark nail polish, carbon monoxide poisoning).
- **Capillary refill time**, **skin turgor**, **mucous membranes**, **mental state** (AVPU: Alert, responds to Voice, responds to Pain, Unresponsive).
- **Weight and height** (and mid-upper arm circumference in children) as nutritional markers.
- **Urine output**, which is a sensitive marker of perfusion.

#### Simple bedside tests

Where no laboratory exists, these are often available and informative:

- **Urine dipstick** (glucose, protein, blood, leucocytes, nitrites, ketones).
- **Capillary blood glucose** for any altered mental state or seizure.
- **Haemoglobin** by point-of-care meter, or by clinical pallor assessment (palmar creases, conjunctivae) with awareness of its limits.
- **Rapid diagnostic tests** (malaria, HIV, syphilis, pregnancy).
- **Peak flow**, **simple spirometry**.
- **Sputum microscopy** or rapid molecular testing for tuberculosis.
- **ECG** where available, and ideally with a systematic reading approach (Chapter 65).
- **Bedside ultrasound** if equipment and training exist (Chapter 67).

#### Examination as diagnostic test

The following findings have well-studied discriminating value and are free:

- **Raised JVP, third heart sound, bilateral basal crackles, peripheral oedema**: heart failure.
- **Dullness to percussion with bronchial breathing**: consolidation.
- **Stony dullness with absent breath sounds**: pleural effusion.
- **Hyperresonance with absent breath sounds in a patient who is unwell**: pneumothorax.
- **Rebound tenderness and guarding with rigidity**: peritonitis.
- **Pulsatile abdominal mass**: aneurysm.
- **Neck stiffness, photophobia, non-blanching rash with fever**: meningitis, meningococcaemia.
- **Unilateral leg swelling with calf tenderness**: deep vein thrombosis.

### 5.4 Syndromic and algorithmic approaches

Where specific tests are not available, **syndromic management** groups patients by clinical presentation and treats the likely causes according to local epidemiology. Examples include:

- **Integrated Management of Childhood Illness (IMCI)**: structured assessment of the sick child using danger signs (inability to drink, convulsions, lethargy, vomiting everything), cough or difficulty breathing (with fast breathing thresholds by age), diarrhoea (dehydration signs), fever (malaria risk), ear problems, malnutrition and anaemia (Chapter 59).
- **Syndromic management of sexually transmitted infections** (urethral discharge, genital ulcer, vaginal discharge, lower abdominal pain), in which one visit leads to treatment covering the most likely causes.
- **Symptom screening for tuberculosis**: cough of more than two weeks (or any cough in high-risk groups), fever, night sweats and weight loss trigger confirmatory testing.
- **WHO and national emergency triage tools** to sort patients quickly into immediate, urgent and routine categories.

The strengths of syndromic approaches are speed, consistency and the ability to be used by non-physician staff. Their weaknesses are over-treatment (some patients receive drugs they do not need) and loss of specificity (the exact cause remains unknown). They work best when:

- they are based on **good local data** on causes and resistance;
- they are **validated and regularly updated**;
- clinicians know **when to leave the algorithm** because the patient does not fit.

### 5.5 Clinical scoring rules

A **clinical prediction rule** (or score) combines several history and examination findings into an estimate of probability, sorting patients into risk groups. When validated in a population similar to yours, rules often perform as well as or better than unaided judgement, and they require no technology. Chapter 70 covers them in detail; some key examples to illustrate their role in a limited-resource setting:

| Question | Rule (example) | Typical use |
|---|---|---|
| Is this sore throat likely streptococcal? | Centor / McIsaac | Decide whether to test or treat; avoid antibiotics when probability is low |
| Is pneumonia severe? | CURB-65 (or CRB-65 without urea) | Decide on home care vs. admission; CRB-65 uses no laboratory test |
| Is DVT likely? | Wells score for DVT | Select patients for ultrasound or D-dimer |
| Is PE likely? | Wells score / PERC / Geneva | Decide whether imaging is needed |
| Is an ankle or knee X-ray needed? | Ottawa rules | Avoid unnecessary radiographs |
| Is a child with head injury likely to need a CT? | PECARN | Avoid unnecessary CT and radiation |
| Is appendicitis likely? | Alvarado score | Help decide between observation, imaging and surgery |
| Is the patient at risk of sepsis? | qSOFA, early warning scores | Prompt escalation (Chapter 39, 44) |

How to use rules wisely:

1. **Use them for the population they were derived in.** A rule validated in emergency department adults may not apply to children, pregnant women, or patients in a different region.
2. **They support, not replace, judgement.** If the rule says low risk but you are worried, think again and explain why.
3. **They shift probabilities,** they don't give certainties.
4. **Know what is not captured.** Rules often omit the "gestalt" of a very sick-looking patient.

### 5.6 Empirical treatment and therapeutic trials

#### Empirical treatment

**Empirical treatment** means starting treatment based on the most likely diagnosis without waiting for, or without access to, confirmatory tests. It is appropriate when:

- the **delay would be harmful** (sepsis, suspected bacterial meningitis, suspected severe malaria, anaphylaxis, status epilepticus);
- the **diagnosis is highly probable** by clinical features and local epidemiology;
- the **treatment is safe, effective and available;**
- **testing is impossible, delayed or unreliable.**

It must be coupled with:

- **Documented reasoning,** including the alternative diagnoses.
- **Specimen collection before treatment** where possible (blood cultures before antibiotics, malaria smear before antimalarials), because treatment may later make the diagnosis impossible to confirm.
- **Review** to check response and to rationalise (stop, narrow, or change).
- **Awareness of antimicrobial stewardship:** unnecessary or broad antibiotics promote resistance and cause harm.

#### Therapeutic trials (diagnostic therapy)

A **therapeutic trial** gives a treatment for a suspected diagnosis and uses the response as evidence. Examples include:

- A bronchodilator trial for suspected asthma (symptom and peak flow response).
- Proton pump inhibitor trial for dyspepsia (with caution about alarm features).
- Iron supplementation for iron-deficiency anaemia (look for a haemoglobin rise of about 10 g/L in 2 weeks, and investigate the cause).
- Antimalarial treatment for febrile illness in malaria-endemic areas when no test is available (a diminishing practice as rapid tests spread).

Cautions:

- A **response is not proof.** Many illnesses improve spontaneously, and the placebo effect is real. Conversely, a lack of response may mean the dose was inadequate, not that the diagnosis is wrong.
- Define in advance **what response you expect, and by when.**
- Avoid a trial when the treatment is risky, irreversible, or may mask evolving disease (e.g. giving strong analgesia for abdominal pain before an adequate surgical assessment is a debated area; modern evidence indicates appropriate analgesia does not impair diagnostic accuracy, but the assessment should still be done).
- If there is **no response, revisit the diagnosis** instead of escalating the dose indefinitely.

### 5.7 Using time and follow-up

When resources are limited, **time** often replaces tests:

- **Watchful waiting** with a defined review point.
- **Serial examinations** (for the child with abdominal pain, for instance), which can substitute for imaging.
- **Community health workers or phone follow-up** to check progress in patients who cannot return easily.
- **Written or verbal safety-netting** with specific, understandable return criteria (Chapter 73).
- **Patient-held records** so information survives referrals.

Key requirement: the follow-up system must be *realistic*. Telling a patient to "come back if worse" is meaningless if the next clinic is a day's journey away. The plan should reflect the patient's actual circumstances: transport, costs, language and literacy, support at home.

---

### 5.8 Knowing your limits: referral and transfer

No clinician or facility can manage everything. A wise clinician knows **what cannot be managed locally,** and acts early. Typical triggers for referral or transfer:

- **Instability that does not respond** to initial resuscitation.
- A condition needing **surgery, intensive care, specialised imaging,** or other facilities you lack (acute abdomen with peritonitis, obstructed labour, severe trauma, complicated malaria with organ failure).
- **Diagnostic uncertainty with serious red flags** that cannot be resolved locally.
- **High-risk groups** (immunocompromised patients, neonates, pregnant women with danger signs).

Good referral practice:

1. **Stabilise first** (airway, breathing, circulation, glucose, analgesia, antibiotics if needed).
2. **Communicate** with the receiving team in advance by phone or radio when possible.
3. **Send a clear note:** problem, findings, treatments given (with times), and what you need.
4. **Arrange a suitable escort** and transport, and ensure the patient can be accompanied by a relative.
5. **Don't delay for tests** that won't change the decision to transfer.
6. **Follow up** on what happened, to learn.

---

### 5.9 Telehealth and remote diagnosis

Remote consultations extend access but remove the physical examination. To use them well:

- **Take a more structured history,** with specific red-flag questions.
- **Ask the patient (or a carer) to perform simple examinations:** count respiratory rate, show the skin, press on the abdomen and describe findings, use a home thermometer or oximeter.
- **Use video where possible** to observe breathing, skin colour, speech and gait.
- **Set a low threshold for in-person assessment** in anything that might be serious.
- **Be explicit about the limits** of the consultation in your safety-netting.

---

### 5.10 Ethical and equitable use of limited resources

When resources are scarce, decisions are not purely technical. Principles to hold on to:

- **Be fair and consistent.** Decisions about who receives a test or bed should rest on clinical need and likely benefit, not on wealth, status or acquaintance.
- **Be transparent** with patients about what you can and cannot offer and why.
- **Do not abandon the patient** when a test is unavailable: offer the best alternative, an honest assessment, and a plan.
- **Avoid wasteful use** of scarce resources; do not order tests "to be safe" that deprive others.
- **Advocate** for the needed resources through appropriate channels, and report system failures.
- **Maintain dignity and confidentiality,** even in crowded or poorly equipped settings.

### 5.11 Worked cases

#### Case 1: Child with cough and fast breathing, rural clinic

*A 2-year-old boy has had 3 days of cough and fever. At a rural health post there is no X-ray and no oximeter. He is alert and drinking. His respiratory rate, counted over a full minute while calm, is 48 breaths/min. There is no chest indrawing, stridor or cyanosis.*

**Reasoning.** IMCI classifies fast breathing for a child aged 12 months to 5 years as 40 or more breaths per minute. With fast breathing and no danger signs or chest indrawing, he is classified as having **pneumonia** (non-severe), treated with oral amoxicillin and home care, with review in 2 days (or earlier if he worsens). A chest X-ray is not required for the management decision.

**Safety-net.** The caregiver is told to return immediately if he cannot drink, becomes drowsy or develops chest indrawing or difficulty breathing. Malaria risk should be considered if the area is endemic, and testing performed if available.

**Lesson.** A carefully counted respiratory rate replaces an X-ray for the decision to treat.

#### Case 2: Fever in an endemic area without a laboratory

*A 25-year-old man living in a malaria-endemic region has had 3 days of fever, headache, chills and malaise. A rapid diagnostic test is available and is positive. He is conscious, drinking, with a normal blood pressure and no jaundice, breathlessness or bleeding.*

**Reasoning.** Uncomplicated malaria; treat according to national guidelines with an artemisinin-based combination therapy, and give clear advice on completing the course and on danger signs.

**What if the test is unavailable?** In a high-transmission area, current practice is to test whenever possible, because many febrile illnesses are not malaria and treating all of them wastes drugs and misses other diagnoses (typhoid, pneumonia, urinary infection, bacterial sepsis, dengue, viral illnesses). When a test is truly unavailable in a high-risk setting, empirical treatment may be justified according to local policy, along with careful assessment for other causes and a review in 24–48 hours.

**Red flags prompting urgent referral:** altered consciousness, convulsions, severe anaemia, jaundice, respiratory distress, persistent vomiting, hypotension, dark urine. These indicate **severe malaria**, which requires parenteral treatment.

#### Case 3: Chest pain at a clinic with no ECG

*A 56-year-old man with hypertension and smoking history reports 30 minutes of central chest tightness with sweating, which began at rest. The nearest hospital is 90 minutes away; there is no ECG and no troponin test. He is pale and sweaty, pulse 98, blood pressure 150/90, oxygen saturation (by portable meter) 97%.*

**Reasoning.** Without tests, the diagnosis rests on history and risk factors: the story is classic for acute coronary syndrome. The danger of missing it is high and the benefit of early treatment is substantial.

**Action.**

1. Treat as suspected acute coronary syndrome: aspirin (if no contraindication such as allergy or active bleeding) according to local guidance, sublingual nitrate if blood pressure allows, analgesia, oxygen only if hypoxic, a calm and monitored environment.
2. Arrange **urgent transfer** to a facility with ECG and treatment capability, with a telephone call to the receiving team.
3. Document the times of onset, medications and examination findings. Send the note with the patient.
4. Do not wait for tests that are unavailable. Do not send the patient by his own transport without an escort if he is unstable.

**Lesson.** When tests are absent, the question is: "What can I safely do now, and how quickly can I get the patient to where the missing test or treatment is?"

#### Case 4: Abdominal pain in a child, serial examination

*A 9-year-old girl has had 14 hours of vague periumbilical pain, mild nausea and a temperature of 37.8 °C. At 14:00 the abdomen is soft with mild tenderness in the right iliac fossa without guarding. There is no ultrasound or surgeon on site, and the hospital is 2 hours away.*

**Reasoning.** The Alvarado score is borderline (perhaps 4–5), meaning appendicitis is possible but not certain. The danger (perforation) is real; the benign alternatives (mesenteric adenitis, gastroenteritis, constipation, urinary infection) are common.

**Plan.** Do a urinalysis; keep her under observation with nothing to eat and drink only small sips; **re-examine in 2–4 hours.** If pain localises, tenderness or guarding increases, or fever and vomiting worsen, arrange transfer to surgical care. If she improves and eats, review again in 24 hours with clear safety-net advice.

**Lesson.** Serial examination by the same careful clinician is a legitimate substitute for imaging, as long as it is *actually done and documented.*

#### Case 5: Anaemia without a laboratory

*A 30-year-old pregnant woman at 28 weeks complains of tiredness and breathlessness on climbing stairs. She appears pale, with pallor of conjunctivae and palmar creases, a pulse of 104, and no signs of heart failure. No haemoglobin measurement is available.*

**Reasoning.** Anaemia in pregnancy is common in many regions, mostly due to iron deficiency, often compounded by malaria, hookworm and folate deficiency. Pallor and tachycardia suggest moderate to severe anaemia. Severe anaemia in pregnancy carries risks for both mother and baby, especially around delivery.

**Plan.** Start iron and folic acid (with a dose appropriate for treatment of anaemia, according to national guidelines), treat for malaria if tested positive and give anthelmintic therapy as per protocol, arrange the earliest possible haemoglobin measurement (perhaps at a nearby health centre), plan delivery at a facility able to manage haemorrhage and transfusion, and review frequently. Urgent referral if there are signs of decompensation (breathlessness at rest, heart failure signs, syncope).

**Lesson.** Clinical signs and risk reasoning drive safe action even without a number, but the missing number is worth obtaining as soon as possible.

### 5.12 Common pitfalls

- **Assuming that "no test" means "no diagnosis."** A thorough history and examination often suffice.
- **Giving up the examination** because technology is unavailable.
- **Blanket treatment without reasoning,** which wastes resources and breeds resistance.
- **Failing to take specimens** before empirical therapy where this is feasible.
- **Delaying referral** in the hope of improvement.
- **Using rules out of context.**
- **Unrealistic safety-netting.**
- **Lack of documentation,** which loses information at handover.

---

### 5.13 Key points

- History and examination are the foundation of diagnosis in every setting, and the main tools when tests are unavailable.
- Count the respiratory rate; check glucose; look for danger signs.
- Use syndromic approaches and validated scores, within their populations, as support and not as a substitute for judgement.
- Empirical treatment is appropriate when delay is dangerous and the diagnosis is probable; document, collect specimens, review.
- Use time and serial examination purposefully, with a realistic follow-up plan.
- Know your limits: stabilise, communicate and refer early.
- Resource constraints raise ethical duties of fairness, transparency and advocacy.

### 5.14 Self-assessment

1. Why is respiratory rate such an important sign?
   *Answer: It is a sensitive, early indicator of serious illness (sepsis, pneumonia, metabolic acidosis, heart failure), requires no equipment, and is frequently under-recorded.*

2. List four conditions in which empirical treatment before confirmation is generally justified.
   *Answer (any four): suspected sepsis; bacterial meningitis; severe malaria; anaphylaxis; status epilepticus; suspected acute coronary syndrome before transfer; giant cell arteritis with visual symptoms.*

3. You start a therapeutic trial for a suspected diagnosis and the patient improves. Why is this not proof?
   *Answer: Many illnesses improve spontaneously, placebo effects occur, and other conditions may also respond to the same treatment.*

4. What should accompany a patient you refer to another facility?
   *Answer: Stabilising treatment first, advance communication with the receiving team, a clear written note (problem, findings, treatments with times, request), and an escort if needed.*

5. Why is "come back if worse" an inadequate safety net in some settings?
   *Answer: If the patient cannot realistically return quickly (distance, cost, transport, literacy), the plan has to be tailored: specific return criteria, community follow-up, a defined review time, or transfer now.*
