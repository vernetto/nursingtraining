## Chapter 34. Rash and Skin Complaints

### Learning objectives

After studying this chapter you should be able to:

1. Describe a skin lesion and eruption precisely, using the standard dermatological vocabulary (primary and secondary lesions, configuration, distribution).
2. Take a history that focuses on the time course, symptoms, drugs, exposures, infections, atopy, and systemic features, and examine the skin, mucous membranes, hair, nails, and lymph nodes systematically.
3. Recognise skin emergencies: meningococcal septicaemia, Stevens–Johnson syndrome/toxic epidermal necrolysis, DRESS, toxic shock and staphylococcal scalded skin syndromes, necrotising fasciitis, erythroderma, severe bullous disease, herpes zoster ophthalmicus, and eczema herpeticum.
4. Generate a differential diagnosis by morphological pattern: maculopapular, urticarial, vesicobullous, purpuric, papulosquamous, pustular, nodular, and pigmentary.
5. Diagnose common conditions: eczema, psoriasis, tinea, scabies, impetigo, cellulitis, herpes zoster, urticaria, drug eruptions, and recognise skin signs of systemic disease.
6. Approach pruritus without a primary rash, and recognise skin cancers (BCC, SCC, melanoma) using the ABCDE and "ugly duckling" rules.
7. Choose basic investigations (swabs, scrapings, biopsy, blood tests) and understand the potency ladder of topical steroids and other core treatments.

---

### 34.1 Why the skin deserves a systematic approach

Skin disease is common (about a quarter of primary care consultations involve a skin problem) and highly visible. The skin is also a **window to the whole body:** infections, drug reactions, autoimmune and malignant diseases, endocrine and metabolic disorders, and nutritional deficiencies all declare themselves on the skin, often before other signs.

Diagnosis is **pattern recognition built on precise description.** A skilled clinician describes before naming. "A red rash" can mean fifty things; "symmetrical, erythematous, blanching macules and papules, starting on the trunk and spreading to the limbs, sparing the palms and soles, in a febrile child" narrows the field to a few.

Three habits help:

1. **Describe the lesion and the eruption** in standard language (below).
2. **Look at the whole patient** (skin, mucosae, hair, nails, nodes, temperature, vital signs).
3. **Decide first whether this is an emergency,** then which pattern, then which specific condition.

### 34.2 The language of dermatology

#### Primary lesions (the basic lesion that arises *de novo*)

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Macule** | Flat, circumscribed colour change, <1 cm (patch if >1 cm) |
| **Papule** | Raised, solid, <1 cm (plaque if >1 cm and flat-topped) |
| **Nodule** | Solid, raised, >1 cm, extending into the dermis or subcutis |
| **Vesicle** | Fluid-filled blister <1 cm (bulla if >1 cm) |
| **Pustule** | Pus-filled vesicle |
| **Wheal (urticaria)** | Transient, itchy, raised, pale or pink oedematous plaque (hours) |
| **Cyst** | Encapsulated, fluid- or semi-solid-filled lump |
| **Petechia / purpura / ecchymosis** | Non-blanching red-purple macules from extravasated blood (petechia <3 mm; purpura 3 mm–1 cm; ecchymosis >1 cm) |
| **Telangiectasia** | Dilated superficial blood vessels that blanch |
| **Burrow** | Linear track in scabies |
| **Comedone** | Plugged follicle (acne) |

#### Secondary changes (evolve from primary lesions)

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Scale** | Flaking of the stratum corneum (fine: pityriasis; silvery: psoriasis; greasy: seborrhoeic dermatitis) |
| **Crust** | Dried exudate (serous: honey-coloured in impetigo; haemorrhagic) |
| **Erosion** | Superficial loss of epidermis, heals without scarring |
| **Ulcer** | Loss of epidermis and dermis, heals with scarring |
| **Excoriation** | Scratch marks (evidence of itch) |
| **Lichenification** | Thickened skin with accentuated markings from chronic rubbing |
| **Fissure** | Linear crack |
| **Atrophy** | Thinning of the skin (shiny, wrinkled, translucent) |
| **Scar** | Fibrous replacement |
| **Hyper- and hypopigmentation** | Post-inflammatory change |

#### Configuration, shape and distribution

- **Shape:** round, oval, annular (ring-shaped), arcuate, polycyclic, target (three zones: erythema multiforme), linear (Koebner phenomenon, contact dermatitis, scratch), serpiginous (cutaneous larva migrans), reticulate (net-like: livedo reticularis), dermatomal (herpes zoster), grouped (herpetiform).
- **Distribution:** localised or generalised; symmetrical or asymmetrical; **flexural** (atopic eczema), **extensor** (psoriasis), **photodistributed** (face, V of neck, backs of hands: photosensitivity, SLE, drugs), **acral** (hands and feet), **intertriginous** (candida), **trunk-predominant** (pityriasis rosea, viral exanthems), **seborrhoeic** areas (scalp, face, chest), **palms and soles** (secondary syphilis, hand-foot-mouth disease, Rocky Mountain spotted fever, Kawasaki disease, erythema multiforme).
- **Colour:** red/pink (inflammation, vasodilatation), violaceous (lichen planus, dermatomyositis, vasculitis), brown (melanin, haemosiderin), yellow (xanthoma, jaundice), white (vitiligo), blue-grey.
- **Blanching:** press with a glass; blanching erythema vs non-blanching purpura.
- **Palpation:** texture, temperature, tenderness, induration, depth, mobility, fluctuance, Nikolsky sign (epidermis separates with lateral pressure: pemphigus, SSSS, TEN).

#### Skin of colour

Erythema is harder to see on dark skin; it appears as **violaceous, dusky, brown or grey** rather than red. Look at texture, warmth, and secondary changes (scale, oedema). Purpura and cyanosis may be difficult to detect; check **mucous membranes, conjunctivae, palms and soles, and nail beds.** Post-inflammatory hyper- and hypopigmentation are prominent and may be the patient's main concern. Include photographs of skin of different tones in your learning, and avoid assuming "no rash" when the colour change is subtle.

### 34.3 History and examination of the skin

#### History

- **Onset and duration:** hours (urticaria, drug reactions, anaphylaxis, viral exanthems), days (infections, contact dermatitis), weeks to months (eczema, psoriasis, tinea), years (chronic dermatoses, tumours).
- **Where did it start and how did it spread?** (centrifugal, central, acral; sun-exposed sites).
- **Symptoms:** **itch** (severity, nocturnal: scabies, atopic eczema, cholestasis), **pain** (burning or tenderness: zoster, cellulitis, vasculitis, erythema nodosum; pain out of proportion: necrotising fasciitis), numbness, stinging, bleeding, weeping.
- **Triggers and relieving factors:** sun, heat, cold, water, sweat, stress, pressure, foods, plants, cosmetics, metals (nickel), occupational substances, detergents, friction.
- **Medications:** **every new drug in the previous 8 weeks** (including over-the-counter, herbal, vaccines, topical agents), timing in relation to the rash (typical latency for morbilliform eruptions 4–14 days; DRESS 2–8 weeks; SJS/TEN 1–4 weeks; urticaria minutes to hours or days).
- **Associated systemic symptoms:** fever, malaise, joint pain, mouth ulcers, eye redness, cough, diarrhoea, weight loss, sore throat, lymph nodes, dyspnoea, wheeze, swelling of lips or tongue (angioedema).
- **Exposures:** infectious contacts (school, nursery, household: scabies, measles, impetigo, chickenpox, hand-foot-mouth), sexual history (syphilis, HIV, herpes), travel (tropical infections, tick bites), animals, insect bites, freshwater, gardening, occupational chemicals.
- **Past history:** atopy (eczema, asthma, hay fever), psoriasis, autoimmune disease, cancer, immunosuppression, HIV, diabetes, venous disease, previous skin cancer, sunburn history and sun exposure, tanning-bed use.
- **Family history:** atopy, psoriasis, skin cancer, genetic skin conditions.
- **Treatments tried:** topical steroids, antifungals, moisturisers (and their effect, because partial treatment alters appearance: *tinea incognito*).
- **Impact:** sleep, work, mood, appearance, relationships.

#### Examination

- **Wash hands; use good light, a dermatoscope, a magnifier, a ruler.** Ask permission and ensure privacy and a chaperone for sensitive areas.
- **Inspect all of the skin** (not just the area of complaint); examine scalp, hair, nails, mouth and genitalia as relevant.
- **Describe the primary lesion, secondary changes, colour, size, borders, configuration, distribution, symmetry.**
- **Palpate** for texture, depth, tenderness, temperature, induration; **blanch** the lesion; **stretch** the skin to reveal scale (Wickham striae, Auspitz sign).
- **Check mucous membranes** (mouth, conjunctivae, genitalia): erosions, ulcers, thrush, Koplik spots.
- **Lymph nodes and organomegaly;** joints; vital signs.
- **Special tests:** Nikolsky sign, Darier sign (urticaria on stroking: mastocytosis), dermographism, diascopy, Wood's light (fluorescence), dermoscopy (pigmented lesions, scabies, warts).

#### Investigations

| Test | Use |
|---|---|
| **Skin scraping with 10–20% KOH, fungal culture** | Tinea, candida, pityriasis versicolor |
| **Bacterial swab and culture; viral swab (PCR)** | Impetigo, abscess, wound infection; HSV/VZV (vesicles) |
| **Scabies preparation (burrow scraping, dermoscopy)** | Scabies mites, eggs |
| **Skin biopsy (punch, shave, excision)** | Diagnosis of inflammatory conditions, neoplasms, vasculitis, bullous disease; **direct immunofluorescence** (perilesional biopsy) for pemphigus, pemphigoid, dermatitis herpetiformis, lupus; culture for atypical infections |
| **Patch testing** | Allergic contact dermatitis |
| **Prick testing and specific IgE** | Immediate allergic reactions (not for chronic urticaria) |
| **Blood tests** | FBC and eosinophils, ESR/CRP, U&E, LFTs, autoantibodies (ANA, ENA, dsDNA), complement, ANCA, cryoglobulins, serology (syphilis, HIV, hepatitis), antibodies for bullous disease (anti-BP180/230, desmoglein 1/3), tTG-IgA (dermatitis herpetiformis), TSH, ferritin, glucose |
| **Urinalysis** | Renal involvement in vasculitis, IgA vasculitis, lupus |
| **Dermoscopy** | Melanocytic and non-melanocytic tumours; reduces unnecessary excisions and detects early melanoma |
| **Imaging, other** | CT, MRI, PET for suspected deep infection or malignancy; ultrasound for lumps |

### 34.4 Skin emergencies: do not miss these

| Condition | Key features | Immediate action |
|---|---|---|
| **Meningococcal septicaemia** | Fever, headache, vomiting, rapidly evolving **non-blanching petechial or purpuric rash,** shock, altered consciousness; may begin with blanching macules | Immediate IV ceftriaxone (or benzylpenicillin in the community), fluids, ICU; do not wait for LP or investigations (Chapter 21, 41) |
| **Toxic shock syndrome** | Fever, diffuse macular erythroderma ("sunburn"), hypotension, multi-organ involvement; tampon use, wound infection (staphylococcal), or streptococcal | Resuscitation, remove source, IV antibiotics including clindamycin, IVIG |
| **Staphylococcal scalded skin syndrome (SSSS)** | Young children (and immunosuppressed or renal-impaired adults); fever, tender erythema, **superficial blisters, Nikolsky sign positive, desquamation,** no mucosal involvement | IV anti-staphylococcal antibiotic, fluids, analgesia, emollients |
| **Stevens–Johnson syndrome / TEN** | See 34.9 | Stop the culprit drug, burn unit/ICU care |
| **DRESS** | See 34.9 | Stop the drug, specialist care |
| **Necrotising soft-tissue infection** | **Pain out of proportion to appearance,** rapid spread, bullae, dusky skin, crepitus, systemic toxicity; diabetes, IV drug use, post-surgery | **Urgent surgical exploration and debridement,** broad antibiotics (including clindamycin); LRINEC score is not a rule-out |
| **Erythroderma** (>90% body surface area involvement) | Generalised redness and scaling, fever, hypothermia, high-output heart failure, fluid and protein loss; causes: psoriasis, eczema, drugs, cutaneous T-cell lymphoma | Admit, emollients, fluids, temperature control, find the cause |
| **Severe pemphigus / pemphigoid, TEN-like** | Widespread blisters, mucosal erosions | Admit; steroids, immunosuppression |
| **Herpes zoster ophthalmicus** | Vesicles in the V1 dermatome, **tip of the nose (Hutchinson sign),** red eye, vision change | Oral/IV antivirals, same-day ophthalmology review |
| **Eczema herpeticum** | Atopic eczema with sudden painful punched-out vesicles and erosions, fever | Immediate IV aciclovir; swabs; ophthalmology if periocular |
| **Anaphylaxis / angioedema** | Urticaria, flushing, swelling of lips/tongue, wheeze, stridor, hypotension | Intramuscular adrenaline; airway (Chapter 31) |
| **Acute limb ischaemia, calciphylaxis, purpura fulminans** | Painful dusky/necrotic lesions | Vascular/specialist urgent care |
| **Severe cellulitis with sepsis** | Rapidly spreading erythema, systemic signs | IV antibiotics, mark margins |
| **Kawasaki disease** | Fever ≥5 days, rash, conjunctivitis, red cracked lips, extremity changes, cervical node | IVIG and aspirin (Chapter 21) |
| **Viral haemorrhagic fever, anthrax, mpox** | Travel/exposure, characteristic lesions | Isolation, notify |
| **Pyoderma gangrenosum and other neutrophilic dermatoses** | Rapidly enlarging painful ulcer with undermined violaceous edge; IBD, RA, leukaemia | Exclude infection, treat with steroids |
| **Cutaneous drug reactions with angioedema or systemic involvement** | | Stop drug |

### 34.5 Approach by pattern

#### A. Maculopapular (morbilliform) eruptions

**Features:** blanching erythematous macules and papules, often confluent, usually symmetrical, spreading from the trunk to the limbs.

| Cause | Clues |
|---|---|
| **Viral exanthems** | Children and young adults; mild fever, malaise; self-limiting; rubella, parvovirus B19, enteroviruses, adenovirus, HHV-6 (roseola infantum: high fever for 3 days then rash after defervescence), EBV |
| **Measles** | Prodrome of fever, cough, coryza, conjunctivitis, **Koplik spots** (white spots on buccal mucosa), then a rash starting on the face and behind the ears and spreading downward over 3 days; unvaccinated; **notifiable** |
| **Rubella** | Mild, postauricular and occipital lymphadenopathy, arthralgia in adults; teratogenic in pregnancy |
| **Parvovirus B19 (fifth disease)** | "Slapped-cheek" erythema in children, lacy reticulate rash on limbs; adults: arthropathy; risk in pregnancy, haemolytic anaemia, immunosuppression |
| **Scarlet fever** | Group A streptococcus pharyngitis, **fine "sandpaper" erythematous rash** on trunk, **strawberry tongue,** circumoral pallor, Pastia lines (accentuated in skin folds), desquamation of fingers and toes |
| **Secondary syphilis** | Non-itchy symmetrical copper-coloured macules/papules, **involving palms and soles,** mucous patches, condylomata lata, generalised lymphadenopathy, alopecia; serology |
| **HIV seroconversion, EBV, CMV** | Fever, pharyngitis, lymphadenopathy, rash, mouth ulcers |
| **Dengue, chikungunya, Zika** | Travel; fever, arthralgia, headache, rash (islands of white in a sea of red in dengue) |
| **Kawasaki disease, Still disease, SLE** | Systemic features |
| **Drug eruption (exanthematous):** the **most common cutaneous drug reaction** | 4–14 days after starting the drug (earlier if previous exposure); aminopenicillins (**ampicillin or amoxicillin rash in EBV**), cephalosporins, sulfonamides, allopurinol, anticonvulsants, NSAIDs; may be mildly itchy; stop the drug if suspected; watch for DRESS (fever, facial oedema, eosinophilia, organ involvement) |
| **Rickettsial (spotted fever, scrub typhus), typhoid, leptospirosis, Lyme** | Travel, tick exposure, eschar |
| **Graft-versus-host disease** | Transplant recipients |

#### B. Urticaria and angioedema

- **Wheals** (itchy, pink or pale, raised, lasting <24 hours each, moving from site to site) ± angioedema. **Acute urticaria** (<6 weeks): infections (viral), foods, drugs (penicillin, NSAIDs, opioids, ACE inhibitor-related angioedema), insect stings, contact; **chronic spontaneous urticaria** (>6 weeks): autoimmune, no trigger found in most, associated with thyroid autoimmunity; **inducible urticaria** (dermographism, cold, pressure, cholinergic, solar, aquagenic).
- **Urticarial vasculitis:** individual lesions **last >24 hours,** painful or burning rather than itchy, leave bruising or pigmentation; low complement, systemic features; biopsy.
- **Treatment:** identify and avoid triggers, **second-generation non-sedating H₁ antihistamines** (cetirizine, fexofenadine, loratadine, bilastine) regularly, up-dosing to **four times the standard dose** if needed, **omalizumab** for refractory chronic urticaria, short oral steroid courses for severe acute flares; avoid NSAIDs and codeine if exacerbating; **adrenaline** if anaphylaxis.
- **Investigations** in chronic urticaria: limited (FBC, CRP/ESR, TSH, ± anti-TPO); extensive allergy tests are not useful.

#### C. Vesicular and bullous eruptions

| Cause | Clues |
|---|---|
| **Herpes simplex** | Grouped vesicles on an erythematous base, lips, genitals; recurrent; tingling prodrome; PCR swab |
| **Herpes zoster (shingles)** | **Unilateral dermatomal** pain, then grouped vesicles that crust in 7–10 days; older adults, immunosuppression; **start antivirals (aciclovir, valaciclovir, famciclovir) within 72 hours** of the rash (or later if new lesions, ophthalmic, immunosuppressed, severe); analgesia; complications: post-herpetic neuralgia, ophthalmic, Ramsay Hunt syndrome (ear vesicles, facial palsy), disseminated disease in immunosuppressed; **zoster vaccination** |
| **Varicella (chickenpox)** | Crops of vesicles at different stages ("dew drops on a rose petal"), centripetal, fever, itch; adults and pregnancy: severe; immunosuppressed; treat with aciclovir if high-risk |
| **Hand, foot and mouth disease** | Children, coxsackievirus; oral ulcers, vesicles on palms and soles; adults with severe disease possible |
| **Impetigo** | Honey-coloured crusts, perioral, children; **bullous impetigo** (*S. aureus*); topical antibiotic (fusidic acid, mupirocin) or oral flucloxacillin if extensive; exclude from school until lesions crusted or 48 hours of treatment |
| **Contact dermatitis (acute)** | Vesicles, erythema, edema in the exposure pattern (linear streaks from plants: Rhus; hands; jewellery sites) |
| **Dyshidrotic eczema (pompholyx)** | Deep itchy vesicles on palms and sides of fingers |
| **Bullous pemphigoid** | Older adults (>70), **tense blisters** on erythematous or urticarial bases, itch, flexures and limbs; mucosa rarely involved; associated with drugs (DPP-4 inhibitors, furosemide, spironolactone), neurological disease; **anti-BP180/BP230 antibodies,** biopsy with immunofluorescence; high-potency topical steroids (clobetasol) or oral prednisolone, doxycycline, immunosuppressants |
| **Pemphigus vulgaris** | **Flaccid blisters and painful oral erosions** (often first), Nikolsky sign positive, middle age; anti-desmoglein 1/3 antibodies; high-dose steroids, rituximab; can be fatal |
| **Dermatitis herpetiformis** | **Intensely itchy grouped vesicles on elbows, knees, buttocks, scalp;** coeliac disease association; IgA deposits in perilesional skin; dapsone and gluten-free diet |
| **SJS/TEN, fixed drug eruption** | See 34.9 |
| **Burns, friction blisters, frostbite, insect bites (papular urticaria, bullae)** | History |
| **Epidermolysis bullosa, porphyria cutanea tarda** (photodistributed blisters, fragile skin on the backs of the hands, alcohol, hepatitis C, haemochromatosis), **bullous lupus, linear IgA disease** | |

#### D. Purpura and petechiae

First, **blanch test:** non-blanching = extravasated blood.

| Type | Causes |
|---|---|
| **Thrombocytopenia (flat petechiae, mucosal bleeding)** | Immune thrombocytopenia (ITP), **TTP/HUS, DIC,** leukaemia, marrow failure, drugs (heparin, linezolid, quinine), hypersplenism, viral infection |
| **Coagulopathy** | Liver disease, vitamin K deficiency, warfarin, DIC |
| **Vascular fragility** | **Senile (actinic) purpura** (dorsa of forearms and hands, older adults), steroids, scurvy, Ehlers–Danlos syndrome, amyloid |
| **Vasculitis (palpable purpura)** | **IgA vasculitis (Henoch–Schönlein:** children, buttocks and legs, abdominal pain, arthritis, haematuria), cutaneous small-vessel vasculitis (drugs, infection), ANCA-associated vasculitis, cryoglobulinaemia, lupus, endocarditis (Janeway lesions) |
| **Infections** | **Meningococcaemia,** other bacteraemia, Rocky Mountain spotted fever, viral haemorrhagic fever, enterovirus |
| **Trauma, increased pressure** | Coughing, vomiting (periorbital and facial petechiae), tourniquet |

**Action:** vital signs, FBC with platelet count, coagulation, urinalysis (blood and protein), CRP, U&E; consider cultures, ANCA, complement. **Platelets <20 × 10⁹/L** or active bleeding is an emergency; **fever + non-blanching rash = antibiotics immediately.**

#### E. Papulosquamous (scaly) conditions

| Condition | Features |
|---|---|
| **Psoriasis (plaque)** | **Well-demarcated red plaques with silvery-white scale,** extensor surfaces (elbows, knees), scalp, sacrum, umbilicus; nail pitting and onycholysis; **Koebner phenomenon** (new lesions at sites of trauma); **Auspitz sign** (pinpoint bleeding on scale removal); guttate psoriasis after streptococcal infection (children, young adults); pustular and erythrodermic forms; associated psoriatic arthritis, metabolic syndrome, depression; treat with topical steroids and vitamin D analogues, phototherapy, methotrexate, ciclosporin, biologics (IL-17, IL-23, TNF inhibitors); avoid systemic steroids (rebound) |
| **Eczema (atopic dermatitis)** | Itchy, ill-defined erythematous scaly patches; **flexural in older children and adults,** face and extensors in infants; lichenification, excoriation; personal or family atopy; dry skin; infection (staph, herpes) complicates; **emollients, topical steroids (potency tailored to site), calcineurin inhibitors,** avoid triggers, treat infection, wet wraps, phototherapy, systemic agents (dupilumab, JAK inhibitors) for severe disease |
| **Seborrhoeic dermatitis** | Greasy yellow scale in scalp, eyebrows, nasolabial folds, ears, chest; antifungal shampoo (ketoconazole), mild topical steroid; severe in HIV and Parkinson disease |
| **Contact dermatitis (irritant/allergic)** | Distribution by exposure (hands, eyelids, neck, feet); patch testing for allergic form; avoid triggers; barrier creams |
| **Nummular eczema** | Coin-shaped itchy plaques on limbs |
| **Asteatotic eczema** | Dry cracked "crazy-paving" skin on shins in older adults, winter |
| **Venous (stasis) eczema** | Lower legs, varicose veins, haemosiderin pigment, oedema |
| **Tinea (dermatophyte)** | **Annular plaques with active scaly edge and central clearing;** tinea corporis, cruris (groin, sparing scrotum), pedis (interdigital, moccasin distribution), capitis (children, alopecia, kerion), unguium (nails); KOH and culture; topical terbinafine or azoles, oral terbinafine for nails, scalp, extensive disease; beware steroid-modified tinea incognito |
| **Pityriasis rosea** | **Herald patch** (single oval plaque) followed by a crop of oval scaly pink patches along skin cleavage lines on the trunk ("Christmas-tree" pattern); young adults; self-limiting 6–8 weeks; exclude secondary syphilis |
| **Lichen planus** | **Purple, polygonal, pruritic, flat-topped papules** on wrists, ankles, lower back; **Wickham striae,** oral lace-like white lines, nail changes; hepatitis C association; potent steroid |
| **Pityriasis versicolor** | Hypo- or hyperpigmented fine scaly macules on trunk, young adults, humid climate; yeast; topical selenium or antifungal |
| **Secondary syphilis, mycosis fungoides (cutaneous T-cell lymphoma), drug eruption** | Biopsy |
| **Lichen sclerosus** | White atrophic plaques on genitalia, itch; potent topical steroid; risk of SCC |

#### F. Pustular eruptions

Acne vulgaris (comedones, papules, pustules), rosacea (central facial erythema, flushing, papules, pustules, telangiectasia, no comedones), folliculitis (bacterial, pseudomonas "hot tub," fungal), hidradenitis suppurativa (recurrent painful nodules, abscesses, sinuses in axillae, groin, under breasts), pustular psoriasis, candidal intertrigo, AGEP (acute generalised exanthematous pustulosis: drug-induced, within 48 hours, fever, sheets of sterile pustules), gonococcaemia.

#### G. Nodules and painful lesions

| Condition | Features |
|---|---|
| **Erythema nodosum** | Tender, red, deep nodules on the **shins,** may be bilateral; causes: streptococcal infection, sarcoidosis (Löfgren syndrome), TB, IBD, drugs (oral contraceptives, sulphonamides), pregnancy, Behçet, lymphoma; resolves over weeks; treat the cause, NSAIDs |
| **Erythema multiforme** | **Target (iris) lesions** with three zones, acral, symmetrical; mild form with minimal mucosal involvement: HSV (most common), Mycoplasma, drugs; distinguish from SJS |
| **Cellulitis / erysipelas** | See 34.8 |
| **Abscess, boil (furuncle), carbuncle** | Red, painful, fluctuant nodule; MRSA; drainage |
| **Epidermoid cyst, lipoma, dermatofibroma** | Chapter 31 |
| **Pyoderma gangrenosum** | See above |
| **Vasculitic nodules, polyarteritis nodosa, erythema induratum** | Biopsy |
| **Panniculitis** | |
| **Skin cancers** | See 34.11 |

#### H. Pigmentary disorders

- **Hypopigmentation/depigmentation:** vitiligo (acquired, autoimmune, well-demarcated white patches; associated thyroid disease, pernicious anaemia, Addison, type 1 diabetes), post-inflammatory hypopigmentation, pityriasis alba, tinea versicolor, albinism, tuberous sclerosis (ash-leaf macules), leprosy (anaesthetic hypopigmented patches).
- **Hyperpigmentation:** melasma (face, pregnancy, oral contraceptives, sun), post-inflammatory, **acanthosis nigricans** (velvety dark plaques in the axillae, neck, groin: insulin resistance, obesity, **malignancy** if sudden and extensive), Addison disease (palmar creases, scars, buccal mucosa), haemochromatosis, drug-induced (amiodarone slate-grey, minocycline, antimalarials), café-au-lait macules (≥6 >5 mm in children suggests neurofibromatosis type 1), lentigines.

### 34.6 Skin signs of systemic disease

| Sign | Associated condition |
|---|---|
| **Malar (butterfly) rash with nasolabial sparing, discoid plaques, photosensitivity, oral ulcers** | SLE |
| **Heliotrope rash (violaceous eyelids), Gottron papules (over knuckles), shawl sign, mechanic's hands, proximal muscle weakness** | Dermatomyositis (screen for cancer in adults) |
| **Skin tightening, sclerodactyly, telangiectasia, calcinosis, digital ulcers, Raynaud phenomenon** | Systemic sclerosis |
| **Palpable purpura, livedo reticularis, ulcers, nodules** | Vasculitis |
| **Erythema nodosum** | Sarcoidosis, IBD, infection |
| **Lupus pernio (violaceous plaques on nose and cheeks), papules in scars** | Sarcoidosis |
| **Pyoderma gangrenosum** | IBD, RA, leukaemia, MGUS |
| **Necrobiosis lipoidica (yellow-brown shiny atrophic plaques on shins), acanthosis nigricans, skin tags, diabetic dermopathy, eruptive xanthomata, bullous diabeticorum** | Diabetes and insulin resistance |
| **Xanthelasma, tendon xanthomata, eruptive xanthomata** | Dyslipidaemia |
| **Dry coarse skin, hair loss, periorbital oedema, pretibial myxoedema** | Hypo- and hyperthyroidism (Graves) |
| **Hyperpigmentation of creases and mucosae; vitiligo** | Addison disease |
| **Purple striae, moon face, buffalo hump, bruising, acne** | Cushing syndrome |
| **Palmar erythema, spider naevi, jaundice, bruising, pruritus, caput medusae** | Chronic liver disease |
| **Pruritus, uraemic frost, half-and-half nails, calciphylaxis, perforating dermatoses** | Chronic kidney disease |
| **Pallor, koilonychia, glossitis, angular stomatitis** | Iron deficiency |
| **Flushing, telangiectasia, diarrhoea, wheeze** | Carcinoid syndrome |
| **Café-au-lait spots, neurofibromas, axillary freckling** | Neurofibromatosis type 1 |
| **Acral lentigines, mucocutaneous pigmentation (peri-oral), GI polyps** | Peutz–Jeghers syndrome |
| **Migratory necrolytic erythema (glucagonoma), Sister Mary Joseph nodule, tripe palms, acanthosis nigricans (sudden), Leser–Trélat (sudden numerous seborrhoeic keratoses), acquired ichthyosis, erythema gyratum repens** | Paraneoplastic markers |
| **Livedo, necrosis, purpura** | Antiphospholipid syndrome, cryoglobulinaemia, cholesterol emboli |
| **Janeway lesions, Osler nodes, splinter haemorrhages, petechiae** | Infective endocarditis |
| **Dermatitis herpetiformis, aphthous ulcers** | Coeliac disease |
| **Erythema migrans** | Lyme disease |
| **Photosensitive blistering, hypertrichosis** | Porphyria |
| **Oral thrush, seborrhoeic dermatitis, extensive molluscum, Kaposi sarcoma, herpes zoster in a young person** | HIV |
| **Hair loss** (alopecia areata, telogen effluvium after illness or delivery, scarring) | Thyroid disease, iron deficiency, SLE, drugs, stress |

### 34.7 Pruritus (itch) without a primary rash

**Generalised itch with normal-looking skin (apart from scratch marks)** needs a systemic work-up.

| Cause | Clues |
|---|---|
| **Xerosis (dry skin)** | Older adults, winter, hot baths; the commonest cause; fine scale, "crazy paving" |
| **Cholestasis** | Itch (often palms and soles, worse at night) with or without jaundice; primary biliary cholangitis, obstructive jaundice, pregnancy cholestasis (Chapter 27) |
| **Chronic kidney disease** | Uraemic pruritus in dialysis patients |
| **Iron deficiency** | Often even without anaemia |
| **Thyroid disease** | Hyperthyroidism (more) and hypothyroidism |
| **Diabetes mellitus** | Generalised or genital itch |
| **Haematological** | **Polycythaemia vera** (aquagenic pruritus after hot water), **Hodgkin lymphoma** (itch, night sweats, weight loss, nodes), leukaemia, myelodysplasia |
| **Malignancy (paraneoplastic)** | Solid tumours, brain tumours (nasal itch) |
| **Infection and infestation** | **Scabies** (burrows, finger webs, wrists, nocturnal itch, close contacts also itchy), lice, HIV, hepatitis C, parasites |
| **Drugs** | Opioids, aspirin, ACE inhibitors, chloroquine, statins, antibiotics, hydroxyethyl starch |
| **Neuropathic** | Brachioradial pruritus, notalgia paraesthetica, post-herpetic, multiple sclerosis |
| **Psychogenic / psychiatric** | Delusional infestation, anxiety, depression, obsessive disorders (a diagnosis of exclusion) |
| **Pregnancy** | Polymorphic eruption of pregnancy, pemphigoid gestationis, cholestasis of pregnancy |

**Approach:** examine for primary lesions, scabies, lymph nodes, hepatosplenomegaly; **tests:** FBC, film, ferritin, U&E, LFTs (including ALP, bilirubin, bile acids in pregnancy), TSH, glucose/HbA1c, HIV and hepatitis serology, coeliac serology (selected), chest X-ray if lymphoma suspected. **Treatment:** emollients, avoid hot baths, cool environment, sedating antihistamines at night (limited), topical agents (menthol, capsaicin), treat the cause; cholestatic itch: cholestyramine, rifampicin, naltrexone, sertraline; renal itch: gabapentin, difelikefalin, UVB; refractory: referral.

### 34.8 Skin infections and infestations

#### Bacterial

- **Impetigo:** see above.
- **Cellulitis and erysipelas:** acute spreading erythema, warmth, swelling and tenderness (erysipelas has a sharply demarcated raised edge and affects face or leg; cellulitis involves deeper tissue); fever, lymphangitis; port of entry (tinea pedis, ulcers, wound); **risk factors:** oedema, lymphoedema, obesity, diabetes, venous disease, previous cellulitis. **Mimics (pseudocellulitis):** venous (stasis) eczema, lipodermatosclerosis, DVT, contact dermatitis, gout, ruptured Baker cyst, drug eruption; bilateral "cellulitis" is rarely infective. **Treatment:** flucloxacillin or alternative (e.g. clarithromycin, doxycycline if allergic) oral for 5–7 days; IV for systemic toxicity; mark the margins; elevate; treat predisposing factors; compression for lymphoedema once infection resolves; prophylaxis for recurrent episodes. **Red flags:** pain out of proportion, rapid progression, bullae, skin necrosis, crepitus, hypotension: **necrotising fasciitis** (Chapter 21).
- **Abscess, furunculosis, carbuncle:** *Staphylococcus aureus* (including MRSA); incision and drainage is the key treatment; antibiotics for surrounding cellulitis, systemic signs, immunosuppression, face; decolonisation for recurrent disease.
- **Erythrasma, intertrigo, folliculitis, hidradenitis.**

#### Viral

- **Warts (HPV), molluscum contagiosum** (umbilicated papules, children, sexually transmitted in adults; resolves), **herpes viruses** (above), **measles, parvovirus, hand-foot-mouth disease,** mpox (vesiculopustular lesions, fever, lymphadenopathy, genital/anorectal in sexual networks).

#### Fungal

- **Tinea** (above), **candidiasis** (intertrigo with satellite lesions, oral/genital thrush, nappy rash, paronychia; risk factors: diabetes, steroids, antibiotics, immunosuppression, obesity), **pityriasis versicolor.**

#### Infestations

- **Scabies:** *Sarcoptes scabiei*; **intense itch, worse at night;** burrows in finger webs, wrists, axillae, waistline, genitalia (men), nipples/breasts (women); pruritic papules and nodules; secondary excoriation and impetiginisation; household contacts; **crusted (Norwegian) scabies** in immunosuppressed and elderly care-home residents (highly contagious). **Treatment:** **permethrin 5% cream** applied to the whole body from the neck down (and head in children and older adults) for 8–14 hours, repeated after 7 days; oral ivermectin as alternative (2 doses a week apart; essential for crusted scabies); **treat all household and close contacts simultaneously;** launder bedding and clothes at ≥50 °C; itch may persist for weeks after cure (post-scabetic itch); manage outbreaks in institutions.
- **Pediculosis (head lice), pubic lice, body lice** (vagrants, trench fever), **bed bugs, fleas** (papular urticaria).
- **Cutaneous larva migrans** (serpiginous track, tropical beaches), **tungiasis,** **myiasis.**

### 34.9 Drug eruptions

Cutaneous drug reactions are common (2–3% of hospitalised patients) and range from trivial to fatal.

| Type | Features | Time to onset | Typical drugs | Management |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Morbilliform (exanthematous)** | Symmetrical blanching macules and papules, itch, trunk to limbs; mild fever; no mucosal involvement | 4–14 days | Aminopenicillins, cephalosporins, sulfonamides, allopurinol, anticonvulsants, NSAIDs | Stop the drug, antihistamines, topical steroids; resolves in 1–2 weeks with desquamation |
| **Urticaria/angioedema/anaphylaxis** | Wheals, swelling, wheeze, hypotension | Minutes–hours | Penicillins, NSAIDs, opioids, contrast media, ACE inhibitors (angioedema only) | Stop; adrenaline if anaphylaxis |
| **Fixed drug eruption** | Well-defined round violaceous or dusky plaque (sometimes bulla) recurring at the same site on re-exposure; lips, genitalia, hands; leaves hyperpigmentation | 30 minutes–8 hours after re-exposure | Tetracyclines, NSAIDs, paracetamol, sulfonamides, barbiturates, carbamazepine | Stop the drug; topical steroid |
| **Photosensitivity** (phototoxic, photoallergic) | Exaggerated sunburn or eczematous eruption in sun-exposed sites, sparing under chin and behind ears | Hours–days | Doxycycline, thiazides, amiodarone, NSAIDs, fluoroquinolones, St John's wort, voriconazole | Sun protection, stop drug |
| **Acute generalised exanthematous pustulosis (AGEP)** | Sudden oedematous erythema with numerous sterile non-follicular pustules, fever, neutrophilia | Within 48 hours | Antibiotics (aminopenicillins, macrolides), antifungals, diltiazem | Stop drug, supportive; resolves within 2 weeks |
| **DRESS (drug reaction with eosinophilia and systemic symptoms)** | **Fever, facial oedema, diffuse morbilliform rash or erythroderma, lymphadenopathy, eosinophilia (or atypical lymphocytes), hepatitis, nephritis, pneumonitis,** reactivation of HHV-6/EBV; can be fatal (about 5–10%) | **2–8 weeks** (latent) after starting | **Allopurinol, carbamazepine, lamotrigine, phenytoin, phenobarbital, sulfonamides, dapsone, minocycline, vancomycin, abacavir** | **Stop the drug immediately;** systemic corticosteroids (prolonged taper over weeks), organ support, specialist care; monitor for relapse and autoimmune sequelae (thyroiditis) |
| **SJS / TEN** | Prodrome of fever, malaise, sore eyes; **painful dusky red or purpuric macules and targetoid lesions** that coalesce and blister, **epidermal detachment, Nikolsky sign positive, mucosal erosions** (mouth, eyes, genitals; >2 sites); SJS <10% body surface area (BSA); SJS/TEN overlap 10–30%; **TEN >30% BSA;** mortality 5–10% (SJS) to 30%+ (TEN) | **4–28 days** (usually 1–3 weeks) | **Allopurinol, carbamazepine (HLA-B*1502 in Han Chinese, Thai and some South-East Asian populations), lamotrigine (risk with rapid titration and valproate co-prescription), phenytoin, sulfonamides (including sulfasalazine), nevirapine, NSAIDs (oxicam), aminopenicillins, quinolones** | **Stop the culprit drug immediately;** admit to ICU/burns unit; **SCORTEN** for prognosis; fluid and electrolyte replacement, nutrition, wound care (non-adherent dressings), temperature regulation, analgesia, ophthalmology for eyes (amniotic membrane), pulmonary and renal monitoring, infection surveillance; immunomodulators (ciclosporin, IVIG, etanercept, or corticosteroids) in specialist units; avoid re-exposure and chemically related drugs; genetic screening before carbamazepine in at-risk populations |
| **Drug-induced vasculitis / serum sickness-like reaction** | Palpable purpura or urticaria, fever, arthralgia | 1–3 weeks | Penicillins, cefaclor, sulphonamides, biologics | Stop drug |
| **Drug-induced lupus** | Photosensitive rash, arthralgia, serositis; anti-histone antibodies | Months | Hydralazine, procainamide, isoniazid, minocycline, TNF inhibitors | Stop drug |
| **Others:** lichenoid, bullous pemphigoid-like, acneiform (steroids, EGFR inhibitors), hyperpigmentation, hair loss, nail changes, calciphylaxis (warfarin) | | | | |

**Key principle:** consider drugs in **every** new rash; list all drugs with dates; **stop the most likely culprit** unless it is essential, and document the reaction (including severity and an "allergy" or "adverse reaction" label with details). Establishing the culprit helps avoid mislabelling (penicillin allergy labels are wrong in most cases), but never rechallenge after SJS/TEN, DRESS, or other severe reactions.

**Red flags of severe cutaneous adverse reactions (SCAR):** skin pain, mucosal erosions, blistering or skin sloughing, facial oedema, high fever, lymphadenopathy, systemic organ involvement, eosinophilia, rapid progression.

### 34.10 Principles of treatment

#### General measures

- **Emollients** are the foundation of treatment of dry and inflammatory skin conditions (apply liberally and frequently; use soap substitutes; ointments for very dry skin, creams for moderate, lotions for hairy or exudative areas; avoid perfumed products).
- **Avoid triggers;** treat infection; manage itch; address sleep and mood.
- **Patient education,** written instructions, review plan.

#### Topical corticosteroids

Use the **lowest potency that is effective,** for the shortest time, with correct quantity. The amount is measured in **fingertip units (FTU):** one FTU (squeezed from the tube along an adult index finger from tip to first crease, about 0.5 g) covers an area of two adult palms (about 2% body surface area). Adult one-arm: 3 FTU; one leg: 6 FTU; trunk (front): 7 FTU; face and neck: 2.5 FTU.

| Potency | Examples | Typical sites |
|---|---|---|
| **Mild** | Hydrocortisone 0.5–2.5% | Face, flexures, genitals, children |
| **Moderate** | Clobetasone butyrate 0.05%, betamethasone valerate 0.025% | Eczema flares on limbs and trunk |
| **Potent** | Betamethasone valerate 0.1%, mometasone, fluticasone | Thick eczema, psoriasis, lichen planus on body |
| **Very potent** | Clobetasol propionate 0.05% | Palms, soles, severe plaque psoriasis, lichen sclerosus, pemphigoid: short courses under supervision |

**Side effects:** skin atrophy, striae, telangiectasia, perioral dermatitis, steroid rosacea, tinea incognito, glaucoma and cataract (periocular), systemic absorption in children, **topical steroid withdrawal** with prolonged use of potent steroids (especially on the face). Avoid on the face unless mild potency for a short time; **never use in untreated infection.**

#### Other topical and systemic agents

- **Calcineurin inhibitors** (tacrolimus, pimecrolimus) for face and flexures in eczema.
- **Vitamin D analogues** (calcipotriol) in psoriasis; **coal tar, dithranol, retinoids.**
- **Antibiotics** (topical for localised impetigo; oral for cellulitis, extensive impetigo), **antivirals** (aciclovir, valaciclovir, famciclovir), **antifungals** (terbinafine, azoles).
- **Antihistamines:** non-sedating for urticaria; sedating at night short-term for itch.
- **Systemic steroids:** for severe acute inflammatory or bullous disease; avoid in psoriasis.
- **Immunosuppressants and biologics** (methotrexate, ciclosporin, azathioprine, mycophenolate, TNF/IL-17/IL-23 inhibitors, dupilumab, omalizumab, JAK inhibitors, rituximab) under specialist care with monitoring.
- **Phototherapy (narrowband UVB, PUVA).**
- **Isotretinoin** (acne): strict pregnancy prevention.

#### When to refer to dermatology

Diagnostic uncertainty, suspected skin cancer, severe or extensive disease, failure to respond, severe drug reactions, bullous disease, erythroderma, need for systemic therapy, impact on quality of life; **urgent same-day referral** for SJS/TEN, DRESS, erythroderma, suspected melanoma, necrotising infection, severe bullous disease.

### 34.11 Skin cancer recognition

#### Basal cell carcinoma (BCC)

- Most common cancer. Pearly or translucent papule/nodule with **telangiectasia, rolled edge, central ulceration ("rodent ulcer")**; sun-exposed areas (face, nose, ears, upper trunk); slow growth; rarely metastasises but locally destructive. Treatment: excision, Mohs micrographic surgery (face), curettage, radiotherapy, topical imiquimod or 5-fluorouracil for superficial lesions, hedgehog inhibitors for advanced disease.

#### Squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) and actinic keratosis

- **Actinic keratoses:** rough, scaly, erythematous macules/papules on sun-damaged skin; precancerous (small progression risk per lesion); treat with cryotherapy, topical 5-FU, imiquimod, diclofenac, photodynamic therapy.
- **SCC:** firm, erythematous, scaly or crusted nodule, often ulcerated, rapidly growing, on sun-exposed skin, lip, ear; **higher-risk in immunosuppressed patients (transplant recipients), chronic scars, Marjolin ulcer;** can metastasise; excision with margins; **keratoacanthoma** (rapidly growing crater-like nodule) is treated as SCC.

#### Melanoma

- Malignant tumour of melanocytes; **the deadliest skin cancer;** early detection and excision are curative; prognosis depends on **Breslow thickness.**
- **ABCDE rule:** **A**symmetry, **B**order irregularity, **C**olour variation, **D**iameter >6 mm (or growing), **E**volving (change in size, shape, colour, symptoms such as itch, bleeding, crusting). The **"ugly duckling" sign:** a mole that looks different from the patient's other moles. **EFG:** Elevated, Firm, Growing (nodular melanoma). Amelanotic melanoma may be pink or red and easily missed.
- **Types:** superficial spreading (most common), nodular (rapid, may lack pigment), lentigo maligna (face of older adults), acral lentiginous (palms, soles, subungual; more common in skin of colour, delayed diagnosis).
- **Risk factors:** fair skin, many or atypical naevi, family history, previous melanoma, intermittent intense sun exposure and sunburns, tanning-bed use, immunosuppression, age.
- **Management:** **urgent referral** (suspected melanoma pathway); **complete excision biopsy with a narrow margin (do not shave or incise partially)** and histology; wide local excision according to Breslow thickness; sentinel lymph node biopsy for intermediate thickness; systemic treatment (immunotherapy, targeted BRAF/MEK inhibitors) for advanced disease.
- **Prevention:** sun protection, sunscreen, avoidance of tanning beds, skin self-examination, surveillance of high-risk individuals.

#### Others

- **Merkel cell carcinoma** (rapid-growing red-violet nodule in older sun-damaged skin), **cutaneous lymphoma** (mycosis fungoides: persistent patches/plaques resembling eczema or psoriasis that fail treatment), **Kaposi sarcoma** (violaceous plaques in HIV or older adults of Mediterranean origin), **dermatofibrosarcoma protuberans,** **angiosarcoma** (scalp of older adults; bruise-like), **metastatic skin deposits.**

**Use dermoscopy** when available, take clear clinical photographs for monitoring, and **biopsy** any lesion that does not fit a benign pattern or that is changing.

### 34.12 Special populations

- **Children:** common conditions: atopic eczema, nappy dermatitis, viral exanthems, impetigo, molluscum, warts, head lice, scabies, hand-foot-mouth disease, birthmarks (infantile haemangioma: propranolol for problematic lesions), Kawasaki disease (recognise early), child abuse (bruises in non-mobile infants, patterned injuries, burns: Chapter 59 and safeguarding).
- **Older adults:** xerosis, pruritus, stasis eczema, venous ulcers, pressure injuries, skin cancer, bullous pemphigoid, herpes zoster, scabies in care homes, cellulitis, drug eruptions (polypharmacy), skin tears.
- **Pregnancy:** physiological changes (striae, melasma, linea nigra); **specific dermatoses of pregnancy:** polymorphic eruption of pregnancy (itchy papules in striae, third trimester, benign), atopic eruption of pregnancy, **pemphigoid gestationis** (blisters around the umbilicus, risk to the fetus), **intrahepatic cholestasis of pregnancy** (itch without rash, raised bile acids; Chapter 27).
- **Immunosuppressed and HIV:** atypical and severe infections, Kaposi sarcoma, drug reactions, skin cancer; low threshold for biopsy and culture.
- **Skin of colour:** see 34.2; conditions of relevance include keloids, pseudofolliculitis barbae, post-inflammatory pigmentary change, central centrifugal cicatricial alopecia, acral melanoma.
- **Occupational dermatology:** hand eczema in healthcare workers, hairdressers, cleaners; patch test and workplace modification.

### 34.13 Worked cases

#### Case 1: A rash that is not benign

*A 3-year-old girl has had fever and vomiting for 12 hours, and her mother notices a few "spots" on the legs. She is irritable and drowsy, pale and mottled with a capillary refill of 4 seconds. The spots do not fade under a pressed glass.*

**Reasoning.** **Fever + non-blanching petechial rash + signs of shock = meningococcal septicaemia** until proven otherwise.

**Action.** Immediate IV or IM ceftriaxone (or benzylpenicillin if in the community), IV fluids, oxygen, urgent senior and paediatric intensive care; blood cultures, PCR, clotting, glucose, lactate; LP only when stable and not contraindicated; notify public health, chemoprophylaxis for close contacts.

#### Case 2: Drug rash or viral exanthem?

*A 7-year-old boy, prescribed amoxicillin for sore throat on day 1, develops a widespread itchy blanching maculopapular rash on day 6. He is afebrile, well, and has no mucosal lesions. There is cervical lymphadenopathy and mild splenomegaly.*

**Reasoning.** An **aminopenicillin-induced exanthem** (non-allergic, common in EBV infection); features suggest underlying infectious mononucleosis. True IgE-mediated penicillin allergy is unlikely (delayed onset, morbilliform, benign).

**Action.** Stop the amoxicillin, symptomatic treatment, check EBV serology and FBC if clinically indicated; document as "rash with amoxicillin during EBV, not a true allergy" and consider formal allergy assessment later.

#### Case 3: Stevens–Johnson syndrome

*A 34-year-old woman, 18 days after starting lamotrigine at an escalated dose with sodium valproate, develops fever, sore eyes and a painful dusky rash on the trunk that blisters, with oral erosions and conjunctivitis. Nikolsky sign positive; 15% body surface area detached.*

**Reasoning.** **SJS/TEN overlap** triggered by lamotrigine.

**Action.** **Stop lamotrigine immediately,** transfer to a burns unit or ICU, fluid and electrolyte replacement, nutritional support, wound care, ophthalmology consult, analgesia, ciclosporin or other immunomodulator per specialist, monitor for sepsis; label lamotrigine as contraindicated; consider alternative antiseizure medication (levetiracetam).

#### Case 4: Scabies

*A 28-year-old man has had 3 weeks of intense itch, especially at night, with papules between his fingers, on wrists, axillae and genitals. His partner and flatmate are also itchy. Dermoscopy shows a "delta-wing jet" sign; scraping shows mites.*

**Reasoning.** **Scabies.**

**Action.** Permethrin 5% cream to the whole body from the neck down, repeat in 7 days (or oral ivermectin); treat all household and sexual contacts at the same time; wash bedding and clothes at ≥50 °C or seal for 72 hours; advise that itch may persist 2–4 weeks; consider secondary infection and sexual health screen.

#### Case 5: Bullous pemphigoid

*An 82-year-old man with Parkinson disease, on a DPP-4 inhibitor for diabetes, has 3 weeks of itchy urticarial plaques followed by tense blisters on the flexures and thighs. No mouth ulcers. Biopsy: subepidermal blister; direct immunofluorescence: linear IgG and C3 at the basement membrane; anti-BP180 positive.*

**Reasoning.** **Bullous pemphigoid** (drug-associated: DPP-4 inhibitors).

**Action.** Stop the DPP-4 inhibitor, ultra-potent topical steroid (clobetasol) or oral prednisolone with taper, doxycycline as steroid-sparing agent, wound care, treat infection, monitor glucose and bone health; dermatology follow-up.

#### Case 6: Necrotising fasciitis

*A 58-year-old diabetic man presents with severe left calf pain after a minor scratch two days ago. The skin is moderately red but extremely painful, with a dusky patch and some bullae. HR 124, BP 92/50, temperature 38.9 °C, lactate 4.5.*

**Reasoning.** **Pain out of proportion,** systemic toxicity, dusky skin with bullae: **necrotising soft-tissue infection.**

**Action.** Immediate resuscitation, broad-spectrum antibiotics (meropenem or piperacillin-tazobactam plus clindamycin and vancomycin), **urgent surgical exploration and debridement** (do not delay for imaging), ICU, IVIG in some streptococcal cases, repeat debridement, glycaemic control.

#### Case 7: Early melanoma

*A 47-year-old woman has a 9-mm pigmented lesion on her back that has become darker and irregular over 6 months, with an itchy sensation. It has asymmetry, irregular borders, and variegated colours. Dermoscopy: atypical network and blue-white veil.*

**Reasoning.** **Suspected melanoma.**

**Action.** Urgent referral; complete excision biopsy with 2-mm margins, histology (Breslow thickness, ulceration, mitoses), wide local excision, sentinel node biopsy as indicated, skin and nodal examination, education on sun protection and surveillance, family risk assessment.

#### Case 8: Herpes zoster ophthalmicus

*A 71-year-old man has 3 days of burning pain on the right forehead and a unilateral eruption of grouped vesicles over the right forehead and the tip of the nose. He has a red, painful right eye with photophobia.*

**Reasoning.** **Herpes zoster in the V1 dermatome** with Hutchinson sign (nasociliary involvement) predicts ocular involvement.

**Action.** Start oral valaciclovir or aciclovir immediately, **same-day ophthalmology review** (keratitis, uveitis, glaucoma, retinal necrosis), analgesia, topical treatment as directed by the ophthalmologist, neuralgia management; advise zoster vaccination later.

#### Case 9: Psoriasis

*A 36-year-old man has had red, scaly plaques on his elbows, knees and scalp for 5 years, with silvery scale, pitted nails, and recent worsening after a sore throat; he has morning stiffness in the fingers.*

**Reasoning.** **Plaque psoriasis** with nail disease and possible psoriatic arthritis.

**Action.** Emollients, topical vitamin D analogue plus potent steroid, scalp treatment; assess for arthritis (screen with PEST or specialist), cardiovascular risk and mood; escalate to phototherapy, methotrexate, or biologics; avoid systemic steroids.

#### Case 10: Palpable purpura in a child

*A 6-year-old boy has a purpuric rash on the buttocks and legs, with abdominal pain and swollen knees, after a cold. Platelets and clotting are normal; urinalysis shows blood 2+ and protein 1+. BP normal.*

**Reasoning.** **IgA vasculitis (Henoch–Schönlein purpura).**

**Action.** Supportive treatment; check BP and urinalysis at each review for at least 6 months (renal involvement), analgesia, watch for abdominal complications (intussusception, GI bleeding) and testicular involvement; refer to paediatric nephrology if proteinuria or renal impairment.

### 34.14 Common pitfalls

- **Naming before describing** (labelling with a diagnosis without recording morphology and distribution).
- **Not testing for blanching** in a febrile child with a rash.
- **Missing drug causes** (not taking a full list of recent drugs); mislabelling a viral exanthem as penicillin allergy.
- **Relying on appearance in dark skin** (missing erythema or purpura).
- **Failing to examine the mouth, eyes, genitalia, nails, hair** and lymph nodes.
- **Treating "eczema" with potent steroids on the face,** or using steroids for undiagnosed rashes (tinea incognito, scabies).
- **Using steroid creams for scabies and impetigo.**
- **Failing to treat contacts in scabies.**
- **Missing necrotising infection** in "cellulitis" with severe pain.
- **Over-diagnosing cellulitis** in bilateral stasis changes and lipodermatosclerosis.
- **Delaying antivirals in zoster** and missing eye involvement.
- **Missing melanoma** (amelanotic, subungual, acral); partial biopsy of a suspected melanoma.
- **Dismissing itch without a rash** (cholestasis, renal, haematological, thyroid, iron deficiency, lymphoma).
- **Failing to biopsy** a persistent or atypical eruption.
- **Starting systemic steroids in psoriasis** (rebound pustular or erythrodermic flares).
- **Re-exposing patients to the drug after a severe cutaneous reaction.**

### 34.15 Key points

- Describe skin lesions precisely: primary lesion, secondary change, shape, distribution, blanching, symptoms; examine the whole skin, mucosae, nails, hair and nodes.
- First exclude emergencies: fever with non-blanching rash, SJS/TEN, DRESS, toxic shock, necrotising infection, erythroderma, severe blistering, zoster ophthalmicus, eczema herpeticum, anaphylaxis.
- Review all drugs for any new rash; morbilliform eruptions are most common; severe cutaneous adverse reactions need immediate drug withdrawal and specialist care.
- Group eruptions by pattern: maculopapular, urticarial, vesiculobullous, purpuric, papulosquamous, pustular, nodular, pigmentary.
- Itch without a rash needs a systemic work-up.
- Common conditions: eczema (emollients and steroids), psoriasis, tinea (KOH, antifungals), scabies (permethrin plus contacts), impetigo, cellulitis (and its mimics), zoster (antivirals early), urticaria (non-sedating antihistamines).
- Use the potency ladder and fingertip units for topical steroids.
- Recognise skin cancers: BCC, SCC, melanoma (ABCDE, ugly duckling); excise suspected melanoma completely and promptly.
- Many systemic diseases announce themselves on the skin.

### 34.16 Self-assessment

1. A febrile child has a rash that does not blanch under a glass. What do you do?
   *Answer: Treat as meningococcal septicaemia: immediate IV/IM ceftriaxone (or benzylpenicillin), fluids, oxygen, urgent senior and intensive care review; do not delay for investigations.*

2. List the features distinguishing SJS/TEN from a simple morbilliform drug rash.
   *Answer: Skin pain, dusky/purpuric macules with blistering and epidermal detachment, positive Nikolsky sign, mucosal erosions (especially eyes, mouth, genitals), fever and systemic illness, onset 1–3 weeks after the drug.*

3. Describe scabies treatment and who should be treated.
   *Answer: Permethrin 5% cream over the whole body for 8–14 hours, repeated in a week (or oral ivermectin); all household members and close contacts simultaneously, with laundering of bedding and clothes.*

4. What is the ABCDE rule for melanoma?
   *Answer: Asymmetry, Border irregularity, Colour variation, Diameter >6 mm (or growing), Evolution/change.*

5. Name three causes of generalised itch without a rash.
   *Answer (any three): xerosis, cholestasis, chronic kidney disease, iron deficiency, thyroid disease, polycythaemia, lymphoma, diabetes, drugs, HIV, scabies (early).*

6. Which fingertip-unit amount covers two adult palms of skin?
   *Answer: One fingertip unit (about 0.5 g).*
