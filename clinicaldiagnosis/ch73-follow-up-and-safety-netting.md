# Chapter 73. Follow-up and Safety-Netting

## Learning objectives

After studying this chapter you should be able to:

1. Define safety-netting and explain why diagnosis is a process over time, not a single event, so that uncertainty must be managed rather than eliminated.
2. Describe the components of effective safety-netting: what to watch for, when to act, how to act, who to contact, and who is responsible.
3. Plan follow-up by matching interval and format to the diagnostic uncertainty, the natural history and the risk of deterioration ("time as a diagnostic test").
4. Design closed-loop systems for test results, referrals, discharge summaries and incidental findings so that nothing falls between settings.
5. Tailor safety-netting to context: primary care, emergency department, hospital discharge, telephone/remote consultations, children and carers, older adults, patients with limited health literacy or language barriers.
6. Provide clear verbal and written advice (return criteria, red-flag symptoms, contact numbers), check understanding with teach-back, and document it.
7. Recognise failure modes (lost results, ambiguous ownership, premature closure, communication gaps, over-reassurance) and the cognitive and system defences against them.
8. Apply safety-netting principles to common clinical scenarios: head injury, abdominal pain, chest pain, fever in children, back pain, early pregnancy bleeding, suspected cancer, mental health crisis, and to the management of medication changes.

> **Note.** Wording, intervals and thresholds in written advice must be adapted to local guidelines and to the individual patient. The examples are illustrative.

## 1. Why safety-netting matters

### 1.1 Diagnosis unfolds over time

At the first contact, many illnesses are undifferentiated: early appendicitis, meningitis, cancer, sepsis and ectopic pregnancy can look benign. Studies of malpractice claims and diagnostic error consistently show that **failure of follow-up, failure to act on abnormal results, and failure to communicate return precautions** are leading contributors to missed or delayed diagnosis. A well-designed safety net converts an uncertain situation into a controlled one:

> "I think this is most likely X. I cannot be sure. Here is what I expect to happen; if something different happens, here is what to do and who will see you."

This is the core of "diagnostic uncertainty management" (Neighbour's safety-netting: "What do I expect to happen? What would tell me I am wrong? What will I do if so?").

### 1.2 Definition

**Safety-netting** is a consultation technique and a system of measures that ensures that a patient (and carers) know **what to watch for**, **when and how to seek help**, and that the clinician or service has **arranged follow-up** and **keeps track of pending items**. It combines:

1. **Information to the patient** (what is likely, expected course, red flags, how to get help).
2. **Planned follow-up** (review appointments, telephone calls, tests).
3. **System safeguards** (result tracking, referral tracking, recall lists, alerts).
4. **Documentation** (what was said, what was arranged, who is responsible).

### 1.3 Time as a diagnostic tool

For self-limiting or evolving conditions, observation over a defined period, with explicit review points, is a legitimate and powerful test ("watchful waiting"). Examples: viral illness vs sepsis, mild abdominal pain, benign headache, non-specific back pain. The trial of time is safe only when:

- Dangerous diagnoses are **sufficiently unlikely** at present (thresholds in chapter 69).
- The patient can **recognise worsening** and **access care** quickly.
- **Follow-up is arranged** or the patient knows how to re-enter the system.
- The **natural history** of the dangerous alternatives is compatible with the delay.

## 2. Components of an effective safety net

### 2.1 The "SAFE-NET" checklist

| Element | Questions |
|---|---|
| **S**pecific symptoms | Which exact symptoms or signs should trigger action? (Not "if it gets worse" alone) |
| **A**ction | What should the patient do (call, attend ED, phone 999, take medication)? |
| **F**ramework of time | When: "if not better within 48 hours", "immediately", "within 2 weeks" |
| **E**xpected course | What is the usual course? How long will symptoms last? |
| **N**umber / **N**ame of contact | Who to call, hours, out-of-hours route, language support |
| **E**vidence of understanding | Teach-back; written/translated information |
| **T**racking | Who is responsible for pending tests, referrals, follow-up? Is it recorded? |

### 2.2 Tailoring content

- **Language and literacy**: plain terms ("black or tarry stools", "a rash that does not fade when you press a glass on it"), short lists (3–5 items), numbers instead of ambiguous words ("if the temperature is above 38 °C for more than 3 days"), pictures or translated leaflets, interpreters, teach-back.
- **Carers**: involve parents, partners, care-home staff; give them direct contact numbers; for children, include parental instinct ("if you feel something is not right").
- **Digital**: SMS reminders, patient portals, apps, video visits; confirm access.
- **Social context**: living alone, lack of transport, caring responsibilities, fear of cost, immigration concerns, previous bad experiences; address barriers (who can accompany, how to reach services).
- **Cultural and religious factors**: fasting, festivals, preferences for gender of clinician.

### 2.3 Telling patients what to expect (including "good news")

"Most sore throats get better by themselves in about a week. If you are not improving by day 7, or you develop X or Y, contact us." Specifying the expected course avoids unwarranted alarm and unwarranted complacency.

### 2.4 Defining ownership

For each pending item, explicitly assign: **who orders, who receives, who acts, who informs the patient, and what happens if that person is away**. A safety net with no owner is a hole.

### 2.5 Documentation

Record: the working diagnosis and differential considered, the key negatives and red flags checked, the advice given (verbatim where critical), the follow-up arranged, pending tests with the responsible clinician, and the patient's understanding. Example:

> "Impression: viral URTI; no red flags; chest clear; SpO₂ 98%. Advised: fluids, paracetamol; expect improvement by day 7–10. Return if SpO₂ concerns, breathlessness, chest pain, fever > 3 more days, drowsiness. Written leaflet given; teach-back confirmed; patient to call if not improving."

## 3. Follow-up planning

### 3.1 Choosing the interval

| Situation | Typical follow-up |
|---|---|
| Possible serious illness, uncertain, patient discharged | Within 12–24 hours (phone call, repeat assessment) |
| Acute self-limiting infection with some uncertainty | Review in 48–72 hours or "if no better in 2–3 days" |
| Unexplained symptom with low-risk features | 1–2 weeks |
| Persistent symptoms/lab abnormality | 2–6 weeks; repeat tests |
| New medication with monitoring needs | Per drug (INR days, U&E 1–2 weeks after ACE inhibitor, TFT 6–8 weeks, lithium levels) |
| Chronic disease | Per guideline (3–6 months) |
| Mental health crisis | Within 24–72 hours (face-to-face or phone), earlier with high risk; crisis plan |
| Suspected cancer, tests pending | Specific date to check results and referral |
| After hospital discharge | Primary care within 48–72 hours (high risk) to 7 days; medication reconciliation |

### 3.2 Follow-up format

Face-to-face, phone, video, nurse-led, community team, specialist clinic, patient-initiated follow-up (PIFU) for stable chronic disease, remote monitoring (BP, glucose, SpO₂, symptom diaries). Choose the format that provides the information needed to answer the question ("Has the abdominal tenderness migrated?" needs examination; "Has the cough resolved?" can be asked by phone).

### 3.3 What the follow-up visit should do

1. **Review outcome**: Did the predicted course occur? New symptoms?
2. **Re-examine** and **re-evaluate** the diagnosis; consider alternative diagnoses if not as expected.
3. **Check tests and responses to treatment**.
4. **Adjust the plan**: escalate, de-escalate, refer, stop treatment.
5. **Reinforce** safety-netting and set the next step.
6. **Close the loop**: record resolution or ongoing plan.

### 3.4 Non-attendance and loss to follow-up

Plan for **DNA** (did not attend): flag high-risk patients; call and text reminders; outreach by community nurses; liaise with family/carers; consider social barriers; escalate if the risk is high (e.g., suspected cancer, anticoagulation monitoring, antipsychotic depot injections, TB treatment). A missed appointment in a high-risk patient is a clinical event requiring action, not an administrative event.

## 4. Closing the loop: systems for results, referrals and discharge

### 4.1 Test results

Failures in test-result follow-up are among the most frequent systems errors. Principles:

1. **The ordering clinician owns the result** until it is explicitly handed over. Communicate who will review results if you are away (delegation, shared inbox, covering colleague).
2. **Tell patients** when and how they will receive results (and what "no news" means). Avoid "no news is good news".
3. **Tracking**: results pending at discharge must be listed in the discharge summary with the named responsible clinician and the action required. Electronic tools can highlight outstanding results and alert when abnormal.
4. **Abnormal results**: act within defined timeframes; document the plan; inform patient; arrange follow-up.
5. **Incidental findings**: record them, communicate to the GP/patient, and ensure ownership for follow-up (for example, pulmonary nodule surveillance schedule).
6. **Critical values**: phone to a clinician and document read-back.
7. **Radiology**: act on "recommend follow-up" statements; use standardised templates and tracking registries.
8. **Microbiology**: culture results after antibiotic start or after discharge need a review mechanism.
9. **Pathology and screening**: recall systems for cervical, breast, bowel screening; "failsafe" processes to catch unreturned samples.

### 4.2 Referrals

- Send referrals with enough information (chapter 72).
- Record the referral and **track acknowledgment and appointment dates**, especially for urgent or cancer pathways.
- **Chase** after a defined interval (e.g., 2 weeks without appointment).
- Tell the patient what to expect and to contact you if not heard from the service within X days.
- Maintain a **referral register** for suspected-cancer pathways; audit conversion and delays.

### 4.3 Discharge and transitions of care

Discharge is a high-risk transition. Essential content of the discharge summary and handover:

1. **Diagnosis** (and uncertainty: "working diagnosis"), procedures, key results.
2. **Medication changes** (new, stopped, changed) with reasons; allergies.
3. **Pending results** and who will act.
4. **Follow-up arrangements** (appointments, tests, community referrals).
5. **Actions for the GP/community** with timeframes ("please check U&E in 1 week").
6. **Information given to the patient** (diagnosis, red flags, who to contact, driving/work advice).
7. **Functional status and care needs**; capacity; advance care plan/escalation status.
8. Send the summary **promptly** (within 24–48 hours; ideally at discharge) and to the patient.

Ensure **medication reconciliation** on admission and discharge, and a **community pharmacy review** for complex regimens. After discharge, early contact (phone call at 48–72 hours) reduces readmissions in high-risk groups.

### 4.4 Handovers between shifts and teams

Use structured tools (SBAR, I-PASS: Illness severity, Patient summary, Action list, Situation awareness and contingency planning, Synthesis by receiver). Include "if–then" contingency plans: "If the patient's lactate rises above 4 or MAP falls below 65, call ICU." Include pending tests and clear owner.

### 4.5 Medication safety-netting

When starting or changing drugs, communicate: purpose, how to take, common side effects, **serious adverse effects requiring urgent action** (for example, anaphylaxis, jaundice, black stools, severe diarrhoea, suicidal thoughts after antidepressant start, muscle pain with statins and dark urine, bleeding on anticoagulants, hyperkalaemia symptoms), sick-day rules (withhold ACE inhibitors, diuretics, metformin, SGLT2 inhibitors, NSAIDs during dehydrating illness), monitoring tests and review dates, and what to do if a dose is missed. Use pharmacy and shared records for communication.

## 5. Context-specific safety-netting

### 5.1 Primary care

- The GP often sees early, undifferentiated illness; time and continuity are tools: "Come back if…" combined with a **set review date** for higher-risk situations.
- Use **"red flag" cards** and standardised leaflets (for example for back pain, headache, abdominal pain, fever in children).
- **Telephone triage**: use structured tools; low threshold for face-to-face assessment; safety-net recorded on the call.
- **Repeated consultations** with the same symptom ("frequent attender"): a flag for missed diagnosis; reconsider at the third visit ("rule of three").

### 5.2 Emergency department

- High-volume, short interaction, no continuity: safety-net must be **explicit and written**, with **return criteria** and **time frames**; **follow-up for test results** (e.g., X-ray over-read, culture results, troponin repeat) by an assigned team; call-back systems for fractures missed on initial films.
- Consider **time-of-day risk** (night, weekend), **crowding**, **handover**.
- For patients leaving against advice: document capacity, risks explained, alternatives offered, and safety-net.

### 5.3 Hospital inpatients and discharge

- Plan discharge early; involve patients and carers; ensure transport, medications, equipment and community support are in place.
- Provide an **escalation plan**, for example, "If breathless or temperature rises, call this number".
- For patients on antibiotic courses or with drains/lines, include removal and review dates.

### 5.4 Remote consultations (phone/video/online)

- Limitations: no examination, limited vital signs, visual cues missing. Compensate with a **lower threshold for face-to-face assessment**, structured questions, home measurements (temperature, SpO₂, BP), photographs for skin, and explicit safety-netting.
- Identify patient, location, and callback number; check if someone can stay with the patient.
- Document that remote assessment occurred, what could not be assessed, and arrangement for in-person review.

### 5.5 Children

- Parents are the primary observers: tell them how to recognise serious illness (breathing difficulty, drowsiness, dehydration, non-blanching rash, persistent fever, inconsolable crying, poor feeding, bilious vomiting) using traffic-light information; emphasise **"trust your instinct"**.
- Provide advice on fever, hydration and when to return (NICE feverish illness leaflet). Review within 24–48 hours if still unwell; earlier in infants.
- Safeguarding concerns: safety-netting includes safe plans for the child.

### 5.6 Older adults and people with frailty or cognitive impairment

- Atypical presentations; low threshold for review; involve carers/care-home staff; ensure they know early warning signs (new confusion, reduced intake, falls, reduced urine output) and the escalation pathway (e.g., RESTORE2, NEWS2).
- Clear advance care planning and treatment escalation plans prevent inappropriate transfers or under-treatment.

### 5.7 Language, disability, health literacy

Use interpreters (not family members for clinical details when avoidable); written materials in appropriate languages and formats; easy-read, large print; hearing and sight adaptations; confirm understanding.

### 5.8 Mental health

Safety plan (Stanley–Brown): warning signs, coping strategies, contacts, professional and crisis numbers, means restriction, reasons for living. Follow-up within 24–72 hours after crisis or discharge from emergency care, since the period after discharge has high suicide risk. Provide numbers for crisis lines and local services; involve family with consent.

### 5.9 Pregnancy and postpartum

Early pregnancy bleeding or pain: advise to return for heavy bleeding (soaking a pad per hour), severe pain, dizziness, shoulder-tip pain; follow-up scan and hCG as per pathway; Rh status; mental health. Antenatal: reduced fetal movements → same-day assessment; pre-eclampsia symptoms; postnatal warning signs (bleeding, fever, leg swelling, dyspnoea, mood).

## 6. Safety-netting advice for common situations (model language)

**Head injury (adult, discharged)**: "Someone should stay with you for 24 hours. Come back to the emergency department or call 999 if: you are increasingly drowsy or hard to wake; severe or worsening headache; repeated vomiting; confusion, unusual behaviour or speech; weakness, numbness, clumsiness; fits; clear fluid from the nose or ear; blood from the ear; double vision."

**Abdominal pain, cause unknown**: "The tests have not shown anything dangerous at the moment. Appendicitis can take 24–48 hours to declare itself. Return immediately if the pain worsens or localises, if you have persistent vomiting, fever, black or bloody stools, you cannot drink, or you feel faint. Please call us in 24 hours to let us know how you are."

**Chest pain, ACS ruled out by serial troponin**: "Your heart tests are reassuring today. If chest pressure returns, especially with exertion, at rest for more than 15 minutes, radiating to arm or jaw, or with breathlessness or sweating, call 999 (do not drive). Attend the chest pain clinic on [date]."

**Child with fever**: "Most fevers are due to viruses. Give fluids and paracetamol/ibuprofen if uncomfortable. Seek help immediately if: rash that does not fade with the glass test, difficulty breathing, very drowsy or floppy, refusal to drink or no wet nappy for 8 hours, fits, stiff neck, bluish skin, or if you feel something is seriously wrong. Contact us if fever lasts more than 5 days or gets worse. We will review tomorrow."

**Back pain**: "Keep active. Come back urgently if you notice numbness around your bottom or genitals, difficulty passing or controlling urine or stool, new weakness in the legs, fever, or severe unrelenting night pain."

**Early pregnancy bleeding**: "Come back if bleeding becomes heavy, you pass clots, have severe pain, shoulder pain, feel dizzy or faint."

**Suspected cancer awaiting investigation**: "You'll be offered an appointment within 2 weeks. If you have not heard from the hospital within 7 days, call us/this number. Meanwhile, tell us if X worsens."

**Antidepressant start**: "It can take 2–4 weeks to work. Some people feel more anxious or restless in the first weeks. If you have thoughts of harming yourself or feel much worse, call us immediately or [crisis number]. We will see you in 1–2 weeks."

## 7. Failure modes and defences

| Failure | Typical mechanism | Defence |
|---|---|---|
| Result not seen or acted upon | Clinician away, inbox overflow, multiple results, discharge | Named owner, delegation, tracking system, discharge pending-test lists |
| Patient didn't know what to watch for | Verbal-only advice, jargon | Written and teach-back safety net, translated materials |
| Over-reassurance | "It's nothing" | Use probabilities ("very unlikely but not impossible"), explicit return criteria |
| No follow-up for uncertain diagnosis | Time pressure, assumption that patient will return | Fixed review date; recall list |
| Ambiguous ownership | Multiple teams, outpatient vs GP | Specify "Responsible: Dr X" in notes and letters |
| Missed incidental finding | Not in summary | Structured incidental finding section; radiology follow-up tracking |
| Referral lost | Fax/paper process, wrong address | Electronic referral with acknowledgment; chase protocol |
| Wrong contact details | Changed phone/address, unreachable | Verify details at every visit; secondary contact |
| Premature closure and anchoring | Diagnostic momentum | Re-examine at follow-up; "rule of three"; senior review |
| Poor communication of uncertainty | Fear of alarming patient | Honest framing: "most likely X; if Y we will reconsider" |
| Fatigue, workload, shift handover | Cognitive overload | Handover tools (I-PASS), reminders, protected time |

**Organisational measures**: significant event analysis, audit of missed or delayed diagnoses, learning from complaints, failsafe processes, health information technology (automatic result routing, outstanding-test dashboards), patient access to records, and a **just culture** that encourages reporting.

## 8. Ethics and communication

- **Honesty and shared uncertainty**: telling patients that diagnosis may change builds trust and enables engagement.
- **Autonomy**: respect decisions to decline further tests or follow-up after informed discussion; document; leave the door open.
- **Avoiding false reassurance and unnecessary alarm**: balance. A message such as "I'm not worried, but I want you to know when I would be" is often effective.
- **Duty of candour**: if a missed result or error is identified, inform the patient promptly, apologise, take remedial action and learn.
- **Respect for privacy**: ensure that safety-net communication (SMS, voicemail, letters) protects confidentiality.

## Worked cases

### Case 1: The "viral illness" that became meningococcal disease

**Presentation.** A 19-year-old student, 12 hours of fever, headache and aches; examined by an out-of-hours doctor: T 38.6 °C, HR 100, no rash, no neck stiffness, drinking well. Diagnosis: viral illness. Advice: paracetamol and fluids; "come back if worse".

**Reasoning.** Early meningococcal disease may be indistinguishable from a viral illness. Safety-netting should include specific, actionable signs and time frames, and a shared understanding of the rare serious possibility.

**Better safety net.** "This looks like a viral illness, which is by far the most likely. Very rarely, serious infections such as meningitis can start like this. If you develop a rash that doesn't fade under a glass, severe headache, a stiff neck, light hurting your eyes, confusion or drowsiness, cold hands and feet, or leg pain, call 999 immediately. Someone should stay with you tonight. Roommates should know. Phone us tomorrow morning/we'll call you."

**Outcome.** At 4 a.m. the roommate noticed purpuric rash and called 999; IV antibiotics were given within the hour. Survived.

**Lessons.** Specific symptom lists, a person nearby and a defined contact allow early recognition.

### Case 2: A normal CT and a lost result

**Presentation.** Ms B., 64, ED visit for abdominal pain; CT abdomen: no acute pathology but a 1.8 cm indeterminate pancreatic cystic lesion mentioned in the report; discharged with analgesia. The report's recommendation (MRI/MRCP in 6 months) was not mentioned in the discharge summary. 18 months later she presents with jaundice and weight loss: pancreatic cancer.

**Reasoning.** An incidental finding with a follow-up recommendation was lost because ownership was not assigned. Systems error: no tracking, no communication to GP or patient.

**Better practice.** The ED clinician (or radiology system) flags the recommendation; discharge summary lists it with "Responsible: GP to arrange MRCP at 6 months"; patient informed; entry in an incidental findings register with recall; GP acknowledges.

**Lessons.** Incidental findings require explicit owners and tracking; results with follow-up recommendations must reach the clinician who can act.

### Case 3: Safety-netting with a language barrier

**Presentation.** Mr C., 58, Arabic-speaking, with chest tightness settled in ED; serial troponins normal; discharged with an English leaflet and instructions to attend a chest pain clinic. He does not understand the follow-up details and does not attend. Two weeks later he has an MI.

**Reasoning.** Language and health literacy were not addressed. No teach-back, no interpreter, no tracking of the clinic referral.

**Better practice.** Use a professional interpreter; provide translated written instructions; teach-back; book the appointment before discharge and give a date/time; send a text reminder; call after 48 hours; flag DNA for follow-up; involve a family member with consent.

**Lessons.** Communication quality determines the effectiveness of a safety net. Track high-risk follow-up.

### Case 4: Time as a diagnostic test in the office

**Presentation.** Ms D., 31, 3 days of right iliac fossa discomfort, mild, no fever, tenderness minimal, WBC and CRP normal, hCG negative. Alvarado 3. Clinician unsure.

**Reasoning.** Probability of appendicitis is low but not zero; risk of evolution over 24–48 hours. Imaging is not clearly indicated now. A planned reassessment is the best test.

**Action.** Explain: "It's probably not appendicitis, but it can be early. Return if pain gets worse, moves or becomes sharper, you vomit, develop a fever or cannot eat. I would like to see you tomorrow at 10:00." Appointment set; documented. Next day: pain increased, tenderness localised, WBC 14 → CT confirms early appendicitis; laparoscopic surgery.

**Lessons.** A scheduled reassessment, combined with symptom triggers, safely manages uncertainty.

### Case 5: Discharge from the medical ward

**Presentation.** Mr E., 77, admitted with pneumonia and AKI (creatinine 180), treated with antibiotics and fluids; discharged with creatinine 120 (baseline 90); ramipril and metformin were stopped temporarily. The discharge summary did not mention the stopped drugs or the need for repeat U&E. Two weeks later, he is readmitted with hypertension crisis and hyperglycaemia.

**Reasoning.** Incomplete discharge communication: drug changes without restart plan; no scheduled blood test.

**Better practice.** Discharge summary includes: "Ramipril and metformin withheld; GP to repeat U&E in 1 week and restart ramipril if creatinine < 110 and K < 5.0; restart metformin if eGFR > 45"; patient given written plan and a date for blood test booked before discharge; pharmacy medication reconciliation; phone call at 48 hours.

**Lessons.** Every held medication needs a restart or stop decision, an owner and a date.

### Case 6: The frequent attender

**Presentation.** Mrs F., 52, third consultation in 6 weeks with fatigue, bloating, early satiety; each time, symptom-treated (PPI, probiotic) with normal basic tests.

**Reasoning.** The "rule of three" prompts a pause: persistent symptoms in a woman over 50 with bloating and early satiety fit ovarian cancer red flags; the previous encounters lacked a diagnostic plan and a safety net.

**Action.** CA-125 and pelvic/abdominal ultrasound (direct access); examine abdomen and pelvis; review family history; document the plan; set a result review date within 1 week; if CA-125 ≥ 35 or imaging suspicious, urgent gynaecological oncology referral. CA-125 is 420 → fast-track pathway.

**Lessons.** Repeated attendance with persisting symptoms warrants a change of approach and a defined follow-up plan.

### Case 7: A mental health safety plan

**Presentation.** Mr G., 24, attends the ED after a suicidal crisis triggered by job loss; no current plan or intent after assessment; lives with flatmates; discharged with community follow-up.

**Reasoning.** The immediate post-discharge period carries high risk. Safety-netting requires a collaborative safety plan and rapid follow-up.

**Action.** Develop with him a Stanley–Brown safety plan: warning signs (isolation, insomnia), coping strategies, people to contact, professional contacts (crisis line, GP), restricting access to means (surrender stockpiled medication), reasons to live; arrange a phone call within 24 hours and an appointment within 72 hours; inform flatmates (with consent); provide numbers (local crisis service); GP letter.

**Lessons.** Effective safety-netting in mental health is a joint plan with specific steps and prompt follow-up.

## Common pitfalls

1. **Vague advice** ("come back if worse") without specific signs or timeframes.
2. **No follow-up arranged** after an uncertain diagnosis.
3. **No ownership** of pending tests, referrals or incidental findings.
4. **Assuming the patient will return**; no plan for non-attenders.
5. **Over-reliance on verbal advice** alone; no written/translated material; no teach-back.
6. **False reassurance** ("it's nothing") or avoidable alarm.
7. **Incomplete discharge communication** (stopped drugs, pending results, monitoring).
8. **Failing to act on abnormal results** or to inform the patient.
9. **Not adjusting for context** (language, literacy, disability, social isolation, remote consultation).
10. **Ignoring patterns** such as repeated attendance with the same symptoms.
11. **Poor documentation** of advice and responsibility.
12. **Not learning from near misses** and failures.

---

## Key points

- Safety-netting converts uncertainty into a managed process: what to expect, what would change the plan, what to do and who will act.
- Time can serve as a diagnostic test, but only with explicit review points and patient understanding.
- Every pending test, referral and incidental finding needs a named owner and a tracking system.
- Tailor advice to the patient's language, literacy, social context and consultation mode; check understanding by teach-back; give written information.
- Discharge summaries must list diagnoses, medication changes (with restart/stop plans), pending results, follow-up and who is responsible.
- Plan for non-attendance in high-risk patients; treat a missed appointment as a clinical event.
- Reflect on failure modes and invest in system defences: results tracking, referral management, structured handover and audit.
